Diagnosis

Cognitive tests

Brief tests of memory, attention and language, such as the MoCA or the clock test, and full neuropsychological assessments. What they measure and why a result is not a diagnosis.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. What they are
  2. Screening tests
  3. Full neuropsychological assessment
  4. Functional scales
  5. Tips for taking the tests
  6. Sources

What they are

Cognitive tests are standardised tests that measure mental functions: memory, attention, language, orientation, executive functions (planning, reasoning, switching tasks) and visuospatial ability. They compare the results with those of people of the same age and educational level. They show whether there is impairment, which functions it affects, how much and how it changes over time.

Their results do not diagnose a disease on their own: they show a pattern that is interpreted together with the interview, the examination, information from a relative and imaging or biomarker tests.

Screening tests

These are brief (5-15 minutes) and are done by a GP or a neurologist.

Test Duration Features
MMSE (Mini-Mental) 10 min The best known; scored out of 30; not very sensitive in mild stages
MoCA 10-15 min More sensitive for mild cognitive impairment; includes executive functions
Clock drawing test 2-3 min Drawing a clock showing a time; assesses planning and visuospatial skills
Fototest 3 min Naming and recalling photos; useful in people with little schooling (developed in Spain)
Memory Alteration Test (T@M) 5 min Episodic memory; validated in Spanish
Verbal fluency 1 min Naming animals or words beginning with a letter in one minute

An abnormal result is not a diagnosis of dementia: it may be due to anxiety, depression, sensory loss, medicines or a low level of education. And a normal one does not exclude a mild problem in people with high cognitive reserve.

Full neuropsychological assessment

This is done by a neuropsychologist over one or more sessions of 1-3 hours. It explores each function in depth with test batteries, and allows:

  • Distinguishing normal ageing from mild cognitive impairment and dementia.
  • Pointing to the type of dementia by its pattern: memory failures that do not improve with cues in Alzheimer's; attention and visuospatial problems in Lewy body dementia; behaviour and executive function problems in frontotemporal dementia; slowness in vascular dementia.
  • Assessing the after-effects of a stroke, a head injury, multiple sclerosis or epilepsy.
  • Preparing for epilepsy or tumour surgery, to protect language and memory.
  • Designing cognitive rehabilitation and assessing the ability to drive, work or make decisions.

Functional scales

It is just as important to know how the person manages in daily life. Scales answered by a relative are used, such as the Barthel index (personal care), the Lawton scale (handling money, medication, transport) or Reisberg's GDS, which classifies dementia into seven stages. The difference between mild cognitive impairment and dementia lies precisely in the fact that in dementia daily life is affected.

Tips for taking the tests

  • Bring your glasses and hearing aids.
  • Sleep well the night before and take your usual medication.
  • Come with a relative who knows the patient well.
  • There is no need to "revise": it is not an exam, and you do not pass or fail.
