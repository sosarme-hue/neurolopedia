Doppler ultrasound and angiography

Ways to see the arteries that carry blood to the brain: Doppler, with no needles, and angiography, the most precise test, which also allows treatment.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Seeing the brain's arteries

The brain receives its blood through four arteries in the neck: the two carotids and the two vertebrals (brain circulation). Narrowing or blockage of one of them is a common cause of stroke and transient ischaemic attack. Several tests are available to study them, from the simplest to the most invasive.

Carotid Doppler ultrasound

It uses ultrasound, like a pregnancy scan. A probe placed on the neck shows the wall of the carotid and vertebral arteries, atheroma plaques and, with the Doppler effect, the speed of the blood. The narrower the artery, the faster the blood flows through it.

  • It is harmless, with no radiation or contrast, quick and inexpensive.
  • It is requested after a stroke or TIA, when a bruit is heard in the neck, or to monitor a stenosis.
  • It decides whether a carotid stenosis is significant enough (generally more than 70% with symptoms) to operate (endarterectomy) or place a stent. See stroke prevention.
  • It can detect an artery dissection.

Transcranial Doppler

The same technique applied through thin areas of the skull (the temple, the back of the neck) measures flow in the arteries inside the brain. It is used to:

  • Detect stenoses or blockages inside the skull.
  • Monitor vasospasm after a subarachnoid haemorrhage.
  • Detect bubbles reaching the brain after injecting agitated saline: this indicates a patent foramen ovale, a connection between the upper chambers of the heart that can cause stroke in young people.
  • Screen for stroke risk in children with sickle cell anaemia.
  • Monitor recanalisation during thrombolysis.

CT angiography and MR angiography

They show the whole arterial tree, from the aortic arch to the brain, in a few minutes. CT angiography is the urgent test in the stroke code to see whether a large artery is blocked. MR angiography can be done without contrast. Both detect aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations and dissections. See CT head scan and MRI.

Cerebral angiography

This is the reference test. A catheter is passed through the artery in the groin or wrist up to the neck arteries and contrast is injected while a series of X-rays is taken. It offers the highest resolution and shows the blood in motion.

  • It is used when other tests do not clarify the diagnosis: small aneurysms, malformations, fistulas, vasculitis.
  • Above all, it is the route for treatment: thrombectomy, embolisation of aneurysms and malformations, or stents.
  • It carries a small risk (around 0.5-1%) of stroke, bleeding at the puncture site or reaction to the contrast.

Other heart tests in stroke work-up

As many strokes come from the heart, the work-up is completed with an electrocardiogram, a Holter monitor over several days or an implantable monitor to look for atrial fibrillation, and an echocardiogram to look for clots or a patent foramen ovale.
