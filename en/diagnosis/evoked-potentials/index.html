Evoked potentials

They measure how long the brain takes to respond to a visual, auditory or touch stimulus. They detect "silent" lesions of the nerve pathways, for example in multiple sclerosis.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  2. Types
  3. What they are used for
  4. How they are done
  5. Limitations

What they are

Evoked potentials record the electrical response of the nervous system to a specific stimulus: an image, a sound or a small pulse to a nerve. That response is so small that it is hidden in the brain's normal activity, so the stimulus is repeated hundreds of times and the computer averages the signals until a clear wave appears. What is mainly measured is the time (latency) the signal takes to arrive: if the pathway is damaged, especially by loss of myelin, the signal arrives late.

Their value lies in showing that a nerve pathway is not working properly even when there are no symptoms or the MRI is normal.

Types

Visual evoked potentials (VEP)

The patient looks at a chequerboard whose squares alternate on a screen, with one eye covered at a time, and the response is recorded at the back of the head (over the visual cortex). A delay indicates a lesion of the optic nerve, typical of optic neuritis, even years later and even if sight has recovered.

Brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEP)

Clicks are played through headphones. The waves reflect the passage of the signal through the auditory nerve and the brainstem. They are used for:

  • Newborn hearing screening (automated version).
  • Detecting tumours of the hearing nerve (acoustic neuroma).
  • Assessing the brainstem in coma.

Somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP)

A nerve at the wrist or ankle is stimulated with small pulses and the response is recorded over the spine and scalp. They assess the sensory pathway through the spinal cord to the brain.

Motor evoked potentials

With transcranial magnetic stimulation over the motor cortex, the response is recorded in the muscles. They assess the motor pathway.

What they are used for

  • Multiple sclerosis: they detect "silent" lesions in other pathways and help when MRI is inconclusive, although they are used less today because MRI is more sensitive. Also in neuromyelitis optica.
  • Intraoperative monitoring: during spine, spinal cord or brain tumour surgery, they are recorded continuously. If a signal changes, the surgeon knows at once that a pathway is at risk and can correct it. This is their most widespread use today.
  • Prognosis of coma after cardiac arrest: bilateral absence of the cortical somatosensory response indicates a very poor outlook.
  • Babies and people who cannot cooperate: they assess sight or hearing without needing a response.
  • Myelopathies, hereditary ataxias, vitamin B12 deficiency.

How they are done

Electrodes are placed on the scalp with paste. They are painless apart from slight discomfort from the pulses in somatosensory studies. Each type takes 30 to 60 minutes. For visual studies you should wear your usual glasses or contact lenses and be awake and attentive.

Limitations

They show that a pathway is not working properly, but not why: a delay can be due to demyelination, compression or an eye problem. That is why they are always interpreted together with the examination and other tests, such as EEG or MRI.
