Genetic testing in neurology

Many neurological diseases have a genetic cause. Which tests exist, when they are requested, what a result means and why genetic counselling is essential.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. Why they matter
  2. Types of test
  3. Diseases where they are used
  4. Genetic counselling: essential
  5. Direct-to-consumer tests
  6. Sources

Why they matter

Hundreds of neurological diseases are known to be caused by changes in a single gene, and in many others genes increase the risk. A genetic test can confirm a diagnosis, save families with rare diseases years of tests, guide the outlook, inform other relatives and, increasingly, give access to specific treatments such as gene and RNA therapies.

Types of test

Test What it examines Example
Targeted test One gene or a specific mutation CAG repeats in Huntington's
Gene panel Dozens or hundreds of genes in a group Panel for ataxias, neuropathies or epilepsies
Exome The protein-coding parts of all genes A child with epilepsy and developmental delay without a cause
Whole genome All of the DNA Undiagnosed cases after exome sequencing
Array-CGH Losses or gains of pieces of chromosomes Intellectual disability, autism
Repeat expansion testing Triplet repeats Friedreich's ataxia, myotonic dystrophy, C9orf72 in ALS and frontotemporal dementia

They are almost always done on a blood or saliva sample.

Diseases where they are used

  • Spinal muscular atrophy: the SMN1 gene. Many Spanish regions include it in newborn screening (heel-prick test), because treating it before symptoms completely changes its course.
  • Muscular dystrophies: Duchenne (dystrophin gene), facioscapulohumeral, myotonic.
  • ALS: C9orf72, SOD1 (with a specific treatment, tofersen), FUS, TARDBP.
  • Frontotemporal dementia and early-onset Alzheimer's (APP, PSEN1, PSEN2). The APOE4 gene increases the risk of Alzheimer's but does not determine it, and routine testing for it is not recommended.
  • Parkinson's: LRRK2, GBA1, PRKN, in young or familial cases.
  • Hereditary neuropathies (Charcot-Marie-Tooth), transthyretin amyloidosis (with treatments that halt nerve damage).
  • Wilson's disease, neurofibromatosis and tuberous sclerosis, channelopathies, leukodystrophies.

Genetic counselling: essential

A genetic test is not just another blood test. It has consequences for the person and their family, so it must be accompanied by genetic counselling before and after:

  • Predictive testing: in people without symptoms who have an affected relative, for example with Huntington's. A protocol is followed with several appointments, psychological support and time to decide. Nobody is obliged to know, and the decision not to be tested is just as valid as the opposite.
  • Uncertain results: variants of uncertain significance often appear, from which nothing can be concluded and which may be reclassified over the years.
  • Secondary findings: exome or genome sequencing can reveal other risks (hereditary cancer, heart disease). Whether you want to know about them is agreed beforehand.
  • Family planning: prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic diagnosis with IVF.

In Spain, the Biomedical Research Act (2007) requires written consent and genetic counselling, and protects genetic data from discriminatory use.

Direct-to-consumer tests

Genetic tests sold online look at only a few variants, do not replace a clinical test and can give false reassurance or unnecessary alarm. If you are worried about a family condition, see a neurologist or a clinical genetics unit.
