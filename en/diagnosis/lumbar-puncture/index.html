Lumbar puncture

A small amount of cerebrospinal fluid is taken with a fine needle in the lower back. It is key in meningitis, multiple sclerosis or Alzheimer's. What it is like and what discomfort it can cause.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. What it is
  2. What it is used for
  3. How it is done
  4. Before the puncture
  5. Side effects
  6. Sources

What it is

A lumbar puncture (spinal tap) involves inserting a fine needle between two vertebrae in the lower back to obtain a sample of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the clear fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It is done below the end of the spinal cord (which ends at about the first or second lumbar vertebra), so the needle does not touch the cord. It was described by Heinrich Quincke in 1891.

What it is used for

Diagnosis

  • Meningitis and encephalitis: the key test. It identifies the infection (bacteria, viruses, fungi) and guides the antibiotic. If bacterial meningitis is suspected, the antibiotic is given first, without waiting for the puncture.
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage: when CT is normal but suspicion remains, it looks for blood or its pigment (xanthochromia).
  • Multiple sclerosis: oligoclonal bands support the diagnosis.
  • Alzheimer's disease: biomarkers (low beta-amyloid, high tau and phosphorylated tau) confirm the disease during life, even in early stages. They are now required for the new anti-amyloid treatments.
  • Guillain-Barré: high protein without cells.
  • Creutzfeldt-Jakob: the RT-QuIC test, which is highly specific.
  • Autoimmune encephalitis, tumour cells (meningeal carcinomatosis).
  • Measuring the fluid pressure in idiopathic intracranial hypertension.

Treatment

  • Removing fluid: relieves intracranial hypertension and serves as a test in normal pressure hydrocephalus (if walking improves after fluid is removed, a shunt will probably help).
  • Giving medicines: spinal anaesthesia, chemotherapy or nusinersen in spinal muscular atrophy.

How it is done

  1. The patient sits leaning forward or lies on their side curled up, to open the space between the vertebrae.
  2. The skin is cleaned and a local anaesthetic is given.
  3. The needle is inserted and the fluid is collected drop by drop into tubes. It takes about 15-30 minutes.

You feel a prick and pressure; sometimes a brief shock down one leg, if the needle brushes a nerve root.

Before the puncture

  • Say if you take anticoagulants or antiplatelets: they often need to be stopped for a few days.
  • In some patients (reduced consciousness, focal signs, papilloedema, weakened immunity) a CT scan is done first to rule out a mass that would make the puncture dangerous.

Side effects

  • Post-lumbar puncture headache: the most common (up to 1 in 10 with traditional needles, fewer with fine atraumatic needles). It is typical because it appears on standing and improves on lying down. It usually lasts a few days and is treated with rest, fluids, painkillers and caffeine; if it persists, with an epidural blood patch.
  • Mild lower back pain.
  • Very rare: infection, haematoma, brain herniation if there was undetected intracranial hypertension.

Prolonged bed rest after the puncture does not prevent the headache.
