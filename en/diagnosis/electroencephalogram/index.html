Diagnosis

Electroencephalogram (EEG)

Records the brain's electrical activity with electrodes on the scalp. It is the basic test in epilepsy and is also used in coma, sleep and encephalitis.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  2. Brain rhythms
  3. What it is used for

What it is

The electroencephalogram (EEG) records the brain's electrical activity through electrodes placed on the scalp. The neurons of the cortex generate small currents that, added together, produce waves of a few microvolts. It was invented by the German psychiatrist Hans Berger, who published the first human EEG in 1929 and described the alpha rhythm, which appears when the eyes are closed at rest.

Brain rhythms

Rhythm Frequency When it appears
Delta < 4 Hz Deep sleep; when awake, it indicates a lesion or encephalopathy
Theta 4-7 Hz Drowsiness, children
Alpha 8-13 Hz Awake, relaxed, eyes closed
Beta > 13 Hz Attention, concentration; some medicines (benzodiazepines)

What it is used for

  • Epilepsy: it is the main test. It looks for epileptiform discharges (spikes, sharp waves) between seizures, helps distinguish types of epilepsy and syndromes (childhood epilepsies), and guides treatment.
  • Non-convulsive status epilepticus: in a confused or comatose person, only the EEG shows they are in continuous seizure.
  • Encephalopathies: metabolic, toxic, infectious (encephalitis) or Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, which has a characteristic pattern.
  • Prognosis after cardiac arrest or coma.
  • Supporting the diagnosis of brain death.
  • Sleep studies (polysomnography), together with other signals.

An important point: a normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy. Between seizures, the first EEG is normal in half of people with epilepsy. That is why it is repeated, done after sleep deprivation or during sleep. Conversely, some mild abnormalities appear in healthy people.

Types

  • Routine EEG: 20-30 minutes, with flashing light (photic stimulation) and rapid breathing (hyperventilation), which can provoke abnormalities.
  • Sleep-deprived EEG: you sleep little the night before to increase the yield.
  • Ambulatory EEG: with a portable device for 24-72 hours at home.
  • Video-EEG: a stay of several days with continuous video and EEG recording, to capture seizures. It is used to confirm whether episodes are epileptic, to distinguish them from psychogenic seizures or faints, and to locate the focus before epilepsy surgery.
  • Intracranial EEG: with electrodes inside the skull, in surgical candidates.

How it is done

It does not hurt or give electric shocks: it only records. About 20 electrodes are attached with paste or a cap. It is best to have clean, dry hair, without hairspray or oils, take your usual medication (unless told otherwise) and not to fast, because low blood sugar alters the recording.

Beyond diagnosis

EEG is the basis of non-invasive brain-machine interfaces and of monitoring the depth of anaesthesia. And in research, together with magnetoencephalography, it allows the study of how the brain processes information on a millisecond scale.
