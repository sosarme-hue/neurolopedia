Hippocampus and limbic system

The structures that form memories and give experience its emotional colour: the hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate gyrus and their connections. What patient H. M. taught us.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The brain of emotions and memories

The limbic system is a set of structures on the inner edge of the hemispheres (limbus means "edge"), around the thalamus. It is involved in emotions, memory, motivation and some basic behaviours. James Papez described it in 1937 as the circuit of emotions (the Papez circuit), and Paul MacLean popularised the term. Its main structures are the hippocampus, the amygdala, the cingulate gyrus, the fornix, the mammillary bodies and parts of the thalamus and hypothalamus.

The hippocampus

It is a curved structure on the inner surface of the temporal lobe, shaped like a seahorse (hence its name). There is one in each hemisphere. It is essential for:

  • Forming new memories of facts and experiences (declarative or explicit memory). It is not the final "store": over weeks or years, and especially during sleep, it transfers memories to the cortex.
  • Finding our way in space: it contains place cells, which fire when we are in a particular location. See spatial navigation.
  • It is one of the few areas where new neurons form in adults.

The case that proved this was H. M., a young man whose two hippocampi were removed in 1953 to treat his epilepsy. From then on he could not form new memories, although he kept old ones and could learn motor skills. He was studied for decades by Brenda Milner. See memory.

A vulnerable hippocampus

  • It is one of the first areas damaged in Alzheimer's, which is why the first symptom is usually forgetting recent events. Its shrinkage is measured on MRI.
  • It is very sensitive to lack of oxygen (cardiac arrest), low blood sugar and chronic stress through cortisol.
  • Hippocampal sclerosis is the most common cause of drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy, which can be cured with surgery.
  • Herpes and autoimmune encephalitis preferentially affect it.
  • In transient global amnesia, small temporary lesions are seen in the hippocampus.

The amygdala

It is an almond-shaped nucleus in front of the hippocampus. It is the centre for detecting threats and for fear: it reacts within milliseconds to danger, even before we are aware of it, and sets off the stress response. It also gives memories their emotional charge, which is why we remember better what moved us. People with damage to both amygdalae (such as patient S. M., who had Urbach-Wiethe disease) do not feel fear. Its overactivity is linked to anxiety and post-traumatic stress. See emotions.

Other structures

  • Cingulate gyrus: attention, motivation, error detection, the emotional component of pain.
  • Mammillary bodies and fornix: memory. They are damaged by lack of vitamin B1 in alcoholism (Korsakoff syndrome).
  • Entorhinal cortex: the gateway to the hippocampus; it contains the grid cells discovered by May-Britt and Edvard Moser.
  • Olfactory bulb: smell is the only sense connected directly to the limbic system, which is why smells bring back memories so powerfully.
