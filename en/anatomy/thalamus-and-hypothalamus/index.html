Anatomy

Thalamus and hypothalamus

The thalamus is the relay station for almost all information reaching the cortex; the hypothalamus is the control centre for hunger, thirst, temperature, sleep and hormones.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. At the centre of the brain
  2. The thalamus
  3. The hypothalamus
  4. Sources

At the centre of the brain

The diencephalon lies at the centre of the brain, between the hemispheres and the brainstem, around the third ventricle. Its two main structures are the thalamus and the hypothalamus: the first a great relay station, the second the control centre for the body's internal environment.

The thalamus

It consists of two oval masses, each about the size of a walnut, on either side of the third ventricle. It is called the "gateway to the cortex":

  • Sensory relay: almost all information from the senses (sight, hearing, touch, taste, pain) passes through the thalamus before reaching the cortex. Only smell goes direct.
  • Filter: it selects which information reaches consciousness and which is discarded. That is why we can focus on a conversation in a noisy place.
  • Movement: it is part of the circuits of the basal ganglia and cerebellum.
  • Arousal and consciousness: it takes part in the sleep-wake cycle.
  • Memory and emotions, through its connections with the limbic system.

If it is damaged

A small thalamic stroke causes loss of sensation on one half of the body. Sometimes, weeks later, intense burning thalamic pain appears (Dejerine-Roussy syndrome), a type of central neuropathic pain. Damage to both thalami can cause drowsiness, memory problems or coma. The thalamus is also a target for deep brain stimulation in tremor and epilepsy.

The hypothalamus

The hypothalamus, below the thalamus, weighs only 4 grams, less than 1% of the brain, yet it controls functions essential to life. It is the link between the nervous system and the hormonal system:

Function How it works
Temperature It acts as a thermostat, triggering sweating or shivering; fever "raises" its set point
Hunger and fullness It responds to hormones such as leptin and ghrelin
Thirst and water It releases antidiuretic hormone, which makes the kidney retain water
Circadian rhythms The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the body clock, set by light
Sleep and wakefulness Orexin neurons, whose loss causes narcolepsy
Hormones It controls the pituitary gland: growth, thyroid, adrenal glands, reproduction, breastfeeding
Stress It starts the cortisol response; see stress and the brain
Autonomic nervous system It coordinates the response of the heart, blood vessels and internal organs
Basic behaviours Reproduction, aggression, fear, together with the limbic system

Oxytocin, released during childbirth, breastfeeding and close relationships, is made in the hypothalamus.

If it is damaged

Tumours (craniopharyngioma, large pituitary adenomas), injuries, surgery or inflammatory diseases can disrupt the hypothalamus and cause obesity or extreme weight loss, temperature disturbances, excessive thirst and urination (diabetes insipidus), sleep disorders or hormonal problems.
