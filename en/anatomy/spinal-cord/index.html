Anatomy

Spinal cord

The cable that connects the brain with the body, protected inside the spine. It carries commands and sensations, and on its own organises reflexes and patterns of movement.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The motorway between brain and body

The spinal cord is a cord of nerve tissue about 45 centimetres long and slightly thicker than a pencil, running down from the brainstem inside the spine. In adults it ends at the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra, at the conus medullaris. Below that there are only loose nerve roots, the cauda equina ("horse's tail"), which is why a lumbar puncture is done lower down without risk to the cord.

It is protected by the vertebrae, the meninges and the cerebrospinal fluid.

Structure

In cross-section the spinal cord shows:

  • In the centre, grey matter shaped like a butterfly or an "H":
  • Anterior horns: contain the motor neurons, which send commands to the muscles.
  • Posterior horns: receive sensory information.
  • Lateral horns (in the thoracic region): neurons of the autonomic nervous system.
  • Around it, white matter, with the bundles of fibres running up and down.

The segments

31 pairs of spinal nerves leave the cord, each with an anterior (motor) and a posterior (sensory) root: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal. Each root collects sensation from a strip of skin, the dermatome, and moves a group of muscles, the myotome. That is why a doctor can tell which root or which level of the cord is damaged from the area that has lost sensation.

The major pathways

Pathway What it carries Where it crosses
Corticospinal (pyramidal) Voluntary movement commands In the medulla
Dorsal columns Fine touch, vibration, joint position In the medulla
Spinothalamic Pain and temperature In the cord itself, at the level of entry

Because the pathways cross at different levels, damage to one half of the cord causes weakness and loss of position sense on one side, and loss of pain sensation on the other (Brown-Séquard syndrome).

Reflexes

The spinal cord is not just a cable: it can respond on its own. In the knee-jerk reflex, the tap stretches the muscle, the signal reaches the cord and goes straight back to the motor neuron, which contracts the muscle, without passing through the brain. The withdrawal reflex from a pinprick is another example. The cord also contains circuits that generate the rhythmic movements of walking. Reflexes are tested in the neurological examination.

When the cord is damaged

Spinal cord damage causes, below the level affected: weakness or paralysis, loss of sensation and problems with bladder, bowel and sexual function. In the neck it can affect the arms, legs and breathing (tetraplegia); lower down, only the legs (paraplegia).

The main causes:

  • Traumatic spinal cord injury: road traffic accidents, falls, diving, sport.
  • Compression: herniated discs, cervical spondylosis (cervical myelopathy), tumours or spinal metastases. Acute compression is an emergency.
  • Transverse myelitis, multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica.
  • Spinal cord infarcts, vitamin B12 or copper deficiency.
  • ALS and spinal muscular atrophy: loss of motor neurons.
  • Spina bifida: a defect in the closure of the neural tube; folic acid in pregnancy prevents it. Syringomyelia, often with Chiari malformation.

The roots leaving the cord can be compressed by a disc herniation and cause sciatica and radiculopathies. Compression of the cauda equina, with loss of bladder control and numbness around the genitals, is a surgical emergency.
