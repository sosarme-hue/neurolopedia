Anatomy

Cerebellum

The "little brain" behind the brainstem coordinates movement, balance and motor learning. It has more neurons than the whole rest of the brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The "little brain"

The cerebellum lies at the back and bottom of the skull, behind the brainstem. It weighs about 150 grams, barely 10% of the brain, yet contains more than half of all the brain's neurons: about 69 billion, mostly tiny granule cells. Its surface is folded into thin leaves (folia), and when it is cut it shows a tree-like pattern, the arbor vitae or "tree of life".

Parts

  • Vermis: the central part, which controls balance and posture of the trunk.
  • Cerebellar hemispheres: control the coordination of the limbs on the same side of the body.
  • Flocculonodular lobe: connected to the inner ear, it controls balance and eye movements.
  • Three peduncles connect it to the brainstem.

Its cortex contains the Purkinje cells, among the largest neurons in the brain, with a huge dendritic tree that receives up to 200,000 connections. They are the only output of the cerebellar cortex.

What it does

Coordinating movement

The cerebellum does not start movements, but it makes them precise, smooth and coordinated. It compares what the brain orders with what actually happens (using information from muscles and joints) and corrects errors in real time. It is like an autopilot that adjusts the direction, force and timing of each movement.

Balance and posture

Together with the inner ear and vision, it keeps us balanced when standing and walking.

Motor learning

Learning to ride a bicycle, type or play an instrument depends largely on the cerebellum, which automates movements with practice.

Cognitive and emotional functions

For a long time it was thought to serve only movement. We now know it also takes part in attention, language, planning and the regulation of emotions. Cerebellar damage can cause the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, described by Jeremy Schmahmann in 1998.

When it fails: ataxia

Damage to the cerebellum causes ataxia, a lack of coordination:

Sign What is seen
Ataxic gait Unsteady, with legs apart, like someone drunk
Dysmetria When touching the nose, the finger overshoots or falls short
Intention tremor Appears and increases as the target gets closer
Dysdiadochokinesia Clumsiness in rapid alternating movements (turning the hand over)
Dysarthria Slow, "scanning" speech with syllables separated
Nystagmus Rhythmic involuntary eye movements
Hypotonia Floppy muscles

Damage to one cerebellar hemisphere affects the same side of the body, the opposite of the cerebral cortex. These signs are looked for in the neurological examination.

Causes of cerebellar damage

  • Alcohol: the most common cause of chronic damage; it mainly affects the vermis. See alcohol, tobacco and drugs.
  • Cerebellar strokes and haemorrhages: they may cause only vertigo and unsteadiness, and be mistaken for an ear problem. They are dangerous because swelling compresses the brainstem.
  • Multiple sclerosis.
  • Hereditary ataxias (Friedreich's, spinocerebellar) and multiple system atrophy, one of the atypical parkinsonisms.
  • Tumours, especially in children (medulloblastoma, astrocytoma); Chiari malformation.
  • Medicines (some antiseizure drugs at high doses), vitamin E or B1 deficiency, coeliac disease, autoimmune and paraneoplastic causes.
