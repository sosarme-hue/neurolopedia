Anatomy

Basal ganglia

Deep nuclei that select which movements are made and which are held back, and take part in motivation and habits. When they fail, the result is Parkinson's, chorea or dystonia.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  2. What they do
  3. Diseases of the basal ganglia
  4. Treatments that act here

Nuclei deep inside

The basal ganglia are a group of grey matter nuclei located deep in each hemisphere, below the cortex. Despite the name, they are not ganglia (the term for clusters of neurons outside the central nervous system). The main ones are:

Nucleus Detail
Striatum Made up of the caudate nucleus and the putamen; the main entry point
Globus pallidus Internal and external; the main output, together with the substantia nigra
Subthalamic nucleus Small but key; the target of deep brain stimulation
Substantia nigra In the midbrain; its pigmented dopamine neurons give it a dark colour
Nucleus accumbens The ventral part of the striatum; the reward centre

What they do

The basal ganglia form circuits with the cortex and the thalamus: they receive information from the cortex, process it and send it back through the thalamus. They work as a filter that selects which movements, thoughts or behaviours go ahead and which are held back.

  • Movement: they facilitate wanted movements and inhibit unwanted ones. They regulate size and speed. There is a direct pathway that "accelerates" and an indirect pathway that "brakes", and dopamine from the substantia nigra balances both.
  • Habits and learning: they automate sequences (driving, tying shoelaces).
  • Reward and motivation: the dopamine circuit of the nucleus accumbens is involved in pleasure, motivation and addiction.
  • Cognitive and emotional functions: planning, switching tasks, emotional regulation, through circuits with the prefrontal and limbic cortex.

Diseases of the basal ganglia

When they fail, the result is movement disorders, which can involve too little movement or too much:

Disease What goes wrong Symptoms
Parkinson's Loss of dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra Slowness, stiffness, tremor at rest
Atypical parkinsonisms Wider degeneration Parkinsonism that responds poorly to levodopa
Huntington's Loss of neurons in the caudate Chorea (dance-like movements), psychiatric and cognitive changes
Dystonia Faulty control of muscle tone Sustained contractions and abnormal postures
Tourette's Cortex-striatum circuits Motor and vocal tics
Wilson's Copper build-up Tremor, dystonia, psychiatric symptoms
Hemiballismus Damage to the subthalamic nucleus (often a stroke) Sudden, wide flinging movements on one side

Obsessive-compulsive disorder and some addictions are also linked to these circuits.

Treatments that act here

  • Levodopa and Parkinson's medicines: replace the missing dopamine.
  • Deep brain stimulation: electrodes in the subthalamic nucleus or internal globus pallidus that regulate the circuit. Useful in Parkinson's, dystonia and tremor.
  • Focused ultrasound: precisely destroys a tiny area without opening the skull.
  • Gene therapies under research aim to make striatal neurons produce dopamine, or to switch off the harmful protein in Huntington's.

Their role has been key in history: James Parkinson described the disease in 1817, and in 1960 it was discovered that the brains of patients lacked dopamine, which led to levodopa.
