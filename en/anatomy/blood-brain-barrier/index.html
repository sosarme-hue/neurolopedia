Blood-brain barrier

A highly selective filter between the blood and the brain that protects neurons from toxins and microbes, but also stops many medicines getting through.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A selective wall

The blood-brain barrier separates the blood from brain tissue and decides what can pass and what cannot. It protects the brain from toxins, microbes and sudden changes in the composition of the blood, and maintains the stable chemical environment neurons need to work. Paul Ehrlich glimpsed its existence at the end of the 19th century: when he injected a blue dye into the blood of animals, every organ was stained except the brain. His student Edwin Goldmann did the reverse: injected into the cerebrospinal fluid, the dye stained only the brain.

What it is made of

In most of the body, capillaries have small pores between their cells through which substances pass freely. Not in the brain. The barrier is formed by:

Component Role
Capillary endothelial cells Joined by tight junctions that seal the gaps between them
Pericytes Surround the capillaries, regulate flow and maintain the barrier
Astrocytes Wrap the vessels with their "end-feet" and induce the barrier (glia)
Basement membrane Supporting layer

Together they form the neurovascular unit.

What gets through and what does not

  • Pass easily: oxygen, carbon dioxide and small, fat-soluble substances such as alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, anaesthetics and many psychiatric drugs.
  • Pass with specific transporters: glucose, amino acids, some vitamins. Levodopa uses the amino acid transporter, which is why protein-rich meals can reduce its effect. See levodopa.
  • Do not pass: most bacteria, large proteins, antibodies (only 0.1% reach the brain), many toxins and most large medicines.
  • The barrier also has efflux pumps (such as P-glycoprotein) that return to the blood substances that have managed to get in.

A few small areas of the brain have no barrier (the circumventricular organs), because they need to "sample" the blood: for example, the vomiting centre detects toxins, and parts of the hypothalamus detect hormones.

A challenge for treatments

It is estimated that more than 98% of small-molecule medicines and almost all large ones do not cross the barrier. It is one of the great obstacles to treating neurological diseases. Some strategies:

  • Giving the medicine directly into the cerebrospinal fluid, like nusinersen in spinal muscular atrophy. See gene and RNA therapies.
  • Modified viruses (AAV9) able to cross it, used in gene therapy.
  • "Trojan horse" molecules that bind to transport receptors (such as the transferrin receptor) to get in.
  • Focused ultrasound with microbubbles, which opens the barrier temporarily and locally.
  • The anti-amyloid antibodies used in Alzheimer's get in only in very small amounts, which is why they are given at high doses. See anti-amyloid antibodies.

When the barrier breaks down

The barrier is disrupted in many diseases, which adds to the damage:

  • Multiple sclerosis: immune cells cross it and attack myelin. Active lesions "take up" gadolinium on MRI because the barrier is open. Some treatments, such as natalizumab, work by stopping lymphocytes from crossing it.
  • Stroke: breakdown of the barrier causes swelling and increases the risk of bleeding after thrombolysis.
  • Meningitis and encephalitis: microbes cross it; inflammation opens it, which lets antibiotics in.
  • Brain tumours: tumour vessels are abnormal and take up contrast; but the barrier hinders chemotherapy.
  • Alzheimer's and ageing: the barrier becomes leakier, especially in the hippocampus.

In newborns

The barrier already works at birth, but it is less mature. That is why very high bilirubin in newborns can damage the brain (kernicterus), and a baby's jaundice needs to be monitored.
