Peripheral nerves

The network of nerves connecting the spinal cord with the skin, muscles and organs. How they are built, what fibres they contain and why they can regenerate, unlike the brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. The body's wiring

  3. Divisions
  4. The neuromuscular junction
  5. Diseases of the nerves
  6. How they are tested

The body's wiring

The peripheral nervous system is all the nerve tissue outside the brain and spinal cord: the nerves and ganglia. It connects the central nervous system with the skin, muscles and organs. Unlike the central nervous system, which has very little capacity for repair, peripheral nerves can regenerate, although slowly (about 1 millimetre a day, roughly 3 centimetres a month).

How a nerve is built

A nerve is like an electric cable with many wires. Each "wire" is the axon of a neuron, most of them wrapped in myelin made by Schwann cells. The axons are grouped into bundles (fascicles), wrapped in protective layers of connective tissue. The longest and thickest nerve in the body is the sciatic nerve, as thick as a finger where it starts.

By function, the fibres are:

Fibre type What it carries Features
Motor Commands to muscles Thick, myelinated
Large sensory Touch, vibration, position Thick, fast
Small sensory Pain and temperature Thin, little or no myelin, slow
Autonomic Sweating, blood pressure, internal organs Thin

Divisions

  • Somatic: controls voluntary muscles and collects sensation from the skin. It includes the 31 pairs of spinal nerves leaving the spinal cord, which group into plexuses (cervical, brachial, lumbar, sacral) before forming the nerves of the arms and legs, and the cranial nerves.
  • Autonomic: controls involuntary functions.

The neuromuscular junction

Where the motor nerve meets the muscle there is a special synapse, the motor end plate, which uses acetylcholine as its neurotransmitter. It is the target of myasthenia gravis (antibodies against the receptor), botulinum toxin and the muscle relaxants used in anaesthesia.

Diseases of the nerves

Pattern Examples
Polyneuropathy: many nerves, starting in the feet ("glove and stocking") Peripheral neuropathy due to diabetes (the most common cause), alcohol, B12 deficiency, chemotherapy, hereditary (Charcot-Marie-Tooth)
Mononeuropathy: a single nerve Carpal tunnel (median nerve), ulnar nerve at the elbow, peroneal nerve at the knee (foot drop)
Radiculopathy: one root Sciatica due to a herniated disc
Acute polyradiculoneuropathy Guillain-Barré: weakness that climbs over days
Small fibre neuropathy Burning pain with an almost normal examination

Typical symptoms are tingling, numbness, burning or shooting pain, weakness and loss of reflexes. Pain caused by nerve damage is neuropathic pain.

How they are tested

The main test is electromyography and nerve conduction studies, which distinguishes damage to the axon from damage to the myelin. It is completed with blood tests and sometimes genetic tests, nerve ultrasound, skin biopsy (small fibres) or nerve biopsy.
