Anatomy

The neuron

The cell that thinks: it receives signals through its dendrites, integrates them in the cell body and sends them along the axon. The brain has about 86 billion of them.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The unit of the nervous system

The neuron is the cell specialised in receiving, processing and transmitting information through electrical and chemical signals. The human brain has about 86 billion neurons, and each can connect with thousands of others, giving in the order of 100 trillion connections, or synapses.

That the nervous system is made of individual cells was not obvious. At the end of the 19th century, Camillo Golgi argued that it formed a continuous network. Using Golgi's own stain, Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed that neurons are independent cells that communicate by contact without fusing: the neuron doctrine. The two shared the Nobel Prize in 1906. In the 1950s the electron microscope confirmed that Cajal was right.

Parts of a neuron

Part Function
Soma (cell body) Contains the nucleus and makes proteins
Dendrites Branches that receive signals from other neurons; covered in dendritic spines
Axon A single extension that sends the signal; it can measure from microns to a metre (from the spinal cord to the foot)
Axon hillock Where the decision to fire an action potential is made
Myelin sheath Insulation that speeds up conduction
Synaptic terminals Release neurotransmitters

Cajal formulated the law of dynamic polarisation: information enters through the dendrites and soma and leaves through the axon, always in the same direction.

Types of neuron

By function:

  • Sensory: carry information from the skin, muscles and organs to the central nervous system.
  • Motor: carry commands to muscles and glands. The motor neurons of the spinal cord are the ones lost in ALS and spinal muscular atrophy.
  • Interneurons: connect neurons to each other; they are the great majority.

By shape: pyramidal cells of the cortex, Purkinje cells of the cerebellum (with a spectacular dendritic tree), stellate cells, bipolar cells of the retina and more. Cajal drew hundreds of types.

By neurotransmitter: glutamatergic (excitatory), GABAergic (inhibitory), dopaminergic, cholinergic, serotonergic and so on.

A very demanding cell

  • Neurons use a lot of energy: the brain consumes 20% of the body's oxygen and glucose. Without blood supply they stop working within seconds and start to die within minutes, which is why in stroke "time is brain".
  • Most do not divide: they last a person's whole life. Although there is some neurogenesis in adults (in the hippocampus), it is not enough to replace lost neurons.
  • They transport proteins and organelles along the axon on a system of "rails" (microtubules). When this transport fails, or misfolded proteins build up (tau, alpha-synuclein, TDP-43), neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's or Parkinson's appear.

They are not alone

Neurons are only half of the cells in the brain. The other half are glial cells, which support, feed, insulate and defend them.
