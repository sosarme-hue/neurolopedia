Autonomic nervous system

The body's autopilot: it regulates the heart, blood pressure, breathing, digestion, sweating and bladder without us thinking about it. Sympathetic for action, parasympathetic for rest.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The autopilot

The autonomic nervous system controls the body's functions that do not depend on will: heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, digestion, sweating, pupil size, urination and sexual function. It works without our noticing, although some functions, such as breathing, we can also control. It is coordinated by the hypothalamus and the brainstem.

Its two main branches

They work like an accelerator and a brake: almost every organ receives both, and the balance between them tunes the response.

Organ Sympathetic ("fight or flight") Parasympathetic ("rest and digest")
Heart Speeds up, beats harder Slows down
Blood vessels Constricts (raises blood pressure) Little effect
Pupils Dilates Constricts
Airways Dilates Constricts
Digestion Slows Activates (saliva, gut movement)
Bladder Holds urine Empties the bladder
Sweat Activates —
Glucose Releases glucose Promotes its storage
Final neurotransmitter Noradrenaline (and adrenaline from the adrenal gland) Acetylcholine
  • The sympathetic branch leaves the thoracic and lumbar spinal cord and forms a chain of ganglia alongside the spine. It is activated by danger, exercise or stress.
  • The parasympathetic branch leaves the brainstem (mainly through the vagus nerve; see cranial nerves) and the sacral spinal cord. It predominates at rest.

The enteric nervous system

This is the third branch: a network of about 500 million neurons in the wall of the gut that can work independently. That is why it is called the "second brain". It communicates with the brain through the vagus nerve, in the so-called gut-brain axis. In Parkinson's, alpha-synuclein appears in the gut years before it reaches the brain, and constipation can be one of the first symptoms.

Autonomic reflexes

  • Baroreflex: sensors in the carotid artery and aorta detect blood pressure. When you stand up, blood pools in the legs and pressure drops; within seconds the reflex speeds up the heart and constricts the vessels so that the brain keeps getting blood.
  • The pupil's reflex to light, salivation, urination.

When it fails: dysautonomia

Problem Symptoms
Orthostatic hypotension Dizziness or fainting on standing; a drop in blood pressure of more than 20/10 mmHg within 3 minutes
Vasovagal syncope Fainting due to an exaggerated vagal response (pain, blood, heat, standing for a long time)
Postural tachycardia syndrome (PoTS) Pulse rising by more than 30 beats on standing, tiredness, brain fog; common in young women and after COVID-19
Sweating problems Lack of sweating or excessive sweating
Bladder and bowel Urgency, retention, constipation, diarrhoea
Erectile dysfunction

The main causes are:

  • Diabetes (autonomic neuropathy) and other neuropathies, such as amyloidosis.
  • Parkinson's and multiple system atrophy, one of the atypical parkinsonisms, where autonomic failure is severe; also dementia with Lewy bodies.
  • High spinal cord injury: it can cause autonomic dysreflexia, sudden rises in blood pressure triggered by a stimulus below the injury (a full bladder), which is an emergency.
  • Guillain-Barré: heart rhythm problems and sudden swings in blood pressure.
  • Medicines: for blood pressure, the prostate, antidepressants.

What helps with orthostatic hypotension

Getting up slowly, in two stages; drinking enough water; not overdoing alcohol; compression stockings; raising the head of the bed; reviewing medication. Some patients need extra salt or medicines.
