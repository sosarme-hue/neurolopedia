Corpus callosum and hemispheres

The two cerebral hemispheres are not the same and communicate through the corpus callosum, a bridge of 200 million fibres. What "split-brain" patients taught us, and the myths about the left and right brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Two connected halves

The brain is divided into two hemispheres, left and right, separated by the longitudinal fissure. At first glance they look almost the same, but they are not identical and do not do exactly the same things. Each hemisphere controls and receives sensation from the opposite half of the body, because the pathways cross in the brainstem and spinal cord, and each receives information from the opposite half of the visual field.

The corpus callosum

This is the great bridge joining the two hemispheres: an arch-shaped bundle of about 200 million nerve fibres (myelinated axons) that lets the two halves share information and work as one. It is the largest white matter structure in the brain. There are other smaller connections, such as the anterior commissure.

What each hemisphere does

The specialisation of each hemisphere is called lateralisation:

Left hemisphere (in most people) Right hemisphere
Language: grammar, vocabulary, speech Intonation and emotional aspects of language (prosody), irony, metaphor
Reading, writing, calculation Spatial attention to both sides of space
Planning gestures (praxis) Recognising faces
Analysing details Seeing the whole picture, orientation

Language is in the left hemisphere in 95% of right-handed people and in about 70% of left-handed people. This was first suggested by the observations of Paul Broca in 1861.

This specialisation has given rise to the myth that there are "left-brained" (logical) and "right-brained" (creative) people. It is not true: almost every task uses both hemispheres at once, as we explain in brain myths.

"Split-brain" patients

In the 1960s, to treat very severe epilepsy, some patients had their corpus callosum cut (callosotomy) to stop seizures spreading from one hemisphere to the other. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga studied these patients and found that, in the laboratory, each hemisphere behaved as if it were independent:

  • If an object was shown only to the right hemisphere (in the left visual field), the patient could not name it, because language was in the left, but could pick it up with the left hand.
  • The left hemisphere made up explanations for what the right was doing, a function Gazzaniga called the "interpreter".

Sperry received the Nobel Prize in 1981. In daily life these patients behaved normally, because they used other cues to compensate. Today callosotomy is rarely used, in some epilepsies with drop attacks.

When one hemisphere is damaged

  • Left hemisphere: weakness on the right side and aphasia (language disorder).
  • Right hemisphere: weakness on the left side and often neglect: the patient ignores everything on their left (eating only half the plate, shaving half the face) and may not be aware of their illness (anosognosia), which makes rehabilitation harder.

Both are common after a stroke of the middle cerebral artery. See cerebral circulation and cerebral cortex.

Diseases of the corpus callosum

  • Agenesis of the corpus callosum: being born without it; some people have few symptoms, because the brain develops other connections.
  • It is affected in multiple sclerosis, with typical lesions on MRI, in tumours such as glioblastoma ("butterfly" pattern) and in alcoholism (Marchiafava-Bignami disease).
