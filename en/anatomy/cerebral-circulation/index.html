Cerebral circulation

The brain is 2% of the body's weight but receives 15-20% of its blood. Four arteries, joined in the circle of Willis, supply it. Why a few minutes without blood are enough to damage it.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A very thirsty organ

The brain is 2% of the body's weight but receives 15-20% of the blood pumped by the heart: about 750 ml a minute. It has no reserves of oxygen or glucose, so it depends on a continuous supply. If blood stops arriving, consciousness is lost in about 10 seconds and neurons start to die within a few minutes. That is why stroke is an emergency: "time is brain".

The four incoming arteries

System Arteries What it supplies
Anterior (carotid) Two internal carotids, which divide into the anterior and middle cerebral arteries The front two thirds of the hemispheres, the eyes
Posterior (vertebrobasilar) Two vertebrals, which join to form the basilar, which divides into the posterior cerebral arteries Brainstem, cerebellum, occipital lobe, thalamus

The circle of Willis

At the base of the brain the arteries of the two systems connect to form a ring, the circle of Willis, described by Thomas Willis in 1664. It works like a roundabout: if an artery slowly becomes blocked, blood can arrive by another route. It is complete in slightly less than half of people. It is also where most aneurysms form, at the branching points. See subarachnoid haemorrhage and aneurysms.

Territories and symptoms

Each artery supplies an area, and the symptoms of a stroke depend on the artery affected:

Artery Typical symptoms when it is blocked
Middle cerebral (the most often affected) Weakness and loss of sensation in the face and arm on the opposite side; aphasia if on the left; neglect if on the right
Anterior cerebral Weakness of the leg on the opposite side; apathy
Posterior cerebral Loss of half the visual field
Basilar Coma, tetraplegia, double vision; very serious
Vertebral and cerebellar Vertigo, unsteadiness, difficulty swallowing
Small perforating arteries Lacunar infarcts: pure weakness or pure loss of sensation on one side of the body

Venous drainage

Blood leaves the brain through veins that empty into the venous sinuses of the dura mater and from there into the jugular veins. Blockage of these sinuses causes cerebral venous thrombosis, more common in young women.

Autoregulation

The brain keeps its blood flow stable even when blood pressure rises or falls within a range (roughly 60 to 150 mmHg mean pressure) by constricting or dilating its arterioles. It also increases blood supply where neurons are most active (neurovascular coupling). This is the basis of functional MRI. In long-standing high blood pressure the range shifts, which is why blood pressure should not be lowered abruptly after a stroke.

Diseases of the cerebral blood vessels

  • Ischaemic stroke due to atherosclerosis, embolism from the heart or small vessel disease; TIA.
  • Brain haemorrhage.
  • Cervical artery dissection: an important cause of stroke in young people.
  • Arteriovenous malformations, amyloid angiopathy, vasculitis, moyamoya.
  • Vascular dementia.

The arteries are examined with Doppler ultrasound, CT angiography and angiography.
