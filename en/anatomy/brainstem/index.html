Anatomy

Brainstem

It connects the brain with the spinal cord and controls vital functions such as breathing, heart rate and consciousness. Almost every pathway passes through it, and most cranial nerves arise from it.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The stalk that connects everything

The brainstem connects the brain with the spinal cord. It is about 7-8 centimetres long, barely the thickness of a thumb, yet the most vital functions are packed into that tiny space. All the pathways running up and down between the brain and the body pass through it, and ten of the twelve cranial nerves arise from it. The cerebellum sits behind it.

Its three parts

Part Key functions Cranial nerves
Midbrain (top) Eye movements, visual and auditory reflexes; contains the substantia nigra III and IV
Pons (bridge, middle) Links with the cerebellum; chewing, facial sensation, facial expression, REM sleep V, VI, VII and VIII
Medulla oblongata (bottom) Breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing, coughing, vomiting IX, X, XI and XII

In the medulla the motor pathways cross to the other side (the decussation of the pyramids): that is why the left hemisphere controls the right side of the body.

Vital functions

  • Breathing: the respiratory centres of the medulla and pons set the rhythm of breathing automatically.
  • Heart and blood vessels: they regulate heart rate and blood pressure through the autonomic nervous system and the vagus nerve.
  • Protective reflexes: coughing, swallowing, vomiting, blinking.
  • Arousal and consciousness: the reticular formation, a network of neurons running through the whole brainstem, keeps the cortex awake. If it is damaged, the person falls into a coma. See consciousness.
  • Sleep: nuclei in the pons generate REM sleep and the muscle paralysis during dreams.
  • Neurotransmitters: brainstem nuclei send out the serotonin (raphe nuclei), noradrenaline (locus coeruleus) and dopamine (substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area) fibres that reach the whole brain.

When it is damaged

Because everything is so tightly packed, a small lesion can cause very striking symptoms:

  • Crossed syndromes: a cranial nerve problem on one side and weakness or loss of sensation on the opposite side of the body. They are typical of brainstem strokes.
  • Double vision, vertigo, difficulty swallowing or speaking, persistent hiccups.
  • Locked-in syndrome: damage to the front of the pons paralyses the whole body except the eyes, with consciousness intact. The person can communicate by blinking or with vertical eye movements, and nowadays with brain-computer interfaces.
  • Coma, if the reticular formation is affected.
  • Brain death: its diagnosis is based on the absence of all brainstem reflexes and of breathing.

Causes

  • Stroke in the vertebrobasilar territory: basilar artery occlusion is one of the most serious strokes, and thrombectomy can save lives. See cerebral circulation.
  • Multiple sclerosis: brainstem lesions are common.
  • Herniation due to a tumour, haemorrhage or injury that pushes the brain downwards.
  • Atypical parkinsonisms such as progressive supranuclear palsy (midbrain atrophy), bulbar ALS, Chiari malformation, tumours (brainstem gliomas in children), encephalitis.
