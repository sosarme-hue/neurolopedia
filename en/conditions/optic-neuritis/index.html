Optic neuritis

Inflammation of the optic nerve that causes loss of vision in one eye with pain on moving it. It is often the first symptom of multiple sclerosis.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Optic neuritis is inflammation of the optic nerve, the cable that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. It mainly affects young adults (20-45), more often women, with about 5 cases per 100,000 people a year. It is the most common cause of sudden, painful loss of vision in one eye in young people.

Symptoms

  • Loss of vision in one eye developing over hours or days: blurred vision, as if through a fog, or a spot in the centre.
  • Pain on moving the eye, in 90% of cases.
  • Colours look dull or washed out, especially red.
  • Sometimes flashes of light with eye movements.
  • Vision can get worse with heat or exercise (Uhthoff's phenomenon).

Causes

  • Multiple sclerosis: optic neuritis is the first symptom in about 20% of patients, and half will have it at some point during the disease. After optic neuritis, the risk of developing multiple sclerosis depends mainly on the brain MRI: it is high if there are lesions and low if it is normal.
  • Neuromyelitis optica (anti-AQP4 antibodies) and MOGAD (anti-MOG antibodies): these usually cause more severe neuritis, in both eyes or recurrent.
  • Infections (syphilis, Lyme disease, viruses), autoimmune diseases (sarcoidosis, lupus).
  • Idiopathic: no cause found.

Diagnosis

  • Eye examination: visual acuity, colour vision, visual field, relative afferent pupillary defect (the pupil reacts less when light is shone into the affected eye). The back of the eye is usually normal at first.
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT), which measures the layers of the retina.
  • MRI of the orbits and brain, key to assessing the risk of multiple sclerosis.
  • Anti-AQP4 and anti-MOG antibodies.
  • Visual evoked potentials, in some cases.

Atypical signs

These suggest another cause and call for further tests: involvement of both eyes, no pain, very severe loss of vision, marked swelling of the nerve or retina on fundoscopy, or no improvement after three weeks.

Treatment

  • High-dose intravenous corticosteroids for 3-5 days: they speed up recovery, although they do not change final vision in typical neuritis (ONTT study). They are used mainly when vision loss is significant.
  • In neuromyelitis optica and MOGAD, treatment is urgent and intensive, sometimes with plasma exchange.
  • If multiple sclerosis is diagnosed, a disease-modifying treatment is started. See treatments for multiple sclerosis.

Outlook

In typical optic neuritis, vision improves on its own in most people from 2-3 weeks onwards, and about 90% regain good visual acuity after a year, although a slight change in colour or contrast vision may remain.
