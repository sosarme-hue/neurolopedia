Transient ischaemic attack

Stroke symptoms that go away on their own within minutes. It is not a harmless scare but a warning: the risk of a stroke in the following days is high.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A transient ischaemic attack (TIA), sometimes called a "mini-stroke", is a brief episode of neurological symptoms caused by a lack of blood supply to part of the brain, spinal cord or retina, which recovers completely and leaves no lesion on MRI. A clot blocks an artery for a few minutes and then breaks up or moves on.

Most last less than an hour, and many less than ten minutes. Up to 24 hours used to be accepted, but MRI showed that many longer episodes did leave a small infarct, and the current definition is based on the absence of a lesion, not on the clock.

Why it is an emergency

A TIA is the best warning the body gives before a stroke. Without treatment, up to 10% of people have a stroke in the following 90 days, and half of those strokes occur in the first 48 hours. When investigation and treatment are done the same day, in TIA clinics or stroke units, that risk falls to under 2% (the EXPRESS and SOS-TIA studies). That is why it is treated as an emergency even if the person already feels well: call 112 or go to A&E immediately.

Symptoms

They are the same as those of a stroke, but temporary. They come on suddenly:

  • Weakness or clumsiness of the face, arm or leg on one side.
  • Loss of sensation or tingling on one side of the body.
  • Difficulty speaking or understanding.
  • Sudden loss of vision in one eye, like a curtain coming down (amaurosis fugax): this usually points to a problem in the carotid artery on that side.
  • Loss of half of the visual field, double vision, vertigo with unsteadiness or difficulty swallowing, if the posterior circulation is affected.

What is probably not a TIA

Some isolated symptoms are rarely due to a TIA: dizziness on its own, loss of consciousness (more typical of syncope), isolated confusion, tingling that "travels" across the body over minutes (more typical of migraine aura) or isolated memory loss lasting hours (transient global amnesia). Even so, if in doubt, seek advice.

Causes

The same as those of ischaemic stroke:

  • Atherosclerosis of the carotid, vertebral or intracranial arteries.
  • Atrial fibrillation and other sources of emboli from the heart.
  • Small-vessel disease due to high blood pressure.
  • Less common causes: arterial dissection, clotting disorders, vasculitis.

How the risk is estimated

The ABCD² score adds points for age (60 or over), blood pressure on arrival (140/90 or higher), symptoms (weakness or speech disturbance), duration (more than 60 minutes) and diabetes. We now know it is not enough to decide who should be investigated: all patients with a recent TIA should be assessed urgently. Imaging and the finding of carotid stenosis or atrial fibrillation identify the people at highest risk better.

Diagnosis

Ideally within the first 24 hours:

  • MRI with diffusion imaging, which shows whether a small infarct has been left behind (if so, it is considered a stroke).
  • Imaging of the arteries of the neck and brain with Doppler ultrasound, CT angiography or MR angiography.
  • Electrocardiogram and heart rhythm monitoring to look for atrial fibrillation, sometimes for weeks.
  • Blood tests: glucose, cholesterol, kidney function and clotting.
  • Echocardiogram in selected cases.

Treatment

The aim is to prevent the stroke that the TIA is warning of. Treatment starts immediately:

  • Antiplatelet drugs: in a high-risk TIA, aspirin and clopidogrel together for 21 days (the CHANCE and POINT trials), then one alone.
  • Anticoagulants if the cause is atrial fibrillation.
  • High-dose statins.
  • Control of blood pressure, diabetes and smoking.
  • Endarterectomy or stenting if there is significant carotid stenosis on the side of the symptoms, within the first two weeks.

See stroke prevention.

After a TIA

Even if it leaves no visible lesion, many people notice tiredness, anxiety or difficulty concentrating for weeks. This is normal and usually improves. The most important thing is to keep taking preventive treatment long term and to go to A&E if the symptoms return. Ask your doctor before driving again.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What was the likely cause of the episode?
  • Do I have a narrowed artery? Do I need surgery?
  • How long should I take the two antiplatelet drugs?
  • What should I do if the symptoms come back?
