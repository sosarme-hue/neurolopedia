Cerebral venous thrombosis

A clot blocks one of the veins that drain the brain. It mainly affects young women, starts with a headache and is treated with anticoagulants.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Cerebral venous thrombosis is the formation of a clot in the veins or venous sinuses that collect blood from the brain to return it to the heart. Because the blood cannot drain, pressure inside the skull rises, and the affected area can swell, suffer an infarct or bleed. It accounts for about 0.5-1% of strokes, with about 1-2 cases per 100,000 people a year.

Unlike arterial strokes, it mainly affects young people: the average age is about 40, and 75% are women.

Risk factors

  • Hormonal contraceptives containing oestrogen, the most common factor in young women.
  • Pregnancy and the postnatal period.
  • Thrombophilias: inherited or acquired clotting disorders (factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutation, antiphospholipid syndrome).
  • Infections of the head and neck: ear infections, mastoiditis, sinusitis.
  • Cancer and some cancer treatments.
  • Dehydration, inflammatory diseases (lupus, Behçet's disease, inflammatory bowel disease), obesity.
  • Head injuries and surgery, lumbar puncture.
  • In 2021 a rare form associated with adenovirus COVID-19 vaccines (thrombosis with thrombocytopenia) was described; it has practically disappeared now that these vaccines are no longer used.

Symptoms

  • Headache: the most common symptom (90%). It is usually progressive over days, sometimes sudden, and gets worse when lying down or with effort.
  • Seizures: in about 40%, more than in other strokes.
  • Focal symptoms: weakness, problems with speech or vision.
  • Blurred or double vision due to raised intracranial pressure (papilloedema).
  • Drowsiness or confusion in severe cases.

Symptoms can be variable and progress over days, which delays diagnosis. A new, persistent and different headache in a woman taking contraceptives or after giving birth should be checked. See when to worry about a headache.

Diagnosis

  • CT venography or MR venography: shows the blocked vein.
  • A CT scan without contrast may be normal.
  • Clotting tests, testing for thrombophilia in selected cases and for other causes.

Treatment

  • Immediate anticoagulation with heparin, even if there is associated bleeding, because it stops the clot growing and allows the vein to reopen.
  • Then oral anticoagulants for 3 to 12 months, or for life if there is a severe thrombophilia or recurrences. Direct oral anticoagulants (dabigatran) have been shown to be as effective as the traditional ones.
  • Treating the cause: stopping oestrogen-containing contraceptives, antibiotics if there is infection.
  • Antiseizure medicines if there are seizures.
  • In severe cases: venous thrombectomy or decompressive surgery.

Outlook

It is better than for other strokes: about 80% of patients recover with no significant or only mild after-effects. Some are left with headache, tiredness, cognitive problems or epilepsy. The risk of recurrence is low (2-5% a year). After cerebral venous thrombosis, oestrogen-containing contraceptives are not recommended; in a future pregnancy preventive heparin is usually used.
