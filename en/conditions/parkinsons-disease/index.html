Parkinson's disease

The fastest-growing neurodegenerative disease in the world. Tremor, slowness and stiffness, but also non-motor symptoms. Diagnosis, treatment with levodopa and other options, and daily life.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Parkinson's disease is a neurodegenerative disease in which the neurons that make dopamine in a small area of the brainstem, the substantia nigra, are gradually lost. Dopamine is essential for the basal ganglia to control movement. It is the fastest-growing neurodegenerative disease in the world: it affects more than 10 million people, and in Spain about 160,000-200,000. It usually starts around 60, although one in ten patients develop it before 50.

It was described in 1817 by the English physician James Parkinson in his "Essay on the Shaking Palsy".

What happens in the brain

By the time motor symptoms appear, 50 to 70% of dopamine-producing neurons have already been lost. In the remaining neurons the protein alpha-synuclein builds up, forming Lewy bodies. Many researchers believe the process begins years earlier in the gut or the olfactory bulb, which explains some early symptoms.

Motor symptoms

  • Bradykinesia: slowness and clumsiness of movement, which becomes smaller when repeated. It is essential for the diagnosis.
  • Resting tremor: in one hand, like counting coins, which improves when the hand moves. It affects 70% of patients, but not all.
  • Muscle rigidity.
  • Walking problems: short steps, shuffling, reduced arm swing and, in advanced stages, freezing of gait and falls.
  • Others: a less expressive face, a quiet monotonous voice, small handwriting (micrographia), difficulty turning over in bed.

Symptoms almost always start on one side of the body.

Non-motor symptoms

These are as important as the motor symptoms and often harder to treat:

  • Loss of smell, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder and depression, which can appear years before the motor symptoms.
  • Anxiety, apathy, fatigue, pain.
  • Daytime sleepiness and insomnia.
  • Low blood pressure on standing, urinary and sexual problems, excessive sweating.
  • Cognitive impairment and dementia in advanced stages, and sometimes hallucinations.

Causes and risk factors

  • Age: the main factor.
  • Genetics: 10 to 15% of patients have an identifiable genetic cause. The most common are variants in the LRRK2 gene (more frequent in some populations, such as in the Basque Country) and GBA1. See is Parkinson's hereditary?.
  • Environment: exposure to some pesticides (such as paraquat) and solvents (trichloroethylene), and repeated head injuries.
  • Factors associated with lower risk: physical exercise, coffee and, paradoxically, smoking (which is obviously not recommended).

Diagnosis

It is clinical: based on examination by a neurologist. Tests help in doubtful cases:

  • DaTSCAN: shows the loss of dopamine nerve endings; distinguishes Parkinson's from essential tremor.
  • MRI to rule out other causes.
  • The alpha-synuclein seed amplification test (RT-QuIC) in cerebrospinal fluid or skin, increasingly used.
  • A good response to levodopa supports the diagnosis.

It must be distinguished from other forms of parkinsonism: drug-induced (some antipsychotics, metoclopramide, cinnarizine and flunarizine), vascular and atypical parkinsonism (multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration), which respond less well to treatment.

Treatment

No treatment slows the disease, but symptoms are well controlled for many years. See levodopa and Parkinson's medicines.

  • Levodopa: the most effective medicine. After years of use, fluctuations (the effect wears off sooner, the "off state") and dyskinesias (involuntary movements) may appear.
  • Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine): useful in early stages; they can cause sleepiness and impulse control disorders (gambling, shopping, sex, food), which need monitoring.
  • MAO-B inhibitors (rasagiline, safinamide) and COMT inhibitors (entacapone, opicapone) to prolong the effect of levodopa.
  • Amantadine for dyskinesias.
  • Advanced therapies in stages with fluctuations: deep brain stimulation, focused ultrasound, continuous infusion of levodopa gel through an intestinal tube or under the skin (foslevodopa), or of apomorphine.

Exercise is treatment

Regular, vigorous physical exercise (brisk walking, cycling, dancing, adapted boxing, tai chi) improves mobility, balance and mood, and probably slows progression. Physiotherapy, speech and language therapy (voice and swallowing) and occupational therapy also help.

Living with Parkinson's

  • Take medication at exactly the right times.
  • On hospital admission, make sure levodopa is not interrupted and that medicines that counteract it are avoided.
  • Prevent falls and constipation.
  • Patient associations offer information, therapies and support.
