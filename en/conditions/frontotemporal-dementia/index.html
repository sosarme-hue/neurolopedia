Frontotemporal dementia

A dementia that usually starts before 65 with changes in personality and behaviour or with language problems, rather than forgetfulness.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a group of neurodegenerative diseases that mainly affect the brain's frontal and temporal lobes, the areas that regulate behaviour, personality, judgement and language. It is one of the most common causes of dementia in people under 65: it usually starts between 45 and 65, in the middle of working and family life. Memory can be well preserved at first, which delays diagnosis.

Clinical forms

Behavioural variant

The most common. The first changes are noticed by family and colleagues:

  • Disinhibition: inappropriate comments or behaviour, impulsiveness, excessive spending, embarrassing social behaviour.
  • Apathy and loss of initiative.
  • Loss of empathy: coldness towards the family, indifference to other people's suffering.
  • Repetitive or compulsive behaviours, rituals, hoarding.
  • Changes in eating: binge eating, a preference for sweet foods, eating non-food objects.
  • Lack of awareness of the changes.

At first it is often mistaken for depression, a midlife crisis or a psychiatric disorder.

Primary progressive aphasias

These mainly affect language:

  • Semantic variant: the meaning of words and objects is lost. The person speaks fluently but with vague words ("that thing") and does not understand words they used to know.
  • Non-fluent or agrammatic variant: speech becomes effortful, slow and halting, with grammatical errors.

Link with ALS and parkinsonism

About 15% of patients with FTD also develop amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and many patients with ALS have frontal changes. Other forms are associated with parkinsonism (progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration).

Causes

Abnormal proteins (mainly tau or TDP-43) build up in frontal and temporal neurons. Between 30 and 40% of cases have a family history, and in a significant proportion a mutation is found in genes such as C9orf72, GRN or MAPT. This is why genetic counselling is offered. See genetic testing in neurology.

Diagnosis

  • A detailed history from the family: behavioural changes are the key.
  • Neuropsychological and language assessment.
  • MRI: frontal or temporal atrophy, often asymmetrical.
  • FDG PET: shows reduced activity in those areas, useful in early stages.
  • Biomarkers to rule out Alzheimer's and, where appropriate, genetic testing.

Treatment

No treatment slows the disease. Alzheimer's medicines are not helpful and may even worsen behaviour. Symptoms are treated:

  • Antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram) and trazodone for disinhibition, compulsiveness and agitation.
  • Speech and language therapy for the aphasias.
  • Behaviour management strategies: routines, supervision, controlling access to money, the car and food.
  • Support for the family: personality changes are especially hard for those around the person. Family associations and psychological help are essential.
  • Treatments aimed at genetic causes are being researched, such as medicines that increase progranulin in carriers of GRN mutations.
