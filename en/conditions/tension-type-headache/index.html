Tension-type headache

The most common headache: a band-like pressure of mild or moderate intensity that does not get worse with movement. Why it happens and how to relieve it without overusing painkillers.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

What it is

Tension-type headache is the most common headache: almost 80% of people have it at some point, and about 3% have it almost daily. It is a pressing pain, like a band or helmet squeezing the head, of mild or moderate intensity, on both sides, which does not get worse with physical activity and does not usually come with nausea.

Despite its name, it is not caused only by muscle "tension" or stress, although both play a part. Increased sensitivity of the muscles around the skull and, in chronic forms, altered pain processing in the nervous system are thought to be involved.

Types

Type Frequency
Infrequent episodic Less than 1 day a month
Frequent episodic 1 to 14 days a month
Chronic 15 or more days a month for more than 3 months

Symptoms

  • Pain on both sides of the head, in the forehead, temples or back of the neck.
  • A feeling of pressure or tightness, not throbbing.
  • Mild or moderate intensity: you can carry on with your activities.
  • Lasts from 30 minutes to several days.
  • Sometimes slight sensitivity to light or noise (but not both), and tenderness of the muscles of the head, neck and shoulders.

Triggers

  • Stress, anxiety and depression.
  • Poor or short sleep.
  • Sustained postures, such as hours at the computer.
  • Skipping meals, dehydration.
  • Too much caffeine or stopping it suddenly.

Diagnosis

It is based on the clinical history and a normal examination. It should be distinguished from migraine, which is more intense and throbbing, worsens with movement and comes with nausea. Many people have both. Imaging is only requested if there are red flags, such as sudden pain, a change in the pattern of pain or neurological symptoms. See when to worry about a headache.

Treatment

For attacks:

  • Ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin or paracetamol. Combining them with caffeine may be slightly more effective.
  • Do not take painkillers on more than 10-15 days a month: overuse can turn the headache into a daily one (medication-overuse headache).

Preventive, in the chronic or frequent episodic form:

  • Low-dose amitriptyline at night is the medicine with the most evidence. Alternatives: mirtazapine or venlafaxine.
  • Non-drug measures: regular aerobic exercise, physiotherapy, posture correction, relaxation techniques, biofeedback, cognitive behavioural therapy and good sleep. Acupuncture has shown a moderate benefit.
  • Treating anxiety and depression when present. See stress and the brain.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is this a tension-type headache or could it be migraine?
  • How many painkillers can I take a month?
  • Would preventive treatment help me?
