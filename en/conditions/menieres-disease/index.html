Ménière's disease

Attacks of vertigo lasting hours with hearing loss, tinnitus and a feeling of a blocked ear. Why it happens, how it is diagnosed and which treatments reduce the attacks.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. What it is
  2. Symptoms
  3. Diagnosis
  4. Treatment
  5. Sources

What it is

Ménière's disease is a chronic disorder of the inner ear that causes attacks of vertigo together with ear symptoms. It is associated with an excess of endolymph, the fluid that fills the labyrinth (endolymphatic hydrops). It affects about 1 in every 1,000-2,000 people, usually starts between 40 and 60 and is somewhat more common in women. It is named after the French physician Prosper Ménière, who showed in 1861 that vertigo could arise in the ear and not in the brain.

Symptoms

The attacks combine:

  • Intense vertigo, with a spinning sensation, nausea and vomiting, lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours.
  • Hearing loss in one ear, at first of low tones and fluctuating.
  • Tinnitus: ringing or noise in the ear.
  • Aural fullness: a feeling of a blocked ear or pressure.

Between attacks the person may be well, although over the years hearing gets permanently worse and unsteadiness may remain. In a third of patients the other ear eventually becomes affected. Some have Tumarkin crises: sudden falls to the ground without losing consciousness.

The attacks come in unpredictable clusters, which causes a lot of anxiety and interferes with work and social life.

Diagnosis

  • Medical history: two or more attacks of vertigo lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours, with hearing symptoms in the same ear.
  • Audiometry, showing low-tone hearing loss.
  • Vestibular tests (videonystagmography, head impulse test, myogenic potentials).
  • MRI to rule out a vestibular schwannoma; with intratympanic contrast it can show the hydrops.

It needs to be distinguished from vestibular migraine, which is very common and sometimes coexists. See vertigo and dizziness or vertigo?.

Treatment

No treatment cures the disease, but some reduce the attacks:

During an attack: rest, vestibular sedatives and anti-sickness medicines for as short a time as possible.

To prevent attacks (stepwise):

  1. A low-salt diet, avoiding caffeine and alcohol, good rest and stress management.
  2. Betahistine (its effectiveness is debated) or diuretics.
  3. Intratympanic corticosteroids: injected through the eardrum.
  4. Intratympanic gentamicin: destroys part of the balance organ in a controlled way to stop the attacks, with a risk to hearing.
  5. Surgery in resistant cases: endolymphatic sac decompression, vestibular nerve section or labyrinthectomy.

Hearing aids or a cochlear implant for hearing loss, and vestibular rehabilitation for unsteadiness. See treatment of vertigo.
