Hydrocephalus

An excessive build-up of cerebrospinal fluid in the brain's ventricles. In children it makes the head grow; in older people, normal pressure hydrocephalus causes a treatable triad of walking, memory and bladder problems.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Hydrocephalus is an excessive build-up of cerebrospinal fluid in the brain's ventricles, the cavities through which this fluid flows. The brain produces about half a litre of cerebrospinal fluid every day, which flows through the ventricles, bathes the brain and spinal cord and is reabsorbed into the blood. If its flow is blocked or it is not reabsorbed properly, the ventricles enlarge and compress the brain tissue. See meninges, ventricles and cerebrospinal fluid.

Types

  • Congenital or newborn: 1-2 in every 1,000 births. It is associated with spina bifida, malformations, brain haemorrhages in premature babies or infections.
  • Acquired: after a haemorrhage (especially subarachnoid), meningitis, a head injury or because of a tumour blocking the flow of fluid.
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus in adults: in older people, with no clear cause.

Depending on the mechanism, it can be obstructive (a blockage in the fluid's path) or communicating (the fluid flows, but is not reabsorbed).

Symptoms

In babies: rapid growth of the head, a bulging fontanelle, prominent scalp veins, eyes looking downwards ("sunset eyes"), irritability, vomiting, drowsiness.

In older children and adults: headache, vomiting, blurred or double vision, drowsiness, balance problems. Acute hydrocephalus is an emergency.

Normal pressure hydrocephalus: Hakim's triad

Described by the Colombian neurosurgeon Salomón Hakim in 1965, it affects people over 60 and causes three symptoms that progress slowly:

  1. Walking problems: short steps, feet apart, as if "stuck to the floor", unsteadiness and falls. This is usually the first.
  2. Urinary incontinence or urgency.
  3. Cognitive decline: slowness, apathy, inattention.

It is important because it is one of the few causes of walking and memory decline that can improve with surgery. It is often mistaken for ageing, Parkinson's or Alzheimer's.

Diagnosis

  • Cranial ultrasound through the fontanelle in babies, and prenatal diagnosis on pregnancy scans.
  • CT or MRI: enlarged ventricles.
  • In normal pressure hydrocephalus, a lumbar puncture tap test: 30-50 ml of fluid is removed to see whether walking improves over the following hours; or lumbar drainage over several days.

Treatment

  • Shunt: a thin tube that carries the excess fluid from the ventricles to the abdomen (ventriculoperitoneal shunt), where it is reabsorbed. It has a valve that regulates flow, often programmable from outside with a magnet. It is the most common treatment.
  • Endoscopic third ventriculostomy: a small hole is made in the floor of the third ventricle so that fluid can drain without a shunt. Useful in obstructive hydrocephalus.
  • Treating the cause where possible (removing a tumour).

Shunts can block or become infected and need revision. People with shunts and their families should know the symptoms of malfunction (headache, vomiting, drowsiness, worsening walking) and seek help quickly.

In well-selected normal pressure hydrocephalus, the shunt improves walking in more than 70% of patients; incontinence and memory improve less.

See neurosurgery.
