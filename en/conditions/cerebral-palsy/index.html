Cerebral palsy

A group of permanent disorders of movement and posture caused by damage to the developing brain, before, during or shortly after birth. It does not get worse over time, and early intervention makes the difference.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Cerebral palsy is a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture caused by damage to or a disturbance of the developing brain, before, during or shortly after birth. The damage does not progress, but its effects change as the child grows. It is the most common cause of physical disability in childhood: it affects about 2 in every 1,000 live births, although its frequency has fallen in recent years in high-income countries. In Spain about 120,000 people live with cerebral palsy.

It is often accompanied by other problems: epilepsy, intellectual disability, problems with vision, hearing, speech or swallowing, although many people with cerebral palsy have normal intelligence.

Causes and risk factors

  • Prematurity and low birth weight: the most important risk factor.
  • Lack of oxygen during birth (hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy), less common than was thought.
  • Infections during pregnancy (cytomegalovirus, rubella, toxoplasmosis) and in the newborn (meningitis).
  • Fetal or neonatal stroke.
  • Multiple pregnancies, brain malformations, severe jaundice.
  • In many cases no clear cause is identified, and in some genetic variants are found.

Types

By the type of movement disorder:

  • Spastic (the most common, 80%): stiff, tight muscles.
  • Hemiplegia: one side of the body.
  • Diplegia: mainly the legs (typical in premature babies).
  • Quadriplegia: all four limbs, the most severe form.
  • Dyskinetic: involuntary movements (dystonia, chorea).
  • Ataxic: lack of balance and coordination.
  • Mixed.

Severity is described with the GMFCS scale (levels I to V), from walking with minor limitations to needing a wheelchair and help with everything.

First signs

  • Delay in holding up the head, sitting, crawling or walking.
  • Preferring one hand before the age of one.
  • Stiffness or floppiness, abnormal postures.
  • Difficulty feeding or swallowing.

Today early diagnosis is possible, before 6 months, in at-risk babies, by combining MRI, assessment of the baby's general movements and neurological examination (HINE scale). This allows intervention to start earlier, when the brain is more plastic.

Treatment

There is no cure, but much can be done to improve function and quality of life. The approach is multidisciplinary and family-centred:

  • Early intervention and physiotherapy: stretching, strength, gait training, intensive goal-directed therapies.
  • Occupational therapy and constraint-induced therapy of the unaffected side in hemiplegia.
  • Speech and language therapy and augmentative communication systems.
  • Treatment of spasticity: botulinum toxin, oral or intrathecal baclofen, selective dorsal rhizotomy.
  • Orthoses (splints), orthopaedic surgery of the hips, feet and spine.
  • Treatment of epilepsy, pain, nutrition and breathing problems.
  • Educational support, school inclusion and assistive devices.

Prevention

  • Corticosteroids before birth and magnesium sulphate in threatened premature labour, which reduce the baby's risk of cerebral palsy.
  • Therapeutic hypothermia in newborns with encephalopathy due to lack of oxygen.
  • Rubella vaccination, preventing infections in pregnancy, high-quality newborn care.

Adult life

Cerebral palsy stays with a person for life. In adulthood, pain, fatigue, early osteoarthritis and loss of mobility can appear, so continuity of care is important. Associations, such as the ASPACE Confederation in Spain or Scope in the UK, offer services and defend the rights of people with cerebral palsy.
