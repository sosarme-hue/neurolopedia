Ataxias

Lack of coordination of movement due to a problem in the cerebellum or its connections. It can be caused by alcohol, a stroke, medicines or inherited diseases such as Friedreich's ataxia.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Ataxia means lack of coordination. People with ataxia have difficulty coordinating movements: they walk unsteadily with their legs apart, as if drunk, are clumsy with their hands, speak in a slurred or scanning way (separating the syllables) and may have abnormal eye movements. It is almost always due to a problem in the cerebellum or its connections, although it can also be due to loss of deep sensation (sensory ataxia) or of the inner ear.

Ataxia is a symptom with many causes. Some are acute and treatable; others are inherited, degenerative diseases, the hereditary ataxias, which affect about 10 people in every 100,000.

Causes

Acquired

  • Alcohol: the most common cause of chronic ataxia in adults. It mainly affects walking.
  • Medicines and toxins: phenytoin, lithium, some cancer drugs, heavy metals.
  • Vitamin deficiencies: B1 (thiamine), B12, E.
  • Stroke or haemorrhage in the cerebellum: sudden-onset ataxia, an emergency.
  • Multiple sclerosis, tumours, infections.
  • Autoimmune: gluten ataxia (in people with coeliac disease), ataxia associated with anti-GAD antibodies and paraneoplastic ataxias, which can be the first sign of cancer.
  • Hypothyroidism.
  • Cerebellar-type multiple system atrophy.

Inherited

  • Friedreich's ataxia: the most common recessive form. It starts in childhood or adolescence, with ataxia, loss of reflexes, deformity of the feet and spine, heart involvement (cardiomyopathy) and sometimes diabetes. It is caused by a GAA repeat in the FXN gene.
  • Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCA): dominant, with more than 40 types. The most common in Spain are SCA3 (Machado-Joseph disease), SCA2 and SCA1.
  • Ataxia with RFC1 repeats (CANVAS syndrome): ataxia, neuropathy, vestibular impairment and chronic cough; a common cause of late-onset ataxia.
  • SCA27B (repeats in FGF14): discovered in 2023, late-onset and slowly progressive.
  • Ataxia-telangiectasia and other childhood ataxias.

Diagnosis

  • Neurological examination: walking, finger-nose test, alternating movements, speech, eyes.
  • MRI: atrophy of the cerebellum, lesions.
  • Blood tests: vitamins, thyroid, coeliac and anti-GAD antibodies, onconeural antibodies.
  • Genetic tests, including panels or exome sequencing and testing for repeats.
  • Electromyography to see whether there is neuropathy.

Treatment

  • Treat the cause where possible: stopping alcohol, replacing vitamins, a gluten-free diet, immunotherapy in autoimmune forms, treating a tumour.
  • Omaveloxolone: the first medicine approved for Friedreich's ataxia (in the USA in 2023 and in Europe in 2024). It moderately slows progression.
  • Rehabilitation: intensive coordination and balance physiotherapy improves walking and reduces falls. Speech and language therapy and occupational therapy also help. See neurorehabilitation.
  • Mobility aids: sticks, walking frames, home adaptations.
  • In Friedreich's ataxia, regular cardiology follow-up.
  • Gene therapies and oligonucleotides are being researched for Friedreich's ataxia and some SCAs.
