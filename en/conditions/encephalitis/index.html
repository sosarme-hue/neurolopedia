Encephalitis

Inflammation of the brain itself, caused by viruses or by the immune system. Fever with confusion, strange behaviour or seizures means going to A&E. Autoimmune encephalitis was discovered less than twenty years ago.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Encephalitis is inflammation of the brain. Unlike meningitis, which inflames the membranes covering it, here the brain tissue itself is affected, so changes in behaviour, consciousness and seizures appear. It affects about 5-10 people per 100,000 each year and is a medical emergency. It has two main groups of causes: infections and autoimmunity, which is now known to be almost as common as infection.

Infectious encephalitis

  • Herpes simplex virus: the most common severe sporadic encephalitis in Europe. It mainly affects the temporal lobes.
  • Varicella-zoster, enteroviruses, cytomegalovirus and other herpesviruses.
  • Viruses transmitted by mosquitoes or ticks (arboviruses): West Nile virus (with cases in Spain, especially in Andalusia), tick-borne encephalitis (central and northern Europe), Japanese encephalitis in Asia.
  • Rabies: almost always fatal once symptoms appear, but preventable with a vaccine.
  • Bacteria such as Listeria, tuberculosis, syphilis; parasites such as toxoplasmosis in people with weakened immunity.

Autoimmune encephalitis

The immune system makes antibodies against proteins of the neurons:

  • Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis: the most common. It mainly affects young women and children. It starts with psychiatric symptoms (anxiety, hallucinations, strange behaviour) that can be mistaken for a psychotic episode, and continues with seizures, abnormal movements, reduced consciousness and autonomic disturbances. In some women it is associated with an ovarian teratoma. It was described in 2007 by the Spanish neurologist Josep Dalmau.
  • Anti-LGI1 encephalitis: in older people, with memory problems, low sodium and characteristic brief seizures of the face and arm.
  • Others: anti-GABA-B, anti-CASPR2, anti-AMPA, and paraneoplastic forms, associated with cancer.
  • It can appear after herpes encephalitis.

Symptoms

  • Fever and headache (in infectious forms).
  • Confusion, disorientation, drowsiness or coma.
  • Changes in behaviour and personality, hallucinations, agitation.
  • Loss of recent memory.
  • Seizures, sometimes status epilepticus.
  • Language problems, weakness, abnormal movements.

Diagnosis

  • Lumbar puncture: inflammation of the cerebrospinal fluid, PCR for viruses (herpes and others) and autoimmune antibodies.
  • MRI: typical lesions, for example in the temporal lobes in herpes.
  • Electroencephalogram: epileptic activity or slowing.
  • Antibodies in blood and fluid; search for tumours (gynaecological ultrasound, CT or PET).

Treatment

  • Intravenous aciclovir as soon as it is suspected, without waiting for results: it reduces mortality from herpes encephalitis from more than 70% to less than 20%.
  • Autoimmune encephalitis: corticosteroids, immunoglobulins or plasma exchange; if there is no response, rituximab or cyclophosphamide. Remove the tumour if there is one.
  • Treatment of seizures, intensive care support if needed.
  • Cognitive and behavioural rehabilitation.

See treatment of nervous system infections.

Outlook

It depends on the cause and on how quickly treatment is given. Many people with anti-NMDA encephalitis recover well, although it may take months or a couple of years. After-effects on memory, epilepsy, fatigue and emotional changes are common and need follow-up and rehabilitation.

Prevention

Vaccines against measles, chickenpox, rabies (after exposure or for high-risk travel), Japanese encephalitis and tick-borne encephalitis for travellers; protection against mosquitoes and ticks.
