Dementia with Lewy bodies

A dementia with fluctuating attention, visual hallucinations, Parkinson-like stiffness and restless sleep. Very sensitive to antipsychotics.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Dementia with Lewy bodies is the second or third most common neurodegenerative dementia, after Alzheimer's disease. It is caused by the build-up in neurons of the protein alpha-synuclein, which forms clumps called Lewy bodies, the same ones found in Parkinson's disease. For this reason the two are considered part of a single spectrum: if dementia appears before or at the same time as the motor symptoms, it is called dementia with Lewy bodies; if it appears years after Parkinson's, it is called Parkinson's disease dementia.

It usually starts after 50 and is somewhat more common in men. It is an underdiagnosed disease: it is often mistaken for Alzheimer's or a psychiatric disorder.

Characteristic symptoms

  • Fluctuations: attention and alertness vary greatly from one day to the next or over the course of a day. The person may be lucid in the morning and very confused or drowsy in the afternoon.
  • Detailed, recurrent visual hallucinations: people, children or animals that are not there. At first many patients know they are not real.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder: the person talks, shouts, kicks or punches while dreaming. It can appear years or decades before the other symptoms.
  • Parkinsonism: slowness, stiffness, walking problems; tremor is less common.

Other symptoms: problems with attention and spatial orientation (more than memory at first), falls, fainting and low blood pressure on standing, constipation, loss of smell, depression, anxiety and daytime sleepiness.

Sensitivity to antipsychotics

Up to half of patients have severe reactions to classic antipsychotics (such as haloperidol) and to some atypical ones: severe stiffness, confusion, falls and even a risk of death. This is why the diagnosis matters, and relatives should mention it in A&E and on any hospital admission.

Diagnosis

It is based on clinical symptoms and supported by tests:

  • DaTSCAN: shows a loss of dopamine nerve endings, which does not occur in Alzheimer's.
  • MIBG cardiac scintigraphy, abnormal because the nerves of the heart are affected.
  • Polysomnography confirming REM sleep behaviour disorder.
  • In research and increasingly in practice: the alpha-synuclein seed amplification test (RT-QuIC) in cerebrospinal fluid or skin biopsies.

Treatment

  • Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine, donepezil): especially effective in this dementia, because the acetylcholine deficit is greater than in Alzheimer's. They improve attention, fluctuations and hallucinations. See medicines for dementia.
  • Low-dose levodopa for motor symptoms, with care because it can worsen hallucinations.
  • If hallucinations cause distress: low-dose quetiapine or clozapine, avoiding other antipsychotics.
  • Melatonin or clonazepam for REM sleep behaviour disorder, and making the bedroom safe (removing objects, mattress on the floor).
  • Measures for orthostatic hypotension, constipation and fall prevention.
  • Physiotherapy, occupational therapy and support for carers.

Course

The disease progresses over an average of 5 to 8 years from diagnosis, with variation. Falls, swallowing difficulties and infections are the main complications.
