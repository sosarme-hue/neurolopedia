Sciatica and radiculopathy

Pain that runs down the leg or arm along the path of a compressed nerve root, almost always due to a herniated disc. Most cases improve without surgery within weeks.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A radiculopathy is irritation or compression of a nerve root where it leaves the spine. When it affects the lower lumbar roots that form the sciatic nerve (mainly L5 and S1), it causes sciatica: pain that runs from the lower back or buttock down the back or side of the leg, often to the foot. In the neck, cervical radiculopathy causes pain that runs down the arm.

Sciatica is very common: about 40% of people will have it at some point, mostly between 30 and 50.

Causes

  • Herniated disc: the most common cause in people under 50. The soft core of the intervertebral disc pushes out through a tear in its outer layer and presses on the root, which also becomes inflamed.
  • Narrowing of the spinal canal and of the openings where the roots exit, due to osteoarthritis of the spine: more common from age 60.
  • Spondylolisthesis: one vertebra slips over another.
  • Rarely: tumours, infections or fractures.

Symptoms

  • Leg pain, often worse than the back pain, following the path of the root.
  • Tingling or numbness in the foot or leg.
  • Weakness: difficulty walking on the heels (L5) or on tiptoe (S1).
  • It gets worse with coughing, sneezing or sitting for a long time.
  • Lumbar spinal stenosis causes a different picture: pain, heaviness or weakness of the legs when walking, forcing the person to stop, which improves when sitting or leaning forward (neurogenic claudication), for example leaning on a shopping trolley.

Warning signs: cauda equina syndrome

This is a surgical emergency. Go to A&E immediately if there is:

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or difficulty passing urine.
  • Numbness in the genital area, perineum or buttocks ("saddle" numbness).
  • Weakness of both legs, or weakness that is getting worse quickly.

Fever, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss or a significant injury also need urgent assessment.

Diagnosis

It is clinical: history and examination (the Lasègue or straight-leg-raise test reproduces the pain). Imaging is not needed at first, because most cases improve on their own and because disc herniations show up on MRI in many people without symptoms. An MRI is done if there are warning signs, progressive weakness, or if the pain does not improve after 6-8 weeks and surgery is being considered. Electromyography helps in doubtful cases.

Treatment

About 90% improve within 6-12 weeks without surgery:

  • Stay active as far as the pain allows: bed rest does not help and delays recovery.
  • Painkillers and anti-inflammatories for a short time. Medicines for neuropathic pain (pregabalin, gabapentin) have not been shown to help in acute sciatica. Oral corticosteroids have a small benefit.
  • Physiotherapy and progressive exercise.
  • Epidural corticosteroid injections: short-term relief in some patients.

Surgery (microdiscectomy, decompression):

  • Urgent in cauda equina syndrome or with severe or progressive weakness.
  • In sciatica that does not improve after 6-12 weeks of treatment: surgery relieves pain faster, although after one or two years the results are similar to those of conservative treatment. The decision is shared with the patient.
  • In spinal stenosis with significant symptoms, decompression improves walking ability.

See neurosurgery.

Prevention

  • Regular exercise to strengthen the trunk muscles.
  • Keep a healthy weight and do not smoke (smoking speeds up disc degeneration).
  • Use correct lifting techniques and avoid sitting for long periods without a break.
