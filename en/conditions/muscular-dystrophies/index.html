Muscular dystrophies

Genetic diseases in which the muscles gradually weaken and degenerate. The best known is Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which affects boys. New therapies and multidisciplinary care.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What they are

The muscular dystrophies are a group of more than 30 genetic diseases that cause progressive degeneration of the muscles. They are due to mutations in genes that make proteins needed for the structure and function of muscle fibres. Without them, the fibres are damaged by contraction, die and are replaced by fat and fibrous tissue. It was Guillaume Duchenne who described the best-known form in the 19th century.

The most common

Dystrophy Inheritance Onset Features
Duchenne X-linked (boys) 2-5 years The most common and severe in childhood: 1 in 3,500-5,000 boys
Becker X-linked Childhood or youth Like Duchenne, but milder and slower
Steinert myotonic (type 1) Dominant Youth or adulthood The most common in adults: weakness, myotonia, cataracts, heart and hormonal problems
Facioscapulohumeral Dominant Adolescence Weakness of the face, shoulders and arms, often asymmetrical
Limb-girdle Recessive or dominant Variable Weakness of the hips and shoulders
Oculopharyngeal Dominant Over 40 Drooping eyelids and difficulty swallowing

Duchenne muscular dystrophy

It is caused by the lack of dystrophin, a protein that acts as a shock absorber for the muscle fibre. Boys start walking somewhat late, fall often, have difficulty running, climbing stairs and getting up from the floor (they push their hands against their legs, Gowers' manoeuvre), and their calves look large (pseudohypertrophy). Without treatment they lose the ability to walk at around 10-12. Later, breathing and the heart (cardiomyopathy) are affected. Thanks to current care, life expectancy has risen from adolescence to 30-40 years. Carrier mothers may have mild weakness or heart problems.

Myotonic dystrophy

As well as weakness, it causes myotonia: difficulty relaxing muscles after contracting them (for example, letting go after a handshake). It is a multisystem disease: early cataracts, heart arrhythmias that can be serious, diabetes, frontal baldness, sleepiness, digestive problems. It shows anticipation: it can be more severe in each generation, with a very severe congenital form.

Diagnosis

  • Examination and family history.
  • Creatine kinase (CK) in the blood, very high in Duchenne and Becker.
  • Genetic testing: confirms the diagnosis and allows genetic counselling for the family.
  • Electromyography, muscle MRI and sometimes a muscle biopsy.

Treatment

There is no cure, but care has greatly improved patients' lives:

  • Corticosteroids (prednisone, deflazacort, vamorolone) in Duchenne muscular dystrophy: they delay the loss of walking and complications.
  • Genetic therapies for Duchenne: exon-skipping medicines (eteplirsen, golodirsen, viltolarsen, casimersen, approved in the USA) for specific mutations, ataluren for nonsense mutations, the gene therapy delandistrogene moxeparvovec (approved in the USA in 2023-2024, with debated results) and givinostat, approved in 2024. See gene and RNA therapies.
  • Cardiac follow-up (heart-protective medicines, pacemakers or defibrillators) and respiratory follow-up (non-invasive ventilation, cough assist).
  • Physiotherapy, orthoses, wheelchairs and scoliosis surgery.
  • Psychological, educational and social support; associations such as ASEM in Spain or Muscular Dystrophy UK.
