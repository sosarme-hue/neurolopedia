Sleep apnoea

Pauses in breathing during sleep that fragment sleep, cause sleepiness and increase the risk of stroke, high blood pressure and cognitive decline. It is treated with CPAP.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

What it is

Obstructive sleep apnoea is repeated obstruction of the upper airway during sleep. The throat muscles relax, the airway closes partly or completely, breathing stops for 10 seconds or more and the brain briefly wakes up to reopen it, without the person remembering. It can happen dozens of times an hour.

It is very common and underdiagnosed: it affects about 10-15% of middle-aged adults moderately or severely, more often men and women after the menopause. Almost a billion people worldwide are estimated to have it, and most do not know.

Symptoms

  • Loud snoring with pauses in breathing noticed by a partner, and waking with a choking sensation.
  • Excessive daytime sleepiness, tiredness, falling asleep watching television, reading or, dangerously, driving.
  • Unrefreshing sleep, headache on waking.
  • Needing to pass urine at night.
  • Problems with concentration, memory and irritability.
  • Reduced libido.

Risk factors

  • Obesity, especially neck circumference.
  • Age, male sex, menopause.
  • Alcohol and sedatives at night.
  • Anatomy: a small or receding jaw, large tonsils (especially in children), nasal obstruction.
  • Smoking, hypothyroidism.

Why it matters for the brain

Untreated apnoea:

  • Roughly doubles the risk of stroke and is common after one.
  • Causes or worsens resistant high blood pressure and atrial fibrillation.
  • Is linked to cognitive decline and poorer diabetes control.
  • Multiplies the risk of road traffic and work accidents.
  • Worsens depression and insomnia.

Diagnosis

  • Questionnaires such as STOP-Bang and the Epworth sleepiness scale.
  • Respiratory polygraphy at home or polysomnography in a sleep unit, which measure the apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) per hour:
AHI (events per hour) Severity
5-14 Mild
15-29 Moderate
30 or more Severe

Treatment

  • CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure): a mask connected to a machine that keeps the airway open with pressurised air. It is the most effective treatment for moderate and severe apnoea: it improves sleepiness, blood pressure and quality of life. It takes a few weeks to get used to, but most people adapt with help.
  • Weight loss: can greatly reduce or eliminate apnoea. Tirzepatide was approved in 2024 for sleep apnoea in people with obesity.
  • Mandibular advancement devices (custom-made splints), for mild or moderate apnoea or if CPAP is not tolerated.
  • Positional therapy if apnoeas occur only when lying on the back.
  • Avoid alcohol and sedatives at night, and stop smoking.
  • Surgery: tonsillectomy in children, nasal or maxillofacial surgery in selected cases, and hypoglossal nerve stimulation (an implant that activates the tongue) in patients who cannot tolerate CPAP.
