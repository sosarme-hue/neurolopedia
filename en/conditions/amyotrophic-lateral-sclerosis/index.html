Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

A neurodegenerative disease in which the neurons that control the muscles die. It causes progressive weakness affecting the arms, legs, speech, swallowing and breathing. Multidisciplinary care and treatments.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as motor neurone disease, is a neurodegenerative disease that destroys the motor neurons, which carry movement commands from the brain (upper motor neuron) and from the spinal cord and brainstem (lower motor neuron) to the muscles. Without those commands, the muscles weaken and waste away. What is usually not affected is sensation, sight, hearing, eye control and, in most people, intelligence.

About 2 to 3 cases per 100,000 people a year are diagnosed, and in Spain about 4,000 to 4,500 people live with ALS, with around 900 new cases a year. It usually starts between 50 and 70 and is somewhat more common in men. It was described by Jean-Martin Charcot in 1869. In Spain, the 2024 ALS Law recognises the special care needs of these patients.

Symptoms

  • Limb onset (two thirds): weakness of one hand (dropping things, difficulty doing up buttons or turning a key) or one foot (tripping, foot drop), which gradually spreads.
  • Bulbar onset (one third): difficulty speaking (slurred or nasal speech) and swallowing, with choking.
  • Fasciculations: small visible twitches under the skin (although the vast majority of fasciculations in healthy people are benign).
  • Cramps, stiffness and spasticity.
  • Emotional lability: laughing or crying that is hard to control.
  • Respiratory failure due to weakness of the diaphragm, which is the main cause of death.
  • Between 30 and 50% have cognitive or behavioural changes, and about 15% develop frontotemporal dementia.

Average survival is 3 to 5 years from the onset of symptoms, but there is great variability: about 10% live more than 10 years (the physicist Stephen Hawking lived more than 50 years with a very slow form).

Causes

  • Sporadic (90%): unknown cause, from a combination of genetic and environmental factors.
  • Familial (10%): mutations in genes such as C9orf72 (the most common in Europe), SOD1, TARDBP or FUS. Mutations are also found in some patients with no family history.
  • In almost all cases the protein TDP-43 builds up in the motor neurons.

Diagnosis

There is no single test; it is based on examination and ruling out other diseases:

  • Electromyography: shows the loss of motor neurons in several regions.
  • MRI of the brain and spinal cord to rule out other causes, such as spinal cord compression.
  • Blood tests and, in some cases, neurofilaments in blood or cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Genetic testing, increasingly recommended, especially because there is a treatment for SOD1 ALS.

Treatment

  • Riluzole: prolongs survival by a few months.
  • Tofersen: an antisense oligonucleotide injected by lumbar puncture, approved in the USA (2023) and in Europe (2024) for ALS with a SOD1 mutation. It reduces neurofilaments and may slow the disease. See gene and RNA therapies.
  • Edaravone, approved in some countries but not in Europe, with debated benefit.

The most important thing is multidisciplinary care in ALS units, which prolongs survival and improves quality of life:

  • Neurology, respiratory medicine and night-time non-invasive ventilation with a mask, which prolongs life and improves sleep.
  • Nutrition: an adapted diet and, when swallowing is difficult or weight is lost, a gastrostomy (a feeding tube into the stomach).
  • Speech and language therapy and augmentative communication: tablets, eye-gaze systems, voice banking (recording one's own voice before losing it). Brain-computer interfaces already allow some patients with total paralysis to communicate.
  • Physiotherapy and occupational therapy, mobility aids, wheelchair.
  • Treatment of symptoms: cramps, spasticity, excess saliva, emotional lability, pain, anxiety.
  • Psychology, social work and palliative care, and advance planning of decisions (invasive ventilation, tracheostomy).
