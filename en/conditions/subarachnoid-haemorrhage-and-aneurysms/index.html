Subarachnoid haemorrhage and aneurysms

The "worst headache of your life", starting suddenly, may be due to a ruptured brain aneurysm. What an aneurysm is, when it is treated and what to do about a thunderclap headache.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A subarachnoid haemorrhage is bleeding into the space around the brain, between the meninges, where cerebrospinal fluid circulates. In about 85% of non-traumatic cases it is due to a ruptured aneurysm. It accounts for about 5% of strokes, but affects younger people (the average age is about 55) and is somewhat more common in women.

A brain aneurysm is a small sac-like bulge in the wall of an artery, almost always in the circle of Willis, at the base of the brain. Between 2 and 3% of adults have one, and the vast majority never rupture.

The key symptom: thunderclap headache

The typical symptom is an explosive headache that reaches its peak in less than a minute, and that many patients describe as "the worst headache of my life". It may come with:

  • Nausea and vomiting.
  • Neck stiffness, which may take a few hours to appear.
  • Sensitivity to light.
  • Loss of consciousness, brief or prolonged, or seizures.
  • Double vision or a drooping eyelid if the aneurysm presses on the third cranial nerve.

With a headache like this, call 112. Up to one in three patients with a mild haemorrhage is initially misdiagnosed, for example with migraine. Sometimes a small bleed (a "sentinel headache") precedes the major rupture by days or weeks. See when to worry about a headache.

Risk factors

  • Smoking and high blood pressure: the most important and modifiable.
  • Heavy alcohol use and cocaine.
  • Family history: two or more first-degree relatives with an aneurysm.
  • Inherited conditions such as adult polycystic kidney disease or some connective tissue disorders.
  • Female sex, and some populations, such as the Finnish or Japanese.

Diagnosis

  • Non-contrast CT scan of the head: within the first 6 hours it detects almost 100% of haemorrhages.
  • Lumbar puncture, if the CT is normal and more hours have passed, to look for blood or its breakdown product (xanthochromia).
  • CT angiography and cerebral angiography to locate the aneurysm and plan its treatment.

Treatment

It is an emergency treated in hospitals with neurosurgery and interventional neuroradiology:

  • Securing the aneurysm as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours, to prevent a new bleed, which is very serious. This is done endovascularly, filling it with platinum coils through a catheter, or with surgery, placing a clip across its neck. The choice depends on the shape and location of the aneurysm; the ISAT trial showed better results with the endovascular route when both are possible. See neurosurgery.
  • Oral nimodipine for 21 days, which reduces the risk of delayed ischaemia.
  • Control of blood pressure, pain, sodium and temperature in intensive care.

Complications

  • Rebleeding, especially in the first hours if the aneurysm has not been secured.
  • Hydrocephalus: blood blocks the circulation of cerebrospinal fluid; it is treated with a ventricular drain.
  • Vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischaemia: between days 3 and 14, the arteries can constrict and cause infarcts. It is monitored with transcranial Doppler and treated by maintaining adequate blood volume, raising blood pressure and, in selected cases, angioplasty.
  • Hyponatraemia (low sodium), seizures and heart problems.

Outlook

About a third of patients die, although mortality has fallen in recent decades. Many survivors regain their independence, but fatigue, memory and concentration problems, anxiety and depression are common and deserve attention and rehabilitation.

Unruptured aneurysms

More and more aneurysms are found by chance on an MRI. Most need no treatment, only follow-up and control of blood pressure and smoking. Treatment is considered depending on size (especially from 7 mm), location, shape, growth, age and history, with scores such as PHASES. Screening with MR angiography is recommended in people with two or more affected first-degree relatives and in polycystic kidney disease.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is my aneurysm at risk of rupturing? Does it need treatment?
  • How often should I have check-ups?
  • Should my relatives be screened?
  • Can I exercise, fly or become pregnant?
