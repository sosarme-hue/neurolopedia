Head injury and concussion

From a minor knock to a serious injury. What to watch for after a blow to the head, when to go to A&E, how to recover from concussion and when to return to sport.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A traumatic brain injury (TBI) is an injury to the brain caused by a blow to the head, a sudden acceleration or deceleration (as in a road traffic accident) or an object that penetrates the skull. It is one of the main causes of death and disability in young people, and increasingly common in older people because of falls. Around 27 million new cases occur worldwide every year. In Spain the estimate is about 200 cases per 100,000 inhabitants a year.

It is classified using the Glasgow Coma Scale (from 3 to 15 points):

Severity Glasgow Frequency
Mild 13-15 About 80%
Moderate 9-12 10%
Severe 3-8 10%

Concussion

Concussion is a mild TBI: a functional, temporary disturbance of the brain after a blow, with no lesions visible on CT. You do not have to lose consciousness to have concussion: only one in ten cases involves this. It is very common in contact sports (football, rugby, hockey, boxing, martial arts), in cyclists and in falls.

Symptoms of concussion

  • Headache, a feeling of pressure.
  • Dizziness, unsteadiness, nausea.
  • Confusion, feeling "in a fog", slowness.
  • Amnesia for what happened just before or after the blow.
  • Blurred vision, sensitivity to light and noise.
  • Irritability, mood changes, drowsiness or difficulty sleeping.

Warning signs after a blow to the head

Call 112 (or your local emergency number) or go to A&E if there is:

  • Loss of consciousness, even if brief.
  • Increasing drowsiness or difficulty waking up.
  • A headache that is getting worse.
  • Repeated vomiting.
  • Seizures.
  • Confusion, agitation, strange behaviour, slurred speech.
  • Weakness, tingling or clumsiness on one side of the body, double vision.
  • Pupils of different sizes.
  • Clear fluid or blood coming from the nose or ears.
  • People over 65 or who take anticoagulants or antiplatelets, even if the blow seems minor: they may have a haematoma that appears hours or days later.

Injuries in moderate and severe TBI

  • Extradural haematoma: between the skull and the dura mater, often from a torn artery after a fracture. There may be a "lucid interval" in which the person seems well before suddenly deteriorating.
  • Acute or chronic subdural haematoma: the chronic form is typical in older people or those taking anticoagulants, and appears weeks after a minor blow with headache, confusion or weakness.
  • Brain contusions.
  • Diffuse axonal injury: stretching of nerve fibres by acceleration, which can cause coma with an almost normal CT scan.
  • Brain swelling and raised intracranial pressure.

Diagnosis

  • Neurological examination and the Glasgow scale.
  • CT head scan: done according to clinical rules (such as the Canadian rule), not after every minor blow.
  • Blood biomarkers (GFAP and UCH-L1), already approved in some countries, which help avoid unnecessary CT scans in mild TBI.
  • MRI in selected cases.

Treatment

Mild TBI and concussion:

  • Relative rest for 24-48 hours, then a gradual return to activity, school and work, without symptoms coming back. Prolonged complete rest does not help.
  • Graded return to sport: never return to play on the same day. A second blow before recovery can be serious (second impact syndrome).
  • Simple painkillers; avoid alcohol.

Moderate and severe TBI: treatment in intensive care and neurosurgery, with control of intracranial pressure, oxygenation and blood pressure; surgical removal of haematomas; sometimes a decompressive craniectomy. Then intensive, long-term neurorehabilitation.

After-effects

  • Persistent post-concussion symptoms: 10 to 20% continue to have headache, dizziness, fatigue, concentration problems or anxiety for months. They are treated with rehabilitation, graded exercise and psychological support.
  • In severe TBI: cognitive, behavioural and personality problems, epilepsy, spasticity.
  • Repeated blows (contact sports, boxing) are associated with chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a neurodegenerative disease caused by the build-up of the protein tau.
  • Moderate or severe TBI increases the risk of dementia, and is one of the 14 risk factors for dementia.

Prevention

Seat belts, helmets on bicycles, scooters and motorbikes, child car seats, preventing falls in older people, and rules that limit blows to the head in children's sport. See protecting your head.
