Medication overuse headache

Taking painkillers for headache on too many days a month can turn it into a daily headache. How to recognise it, how to withdraw the medicines and how to avoid it.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. What it is
  2. The limits
  3. How to recognise it
  4. Treatment
  5. Prevention
  6. Sources

What it is

Medication overuse headache is a headache that occurs on 15 or more days a month in a person who already had a headache disorder (almost always migraine or tension-type headache) and who takes painkillers or headache medicines too often for more than three months. It is one of the most common causes of chronic headache: it affects about 1-2% of the population, more often women.

It is a paradox: the medicines that relieve each attack, taken too often, sensitise the pain system and make headaches more frequent.

The limits

Medicine Overuse if taken on
Simple painkillers (paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, metamizole) 15 or more days a month
Triptans, ergots, opioids, combination painkillers (with caffeine or codeine) 10 or more days a month

What counts is the number of days on which something is taken, not the number of tablets.

How to recognise it

  • An almost daily headache, often present on waking.
  • Painkillers work less and less well.
  • Feeling the need to take the medicine "just in case" or at the first symptom.
  • Getting worse when trying to stop them.
  • It is often accompanied by anxiety, depression and poor sleep.

Keeping a headache diary of the days with pain and with medication helps to see it.

Treatment

  1. Information: understanding the problem is already the first step, and in many cases it is enough to cut down.
  2. Withdrawing the overused medicines. With simple painkillers and triptans this can be done abruptly; with opioids or barbiturates, gradually and sometimes in hospital. For the first two weeks the pain may get worse (rebound headache), with nausea, nervousness and poor sleep. Corticosteroids for a few days, or a different scheduled anti-inflammatory, can be used to get through this phase.
  3. Preventive treatment of migraine or tension-type headache from the start: topiramate, amitriptyline, propranolol, botulinum toxin or anti-CGRP antibodies, which have been shown to work even without first withdrawing the painkillers. See migraine treatments.
  4. A plan for attacks: what to take and the limit of days per month.
  5. Non-drug measures: regular sleep, exercise, stress management, cognitive behavioural therapy.

After withdrawal, about two thirds of patients go back to having episodic headache. The risk of relapse is high in the first year, so follow-up is important.

Prevention

  • Use painkillers only when needed and respect the day limits.
  • Seek advice if you need them on more than 8-10 days a month: you probably need preventive treatment.
  • Avoid combinations with codeine and other opioids for headache.
