Multiple sclerosis

An inflammatory disease in which the immune system damages the myelin of the brain and spinal cord. It is the most common cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults. Symptoms, diagnosis and the treatments that have changed its outlook.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an inflammatory, autoimmune and neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system. The immune system attacks myelin, the sheath that covers the nerve fibres of the brain, spinal cord and optic nerves, and leaves scars (plaques or "sclerosis") in multiple places. Axons and neurons are also damaged.

It is the most common cause of non-traumatic disability in young adults. It affects about 2.9 million people worldwide and about 55,000 in Spain. It usually starts between 20 and 40 and is three times more common in women. It was described by Jean-Martin Charcot in 1868.

Causes and risk factors

No single cause is known. A genetic predisposition (more than 200 variants, mainly in the HLA system) combines with environmental factors:

  • Epstein-Barr virus: in 2022 a large study showed that infection with this virus increases the risk of MS 32-fold and that virtually all patients have had it.
  • Vitamin D deficiency and little sun exposure: the disease is more common far from the equator.
  • Smoking.
  • Obesity in adolescence.

Symptoms

They depend on where the lesions are and vary greatly from person to person:

  • Optic neuritis: blurred vision in one eye with pain on moving it.
  • Sensory changes: tingling, numbness, a band-like feeling around the trunk, an electric shock down the back on bending the neck (Lhermitte's sign).
  • Weakness or clumsiness of the legs, spasticity.
  • Double vision, vertigo and unsteadiness.
  • Fatigue: very common and disabling.
  • Bladder, bowel and sexual problems.
  • Cognitive changes: slowness, problems with attention and memory.
  • Depression and anxiety.
  • Pain, including trigeminal neuralgia.
  • Symptoms can get worse with heat (Uhthoff's phenomenon).

Forms of the disease

  • Relapsing-remitting (85% at onset): relapses of symptoms lasting days or weeks, with full or partial recovery.
  • Secondary progressive: after years of relapses, disability increases slowly.
  • Primary progressive (10-15%): steady worsening from the start, without relapses.
  • It is now known that even in relapsing forms there is silent progression from the outset.

Diagnosis

It is based on the McDonald criteria (revised in 2024), which show lesions spread in space and time:

  • MRI of the brain and spinal cord, with contrast. The new criteria include the "central vein sign" and optic nerve lesions.
  • Lumbar puncture: oligoclonal bands and kappa free light chains in the cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Evoked potentials and optical coherence tomography of the retina.
  • Tests to rule out other diseases, such as neuromyelitis optica.

Treatment

We explain it in treatments for multiple sclerosis.

  • Relapses: high-dose corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) for 3-5 days; plasma exchange if they do not respond.
  • Disease-modifying treatments: more than 20 medicines that reduce relapses, new lesions and the progression of disability. They range from moderate-efficacy drugs (interferon beta, glatiramer acetate, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate) to high-efficacy ones (natalizumab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, ublituximab, alemtuzumab, cladribine, S1P modulators). The current trend is to start early with a high-efficacy treatment in many patients.
  • Ocrelizumab is the first medicine approved for the primary progressive form, and siponimod for active secondary progressive MS.
  • Symptom treatment: spasticity, fatigue, bladder, pain, depression. See treatment of spasticity.
  • Rehabilitation and exercise, which improve fatigue, strength and mood.

Living with multiple sclerosis

The outlook has improved greatly: with current treatments, many patients lead an active life for decades. Having children is possible: pregnancy reduces relapses, although they increase somewhat in the months after birth, and treatment needs to be planned. It is advisable to stop smoking, keep vitamin D at adequate levels and exercise.
