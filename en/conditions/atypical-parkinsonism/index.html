Atypical parkinsonism

Multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy and corticobasal degeneration. They look like Parkinson's, but progress faster and respond poorly to levodopa.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What they are

Atypical parkinsonisms are a group of neurodegenerative diseases that cause symptoms similar to those of Parkinson's disease (slowness, stiffness, walking problems), but are distinguished by:

  • A poorer response to levodopa.
  • Faster progression.
  • Early falls, swallowing and speech problems.
  • Additional symptoms that are not typical of Parkinson's.

They account for about 10-15% of parkinsonism. They are often mistaken for Parkinson's at first and the diagnosis becomes clear over time.

Multiple system atrophy

Alpha-synuclein builds up (as in Parkinson's), but in the glial cells. It affects 3-5 people per 100,000 and usually starts at around 55-60.

  • Early, severe autonomic failure: low blood pressure on standing with fainting, urinary incontinence or retention, erectile dysfunction.
  • Parkinsonism (MSA-P form) or cerebellar ataxia (MSA-C form).
  • Night-time stridor (a noise when breathing), REM sleep behaviour disorder, a shaky voice, the neck bent forwards.
  • Average survival is 6 to 10 years.

Progressive supranuclear palsy

The protein tau builds up. It is the most common atypical parkinsonism (5-7 per 100,000).

  • Falling backwards in the first year, without warning.
  • Difficulty moving the eyes vertically, especially downwards: going down stairs or looking at the plate is hard.
  • A fixed stare, a surprised or astonished expression.
  • Stiffness of the neck and trunk more than the limbs.
  • Slowed thinking, apathy, impulsiveness (the "applause sign").
  • Difficulty speaking and swallowing.

Corticobasal degeneration

Also caused by tau, and much rarer.

  • Very asymmetrical symptoms: one stiff, clumsy hand.
  • Apraxia: the person cannot use the hand for learned gestures (using a comb, waving goodbye) despite having strength.
  • "Alien hand": the hand makes movements that the person feels are not their own.
  • Myoclonus, dystonia, changes in cortical sensation and language.

Diagnosis

It is clinical, supported by:

  • MRI: atrophy of the midbrain ("hummingbird" or penguin sign) in progressive supranuclear palsy; atrophy of the pons and cerebellum ("hot cross bun sign") in multiple system atrophy.
  • DaTSCAN and PET.
  • Testing of the autonomic system.
  • New biomarkers: alpha-synuclein seed amplification, neurofilaments.

Treatment

No medicines slow these diseases. The aim is to relieve symptoms and maintain quality of life:

  • Try levodopa at adequate doses: some patients improve somewhat. See levodopa and Parkinson's medicines.
  • Orthostatic hypotension: compression stockings, more salt and water, raising the head of the bed, midodrine, fludrocortisone, droxidopa.
  • Botulinum toxin for dystonia or eyelid closure.
  • Physiotherapy, speech and language therapy and occupational therapy, fall prevention, swallowing assessment and nutrition.
  • Palliative care and support for the family.

Antibodies against tau and alpha-synuclein and other therapies are being trialled.
