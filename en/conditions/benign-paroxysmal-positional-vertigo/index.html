Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo

The most common cause of vertigo: seconds of spinning when lying down, turning over in bed or looking up. It usually clears in the clinic with a manoeuvre, although it can come back.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Dizziness and vertigo are not the same

"Dizziness" is a word used for very different sensations: unsteadiness, light-headedness, feeling about to faint, or vertigo. Vertigo is a specific sensation: the illusion that you or your surroundings are spinning or moving. It almost always arises in the inner ear (peripheral vertigo) and, less often, in the brain (central vertigo). See dizziness or vertigo?.

What BPPV is

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common cause of vertigo: it accounts for one in three cases and affects one in ten people over their lifetime, mostly after 50 and more often women. Its name describes it well:

  • Positional: it appears with certain head movements.
  • Paroxysmal: it is sudden and brief.
  • Benign: it is not due to any serious disease.

It happens when tiny calcium carbonate crystals, the otoliths or otoconia, come loose from the utricle, the part of the inner ear where they are anchored, and slip into one of the semicircular canals, almost always the posterior one. When you move your head, the crystals shift inside the canal, move the fluid and send the brain a false signal of rotation.

Causes and risk factors

  • In most cases there is no clear cause; it is linked to ageing of the inner ear.
  • Blows to the head, even minor ones.
  • Prolonged bed rest.
  • Previous vestibular neuritis, Ménière's disease, migraine.
  • Osteoporosis and vitamin D deficiency, which are linked to more recurrences.

Symptoms

  • Vertigo lasting seconds (usually less than a minute) when lying down, turning over in bed, getting up, bending down or looking up (for example, hanging out washing or reaching for a high shelf).
  • Nausea, sometimes vomiting.
  • A feeling of unsteadiness for hours or days after the attacks.
  • No hearing loss, headache or neurological symptoms.

It usually comes in spells of days or weeks, and recurs in half of patients in the following years.

Diagnosis

It is made in the clinic with the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre: the doctor quickly lays the patient back with the head turned and hanging slightly. If vertigo appears together with a characteristic eye movement (nystagmus), the diagnosis is confirmed and the affected ear and canal are identified. For the horizontal canal, the supine roll test is used. No blood tests or imaging are needed.

Treatment

BPPV is cured with a manoeuvre, not with pills:

  • The Epley manoeuvre for the posterior canal: a sequence of four head positions that guides the crystals back to the utricle. It resolves vertigo in a single session in most patients, and in two or three in almost all.
  • The Semont manoeuvre, an equally effective alternative.
  • The Lempert or barbecue roll and others for the horizontal canal.
  • Some patients learn to do the manoeuvre at home if the vertigo comes back.
  • Vestibular sedatives (such as dimenhydrinate or betahistine) do not cure it and may delay recovery.
  • If unsteadiness persists, vestibular rehabilitation helps.

See treatment of vertigo.

Other causes of vertigo

Cause What it is like Duration
Vestibular neuritis Severe continuous vertigo, with vomiting, no hearing loss Days
Ménière's disease Attacks with hearing loss, tinnitus and a blocked ear Hours
Vestibular migraine Attacks of vertigo in people with migraine, with or without headache Minutes to days
Stroke of the brainstem or cerebellum Sudden vertigo with severe unsteadiness, double vision, difficulty speaking or swallowing Persistent
Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness Daily unsteadiness, worse in visually busy environments Months

Warning signs

Call 112 if the vertigo is sudden and continuous and comes with inability to walk, double vision, difficulty speaking or swallowing, weakness, numbness or a severe headache: it could be a stroke. In A&E, an eye-movement examination called HINTS helps distinguish ear vertigo from a stroke, sometimes better than a CT scan.

Preventing falls

Vertigo and unsteadiness increase the risk of falls, especially in older people. During attacks, get up slowly, sit for a moment before standing, use a light at night and remove loose rugs.
