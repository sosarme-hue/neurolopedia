Dystonia

Involuntary muscle contractions that cause abnormal postures or twisting movements, such as spasmodic torticollis or writer's cramp. Botulinum toxin is the standard treatment.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Dystonia is a movement disorder in which muscles contract involuntarily and in a sustained way, producing abnormal postures, twisting or repetitive movements, sometimes with tremor. It is the third most common movement disorder, after Parkinson's and essential tremor, although it is underdiagnosed. It is caused by malfunction of the circuits of the basal ganglia, the cerebellum and the cortex that control movement.

Types by the area affected

  • Focal: a single part of the body. These are the most common in adults:
  • Cervical dystonia (spasmodic torticollis): the neck turns or tilts, often with pain. It is the most common.
  • Blepharospasm: involuntary closing of the eyelids, which can even prevent seeing.
  • Oromandibular dystonia: of the mouth, jaw or tongue.
  • Spasmodic dysphonia: of the vocal cords, with a broken or strangled voice.
  • Task-specific dystonias: only when doing a particular activity, such as writer's cramp or musician's dystonia.
  • Segmental: two neighbouring areas.
  • Generalised: the trunk and several limbs; it usually starts in childhood and has a genetic cause.

Sensory trick

Many patients discover that lightly touching their face, chin or the back of the neck reduces the contraction. This "sensory trick" (geste antagoniste) is very characteristic and helps with diagnosis.

Causes

  • Idiopathic, or of unknown cause: most focal dystonias in adults.
  • Genetic: more than 25 known genes, such as TOR1A (DYT1), which causes generalised dystonia starting in childhood, or dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa disease), which is important not to miss because it responds very well to treatment.
  • Acquired: brain lesions (stroke, cerebral palsy), medicines (antipsychotics, anti-sickness drugs such as metoclopramide), Wilson's disease.
  • It can be part of other diseases, such as Parkinson's or Huntington's disease.

Diagnosis

It is clinical, by a neurologist specialising in movement disorders. Depending on age and form, MRI, blood tests (copper and caeruloplasmin to rule out Wilson's disease in young people) and genetic tests are carried out. Levodopa is tried in every child or young person with dystonia.

Treatment

  • Botulinum toxin: the treatment of choice for focal dystonias (cervical, blepharospasm, dysphonia, writer's cramp). It is injected every 3 months into the overactive muscles and relaxes them. It works in most patients.
  • Oral medicines: anticholinergics such as trihexyphenidyl (useful especially in young people), baclofen, clonazepam, and levodopa in dopa-responsive dystonia.
  • Deep brain stimulation of the globus pallidus: very effective in genetic generalised dystonia (DYT1) and in severe resistant cervical dystonia. Improvement may take months.
  • Physiotherapy and sensorimotor retraining techniques, especially for task-specific dystonias.

Living with dystonia

Dystonia does not affect intelligence, but it can cause pain, isolation and anxiety because of the visible postures. Psychological support and patient associations, such as ALDE in Spain or the Dystonia Society in the UK, are a great help.
