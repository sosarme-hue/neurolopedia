Alzheimer's disease

The most common cause of dementia. How it begins, how it progresses, how it is diagnosed today with biomarkers, what treatments exist and how to care for someone who has it.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Alzheimer's disease is a neurodegenerative disease that gradually destroys neurons and their connections, starting with the areas involved in memory. It is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60 to 70% of cases. Around 57 million people live with dementia worldwide, and in Spain about 800,000 have Alzheimer's. Age is the main risk factor: it affects fewer than 2% of people aged 65 and more than 30% of those over 85.

It was described in 1906 by the German psychiatrist Alois Alzheimer, based on the case of Auguste Deter.

What happens in the brain

Two proteins build up abnormally:

  • Beta-amyloid, which forms plaques between neurons. It starts to be deposited 15 to 20 years before the first symptoms.
  • Tau, which forms tangles inside neurons and spreads through the brain along its connections, from the hippocampus towards the cortex. Its spread is more closely linked to symptoms.

Added to this are inflammation, loss of synapses and, finally, neuronal death and atrophy of the brain.

Symptoms and stages

The disease progresses slowly over 8 to 12 years on average, although this varies a great deal:

  1. Preclinical stage: there are changes in the brain, but no symptoms.
  2. Mild cognitive impairment: forgetting recent events, repeating questions, losing objects, but still independent.
  3. Mild dementia: forgetfulness that interferes with daily life, difficulty managing money, medication or planning, disorientation in unfamiliar places, word-finding problems, apathy.
  4. Moderate dementia: needs help dressing or washing, disorientation in time and in familiar places, does not recognise some people, behavioural changes (agitation, believing things have been stolen, wandering), sleep disturbances.
  5. Severe dementia: total dependence, loss of speech, difficulty walking and swallowing, incontinence.

In some people, especially when it starts before 65, the first symptoms are not memory problems but problems with language, vision (posterior cortical atrophy) or behaviour.

Risk factors

Non-modifiable:

  • Age.
  • Genetics: the APOE ε4 allele increases risk (about threefold with one copy and up to 10-12 times with two), but does not determine it. Fewer than 1% of cases are inherited through mutations in APP, PSEN1 or PSEN2, and these start before 65. People with Down syndrome have a very high risk.
  • Being a woman, partly because women live longer.

Modifiable: the 2024 Lancet Commission estimates that up to 45% of dementia cases could be delayed or prevented by acting on 14 factors, including high blood pressure, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, inactivity, diabetes, depression, social isolation and vision loss. See the 14 risk factors for dementia.

Diagnosis

  • A medical history taken with the person and a relative, and an examination.
  • Cognitive tests and neuropsychological assessment.
  • Blood tests to rule out treatable causes (vitamin B12, thyroid).
  • MRI, which shows atrophy of the hippocampus.
  • Biomarkers: amyloid and tau in the cerebrospinal fluid or on a PET scan and, since 2024-2025, blood tests (p-tau217) with an accuracy close to that of a lumbar puncture. They allow a biological diagnosis and are essential for the new treatments.

Treatment

  • Medicines for dementia: donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine in the mild and moderate stages, and memantine in the moderate and severe stages. They improve symptoms for a time.
  • Anti-amyloid antibodies: lecanemab and donanemab remove amyloid from the brain and slow progression by about 25-35% in very early stages. They require positive biomarkers, regular infusions and monitoring MRI scans because of the risk of swelling and microbleeds (ARIA), which are more common in people with two copies of APOE ε4.
  • Treatment of behavioural symptoms: first with non-drug measures (routines, activity, a calm environment) and, if needed, with low-dose medicines.
  • Cognitive stimulation, occupational therapy, physical exercise and social activity.

Caring for the carer

Most care is provided by families, mainly women. Caring for a person with dementia is demanding and can affect the carer's physical and mental health. It helps to:

  • Learn about the disease and its stages.
  • Ask for help: day centres, home help, respite care.
  • Apply for an assessment of dependency and disability.
  • Contact family associations, such as CEAFA in Spain or the Alzheimer's Society in the UK.
  • Plan legal decisions and advance directives early.

Common myths

  • "Losing your memory with age is normal and is Alzheimer's." Mild forgetfulness is normal; Alzheimer's is not part of normal ageing.
  • "Aluminium or vaccines cause Alzheimer's." There is no evidence for this.
  • "Nothing can be done." There are treatments for symptoms, help with daily life and, in early stages, medicines that slow the disease.
