Cervical artery dissection

A tear in the wall of the carotid or vertebral artery. It is an important cause of stroke in young people, sometimes after a sudden neck movement. Neck pain and headache are the warning.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A cervical artery dissection is a tear in the inner layer of one of the neck arteries that carry blood to the brain: the internal carotid or the vertebral artery. Blood seeps between the layers of the wall, forming a haematoma that narrows the artery and encourages clots to form, which can travel to the brain and cause a stroke or a TIA.

It is uncommon (2-3 cases per 100,000 a year), but it is one of the main causes of stroke in people under 50: it accounts for up to 20-25% of strokes in young people.

Causes

  • Spontaneous, in most cases, often in people with a degree of fragility of the artery wall.
  • Neck injuries, from accidents to sudden or forced movements: neck manipulation, sports, roller coasters, forceful coughing or vomiting, sustained postures (at the hairdresser's, painting ceilings).
  • Connective tissue diseases (vascular Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan) and fibromuscular dysplasia.
  • Recent infections, migraine and high blood pressure are associated with a higher risk.

Symptoms

  • Neck pain or pain at the back of the head (vertebral artery), or a headache in the forehead and around the eye, or pain on one side of the face and neck (carotid). It is usually the first symptom, hours or days before the stroke.
  • In the carotid: a drooping eyelid and small pupil on the same side (Horner's syndrome), pulsatile tinnitus, altered taste or tongue problems.
  • Symptoms of stroke or TIA: weakness, problems with speech or vision; in the vertebral artery, vertigo, unsteadiness, double vision or difficulty swallowing.

A new, severe neck pain or headache after exertion or a sudden movement, especially with any neurological symptom, should be checked in A&E.

Diagnosis

  • CT angiography or MR angiography of the neck and brain arteries; MRI with special sequences shows the haematoma in the wall.
  • Doppler ultrasound as an additional test.

Treatment

  • If there is an acute stroke, thrombolysis and thrombectomy as for other strokes. See stroke code.
  • Antiplatelets (aspirin) or anticoagulants for 3 to 6 months, to prevent clots. The CADISS and TREAT-CAD trials did not show that one is clearly better than the other, and in practice antiplatelets are used more often.
  • Avoid strenuous exertion and neck manipulation during recovery.
  • Rarely, a stent if the condition worsens despite treatment.

Outlook

Most dissections heal on their own over weeks or months, and the artery reopens in many cases. The risk of recurrence is low (about 1% a year), somewhat higher in people with connective tissue diseases. The outlook depends mainly on whether there has been a stroke and how severe it was.
