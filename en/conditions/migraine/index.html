Migraine

Much more than a headache: attacks of throbbing pain with nausea and sensitivity to light that can be disabling for hours or days. Triggers, aura, treating attacks and prevention.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Migraine is a neurological disease, not just a headache. It causes repeated attacks of moderate or severe headache lasting 4 to 72 hours, accompanied by nausea and sensitivity to light and noise. It affects about 15% of the population, three times more women than men, and is the second leading cause of years lived with disability in the world, the first in young women. In Spain about five million people have it, according to the Spanish Society of Neurology.

It has a strong hereditary component: if one parent has it, the children's risk is much higher.

Symptoms

A typical attack has several phases, although not everyone has all of them:

  1. Prodrome, hours or a day before: yawning, tiredness, mood changes, neck stiffness, craving for sweet food.
  2. Aura, in one in four patients: temporary neurological symptoms lasting 5 to 60 minutes that spread gradually. Visual disturbances are the most common (flashes, zigzag lines, a growing blind spot), but there can also be tingling that spreads from the hand to the face, or difficulty speaking.
  3. Pain: typically on one side of the head, throbbing, worse with physical activity and forcing the person to stop. It comes with nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia.
  4. Postdrome or "hangover": tiredness and difficulty concentrating for one or two days.

Episodic and chronic migraine

Chronic migraine means headache on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least eight of them. It affects about 2% of the population and has a large impact. One factor that favours it is painkiller overuse: taking them on more than 10-15 days a month can cause a medication-overuse headache, which resolves when they are withdrawn with medical help.

What causes it

Migraine is a disorder of brain excitability. The aura is due to a wave of electrical activity that slowly travels across the cortex (cortical spreading depression). The pain is produced by activation of the trigeminovascular system, which releases inflammatory substances such as CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) around the blood vessels of the meninges. The discovery of the role of CGRP has led to the newest treatments.

Common triggers

  • Changes in sleep (too little or too much) and skipping meals.
  • Stress and, often, the moment stress eases (the "weekend migraine").
  • Menstruation and hormonal changes.
  • Alcohol, especially red wine; dehydration.
  • Bright lights, strong smells, weather changes.

A headache diary helps identify them, but there is no need to become obsessed: many attacks have no clear trigger.

Diagnosis

It is based on the clinical history and a normal examination. No imaging is needed for typical migraine. Tests are requested when there are red flags:

  • A sudden, explosive headache (thunderclap headache).
  • A new headache after 50, or one that changes character.
  • Fever, neck stiffness, weight loss or a known serious illness.
  • Neurological symptoms that do not go away, seizures or confusion.
  • Pain that gets worse on coughing or lying down, or that progressively wakes you at night.

See when to worry about a headache.

Treatment

We explain it in detail in migraine treatments.

For an attack, take treatment early, at the start of the pain:

  • Anti-inflammatories (ibuprofen, naproxen, dexketoprofen) or paracetamol for mild or moderate attacks, together with an anti-sickness medicine if there is nausea.
  • Triptans (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, eletriptan…) for moderate or severe attacks.
  • Gepants (rimegepant) and lasmiditan for people who cannot take triptans.

Preventive treatment, if there are four or more migraine days a month or attacks are very disabling:

  • Classic options: propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, flunarizine, candesartan.
  • Botulinum toxin in chronic migraine.
  • Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, galcanezumab, fremanezumab, eptinezumab) and preventive gepants (atogepant), when the previous ones fail.

Non-drug measures

  • Regular sleep and meal times.
  • Regular aerobic exercise, which reduces the frequency of attacks.
  • Relaxation techniques, biofeedback and cognitive behavioural therapy.
  • Avoiding painkiller overuse.

Migraine in special situations

  • Women and hormones: many women have attacks with their period. In pregnancy migraine usually improves, especially in the second and third trimesters. In migraine with aura, oestrogen-containing contraceptives are not recommended, because they increase the risk of stroke, especially in smokers.
  • Children: attacks are shorter and often on both temples; sometimes they show up as recurrent tummy pain or vomiting.
  • Vestibular migraine: attacks of vertigo in people with migraine. See treatment of vertigo.

Common myths

  • "It's just a headache." Migraine is a neurological disease that can be very disabling.
  • "If I have a scan, they'll find the cause." In migraine, imaging is normal.
  • "Triptans are dangerous." They are safe in people without cardiovascular disease.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Do I need preventive treatment?
  • How many days a month can I take painkillers without risk of overuse?
  • What should I do if my attack treatment doesn't work?
  • Can I take hormonal contraceptives?
