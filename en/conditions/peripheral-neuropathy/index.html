Peripheral neuropathy

Damage to the nerves that run from the spinal cord to the body. It causes tingling, numbness, burning pain or weakness, often in the feet. Diabetes is the most common cause.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the peripheral nerves, which connect the brain and spinal cord with the skin, muscles and organs. When many nerves are affected at once it is called polyneuropathy. It is very common: it affects about 2-3% of the population and up to 8% of people over 55.

Symptoms

The most common form is "stocking and glove" polyneuropathy: it starts at the tips of the toes, slowly rises up the legs and, when it reaches the knees, appears in the hands.

  • Sensory: tingling, numbness, a feeling of wearing socks, burning or stabbing (neuropathic pain), pain at the touch of the bedsheets, loss of sensitivity to pain and temperature. They often get worse at night.
  • Unsteadiness, especially in the dark, due to loss of position sense.
  • Motor: weakness, cramps, wasting of the foot muscles, tripping.
  • Autonomic: dizziness on standing, digestive, urinary or sexual problems, abnormal sweating.

See tingling in the hands and feet.

Causes

Cause Comment
Diabetes The most common: affects half of people with diabetes over their lifetime. Prediabetes too
Alcohol Through direct toxicity and vitamin deficiency
Vitamin B12 deficiency Common in older people, vegans and people who take metformin or omeprazole for years
Medicines Chemotherapy (platinums, taxanes, vincristine, bortezomib), some antibiotics, amiodarone, excess vitamin B6
Kidney failure, hypothyroidism
Autoimmune Guillain-Barré, CIDP, vasculitis, Sjögren's, coeliac disease
Infections HIV, hepatitis C, leprosy (the most common cause in some countries), Lyme disease
Gammopathies and amyloidosis Including hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis
Inherited Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease: high-arched feet, hammer toes, slow weakness from youth
Idiopathic No cause is found in 20-30%

Diagnosis

  • History and examination: sensation, reflexes, strength.
  • Blood tests: glucose and glycated haemoglobin, vitamin B12, kidney and thyroid function, protein electrophoresis and immunofixation, and others depending on the case.
  • Electromyography and nerve conduction: confirms the neuropathy and whether it damages the myelin or the axon.
  • In selected cases: skin biopsy (small fibre neuropathy), genetic testing, nerve biopsy.

Treatment

  • Treat the cause: control blood glucose, stop alcohol, replace vitamin B12, adjust medicines, immunotherapy in autoimmune forms, tafamidis or gene silencers in transthyretin amyloidosis.
  • Neuropathic pain: pregabalin, gabapentin, duloxetine or amitriptyline; capsaicin or lidocaine patches. See treatment of neuropathic pain.
  • Physiotherapy and balance exercises to prevent falls.
  • Ankle-foot orthoses for foot drop.

Foot care

When sensation is lost, small wounds can go unnoticed and become infected, especially in people with diabetes:

  • Check your feet every day, with a mirror if needed.
  • Comfortable, seamless footwear, and do not walk barefoot.
  • Test the water temperature with your elbow.
  • Cut nails straight across and see a podiatrist.
