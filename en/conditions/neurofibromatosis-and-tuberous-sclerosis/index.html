Neurofibromatosis and tuberous sclerosis

Two genetic diseases that cause benign tumours in the skin, nerves and brain. Café-au-lait spots, neurofibromas, epilepsy and new targeted treatments.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What they are

Neurofibromatosis and tuberous sclerosis belong to the so-called phakomatoses or neurocutaneous syndromes: genetic diseases that affect the skin and the nervous system, and cause benign tumours in various organs. They are due to mutations in genes that normally restrain cell growth (tumour suppressor genes). They are inherited in an autosomal dominant way: each child of an affected person has a 50% chance of inheriting the condition, although in about half of cases the mutation appears for the first time in the patient.

Neurofibromatosis type 1

The most common: it affects 1 in every 3,000 people. It is caused by mutations in the NF1 gene.

  • Café-au-lait spots: six or more, present from childhood.
  • Freckles in the armpits and groin.
  • Neurofibromas: benign nerve tumours under the skin, which appear mainly from adolescence. Some, the plexiform ones, are large and can cause disfigurement and pain.
  • Lisch nodules in the iris.
  • Optic nerve glioma in young children.
  • Bone abnormalities, scoliosis.
  • Learning difficulties and ADHD in half of children.
  • A slightly higher risk of some malignant tumours (malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour, breast cancer in young women).

Treatment: annual multidisciplinary follow-up. MEK inhibitors (selumetinib, mirdametinib) shrink inoperable plexiform neurofibromas, a major advance of recent years.

Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2-related schwannomatosis)

Much rarer (1 in 25,000-30,000). It causes vestibular schwannomas on both sides (benign tumours of the hearing nerves), with hearing loss, tinnitus and unsteadiness, as well as meningiomas and other tumours. It is treated with surgery, radiosurgery and, in some cases, bevacizumab.

Tuberous sclerosis

It affects 1 in every 6,000-10,000 births. It is caused by mutations in TSC1 or TSC2, which overactivate the mTOR pathway.

  • Epilepsy in 80-90%, often from the first year, frequently as West syndrome. It is the most important feature.
  • Cortical tubers: lesions in the cerebral cortex that give rise to seizures.
  • Neurodevelopmental disorders: intellectual disability, autism, ADHD, grouped under the term TAND.
  • Hypopigmented patches ("ash leaf" spots), facial angiofibromas on the nose and cheeks.
  • Cardiac rhabdomyomas (sometimes seen on the pregnancy scan).
  • Kidney angiomyolipomas and lung involvement (lymphangioleiomyomatosis) in women.
  • Subependymal giant cell astrocytomas in the brain.

Treatment: early vigabatrin for spasms, other antiseizure medicines, cannabidiol, epilepsy surgery. mTOR inhibitors (everolimus, topical sirolimus) shrink brain and kidney tumours and skin lesions, and reduce seizures. Lifelong multidisciplinary follow-up.

Diagnosis

It is based on international clinical criteria and confirmed by genetic testing, which also allows genetic counselling and preimplantation or prenatal diagnosis.
