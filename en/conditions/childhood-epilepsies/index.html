Childhood epilepsies

Febrile seizures, absence epilepsy, rolandic epilepsy, West syndrome and Dravet syndrome. What they are like, how they are treated and what the outlook is.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Epilepsy in children

Epilepsy is especially common in childhood: it affects about 5-7 in every 1,000 children. Unlike in adults, many specific epilepsy syndromes are recognised in children, each with a characteristic age of onset, seizure types, electroencephalogram and outlook. Identifying them helps choose treatment and know what to expect. Many childhood epilepsies disappear with age.

Febrile seizures

These are not epilepsy. They are seizures that occur with fever in children aged 6 months to 5 years, without an infection of the nervous system. 2-5% of children have them.

  • Simple ones (generalised, lasting less than 15 minutes, only one in 24 hours) are benign: they do not damage the brain or affect development.
  • They recur in a third of children during another episode of fever.
  • Only 2-3% will develop epilepsy.
  • Fever-reducing medicines do not prevent febrile seizures, although they make the child more comfortable.
  • If a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, use the buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam prescribed by the paediatrician, and call 112 (or your local emergency number).

Benign or self-limited epilepsy syndromes

  • Self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (formerly "rolandic"): the most common at school age (4-10 years). Seizures at night or on waking, with a sensation on one side of the face, drooling, throaty sounds and difficulty speaking, sometimes generalised. It disappears in adolescence, and often needs no treatment.
  • Childhood absence epilepsy: between 4 and 10 years. Blank spells of a few seconds, with a fixed stare, many times a day, which are mistaken for daydreaming. They are triggered by breathing fast (hyperventilating). It responds very well to ethosuximide or valproate, and most cases remit.
  • Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy: in teenagers, with jerks of the arms on waking (dropping the breakfast cup), generalised seizures and sometimes absences. It gets worse with lack of sleep and alcohol. It is well controlled with medicines, but usually needs long-term treatment.

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies

These are severe epilepsies in which the seizures and epileptic activity affect the child's development.

  • West syndrome (infantile spasms): starts between 3 and 12 months, with spasms in clusters (sudden bending of the body, often on waking), developmental arrest or regression and a very abnormal electroencephalogram (hypsarrhythmia). It is an emergency: the sooner it is treated with vigabatrin, corticosteroids or ACTH, the better the outlook. Causes vary widely (brain lesions, tuberous sclerosis, genetic changes).
  • Dravet syndrome: starts in the first year with prolonged seizures with fever, due to mutations in the SCN1A gene. Some medicines (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin) must be avoided, as they make it worse. It is treated with valproate, clobazam, stiripentol, fenfluramine and cannabidiol. An RNA therapy to correct the cause is being researched.
  • Lennox-Gastaut syndrome: seizures of several types, especially sudden drop attacks, with intellectual disability. Valproate, lamotrigine, rufinamide, clobazam, cannabidiol, fenfluramine, the ketogenic diet and vagus nerve stimulation are used.

Diagnosis

  • The parents' account and a video of the episodes recorded on a mobile phone.
  • Electroencephalogram, often including sleep.
  • MRI depending on the type.
  • Genetic tests, increasingly important: they find the cause in a significant proportion of severe early-onset epilepsies.

Treatment and daily life

See antiseizure medicines. Most children with epilepsy can go to school, do sport (with precautions in water) and lead a normal life. It is worth informing the school and having a written plan of what to do in a seizure. See what to do during a seizure.
