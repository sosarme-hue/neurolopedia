Transient global amnesia

A sudden episode of memory loss lasting hours in which the person repeats the same questions. It is very frightening, but benign and almost never recurs.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Transient global amnesia is a sudden episode of loss of the ability to form new memories, lasting a few hours (on average 4 to 6, and always less than 24) with complete recovery. It mainly affects people aged 50 to 70, with about 5-10 cases per 100,000 a year, and it is one of the neurological situations that most alarms families, although it is benign.

What an episode is like

  • The person suddenly cannot remember what has just happened and asks the same questions over and over ("What are we doing here? What day is it?"), even when they are answered.
  • They may also have a memory gap for the preceding hours or days.
  • They know who they are, recognise their family and keep their language, attention and ability to do complex tasks, such as driving or cooking.
  • There are no other neurological symptoms: no weakness, no difficulty speaking, no loss of consciousness, no seizures.
  • They are usually bewildered or anxious.
  • When it ends, they can form memories again, but a permanent gap remains for the episode.

Triggers

There is often a trigger in the preceding hours:

  • Intense physical exertion.
  • Cold water (a swim in the sea or a pool).
  • Intense emotional stress, an argument, bad news.
  • Sexual intercourse.
  • Medical procedures, severe pain.
  • Valsalva manoeuvres (straining).

Cause

There is a temporary disturbance of the hippocampus, the key structure for forming memories (see memory and hippocampus and limbic system). On an MRI done at 24-72 hours, small dots of restricted diffusion may be seen in the hippocampus, which later disappear. The exact mechanism is unknown: changes in venous return, a vulnerability of these neurons to stress or migraine-like phenomena have been proposed, and it is more common in people with migraine.

Diagnosis

It is clinical, based on a witness's account. In A&E, other causes that can look similar are ruled out:

  • A stroke or a TIA (which usually cause other symptoms).
  • A temporal lobe seizure (shorter, repeated episodes, transient epileptic amnesia).
  • Poisoning, low blood sugar, head injuries.

An MRI may be done, and an electroencephalogram if episodes recur or are short.

Outlook

  • It needs no treatment: it is enough to stay with and reassure the person until it passes.
  • It does not increase the risk of stroke or dementia.
  • It recurs in only 5-15% of cases over the years.
  • Some studies have found mild memory difficulties in the following days, which resolve.
