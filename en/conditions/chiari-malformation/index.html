Chiari malformation

The lower part of the cerebellum drops through the opening at the base of the skull. It is often found by chance and needs no treatment; sometimes it causes headache on coughing or syringomyelia.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Chiari malformation is a condition in which the lower part of the cerebellum, the cerebellar tonsils, descends through the foramen magnum, the opening at the base of the skull through which the spinal cord passes. This can compress the brainstem and obstruct the flow of cerebrospinal fluid. It is named after the Austrian pathologist Hans Chiari, who described it in 1891.

Types

  • Chiari type 1: the most common. The tonsils descend 5 mm or more. It is often discovered in adulthood, frequently by chance on an MRI done for another reason: it is found in about 1% of MRI scans. Most of these people have no symptoms.
  • Chiari type 2: more severe, almost always associated with spina bifida (myelomeningocele) and hydrocephalus, and diagnosed during pregnancy or at birth.
  • Types 3 and 4: very rare and severe.

Symptoms of type 1

  • Headache at the back of the head that appears or worsens on coughing, sneezing, laughing, straining or bending, and is short-lived. It is the most characteristic symptom.
  • Neck pain, dizziness, unsteadiness.
  • Double vision, abnormal eye movements (nystagmus), ringing in the ears.
  • Difficulty swallowing, hoarseness, sleep apnoea.
  • Syringomyelia: a fluid-filled cavity inside the spinal cord, which appears in some patients. It causes loss of pain and temperature sensation across the shoulders and arms in a "cape" distribution (burns without noticing), weakness of the hands and scoliosis.

Diagnosis

  • MRI of the brain and the whole spine, which measures the descent of the tonsils and looks for syringomyelia and hydrocephalus.
  • Cerebrospinal fluid flow MRI in some cases.

It is important not to attribute to a mild Chiari symptoms that have another cause, such as migraine or tension-type headache, because that would lead to unnecessary surgery.

Treatment

  • Without symptoms: no treatment is needed, only follow-up if the descent is significant. Seek advice if new symptoms appear.
  • With typical symptoms or syringomyelia: posterior fossa decompression surgery: a small part of the occipital bone and often of the first vertebra is removed, and the dura mater is enlarged with a patch, to give the cerebellum room and restore the flow of fluid. It improves headache in most patients and halts syringomyelia. See neurosurgery.
  • If there is hydrocephalus, it is treated first.
