Spinal muscular atrophy

A genetic disease of the motor neurons of the spinal cord that causes weakness from childhood. It has gone from being the leading genetic cause of infant death to having three effective treatments, even more so if given before symptoms.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a genetic disease in which the motor neurons of the spinal cord are lost, causing progressive weakness and wasting of the muscles, especially those closest to the trunk (hips, shoulders) and the breathing muscles. It affects 1 in every 10,000 newborns and, until recently, was the leading genetic cause of death in childhood. Today it is one of the greatest successes of genetic medicine.

Cause

It is caused by the absence of the SMN1 gene, which makes the SMN protein, essential for the survival of motor neurons. Inheritance is autosomal recessive: the parents are healthy carriers (1 in 40-50 people) and each child has a 25% chance of having the disease. An almost identical "backup" gene, SMN2, makes a little functional protein; the more copies of SMN2 a person has, the milder the disease.

Types

Type Onset Best motor ability without treatment
0 Before birth Very severe, from birth
1 (Werdnig-Hoffmann) Before 6 months Never sit; without treatment, most died before age 2
2 6-18 months Sit, but do not walk
3 (Kugelberg-Welander) After 18 months Walk, although they may lose the ability
4 Adults Mild weakness

In type 1, babies have little strength ("floppy baby"), cannot hold up their head, breathe with their abdomen and have difficulty swallowing, but they are awake and alert.

Diagnosis

  • Genetic testing that shows the absence of SMN1 and counts the copies of SMN2.
  • Newborn screening with the heel-prick test: allows SMA to be diagnosed before symptoms and treated as early as possible. It is already done in many countries and in most Spanish regions.

Treatment

Since 2016 there have been three treatments that have changed the history of the disease:

  • Nusinersen: an antisense oligonucleotide injected by lumbar puncture every 4 months that makes SMN2 produce more functional protein.
  • Risdiplam: works in a similar way, but is taken by mouth daily; since 2025 also as tablets.
  • Onasemnogene abeparvovec: a gene therapy that delivers a healthy copy of SMN1 using a virus, in a single intravenous infusion, in young children.

Results are much better the earlier treatment starts: many children treated before having symptoms sit, walk and develop almost normally. See gene and RNA therapies.

As well as these medicines, multidisciplinary care remains essential: physiotherapy, respiratory support (non-invasive ventilation, cough assist), nutrition, orthopaedics (scoliosis) and support for families.
