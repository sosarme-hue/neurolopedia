Chronic insomnia

Difficulty sleeping at least three nights a week for three months, with tiredness during the day. The most effective treatment is not a pill but cognitive behavioural therapy.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Insomnia is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, despite having the opportunity to sleep, with consequences during the day: tiredness, irritability, problems with concentration or memory, sleepiness. It is considered chronic when it happens at least three nights a week for three months or more.

It is the most common sleep disorder: about a third of adults have symptoms of insomnia at some time, and 10-15% have chronic insomnia. It is more common in women, older people and those with chronic illnesses, pain, anxiety or depression. See sleep.

Why it becomes chronic

The "3 P" model explains chronic insomnia:

  1. Predisposing: a personal tendency to hyperarousal and worry, a family history.
  2. Precipitating: a stressful event, an illness, a shift change, a bereavement.
  3. Perpetuating: what keeps the problem going once the trigger has passed: spending a long time in bed trying to sleep, long naps, worrying about not sleeping, watching the clock, using the bed for other things. The brain learns to associate the bed with being awake and alert.

Causes and associated factors

  • Anxiety, stress and depression: closely linked to insomnia, in both directions. See stress and the brain.
  • Chronic pain, respiratory diseases, reflux, needing to pass urine at night.
  • Other sleep disorders: restless legs syndrome, sleep apnoea, circadian rhythm disorders.
  • Substances: caffeine, alcohol (helps you fall asleep but fragments sleep), nicotine, some medicines (corticosteroids, bronchodilators, some antidepressants).
  • Screens and light at night, irregular schedules, shift work.
  • Menopause, because of hot flushes.
  • Neurological diseases such as Parkinson's or dementia.

Consequences

Chronic insomnia does not just cause tiredness: it is linked to a higher risk of depression, anxiety, accidents, high blood pressure, diabetes and probably cognitive decline. It affects work performance and quality of life.

Diagnosis

It is clinical: a good sleep history, often with a two-week sleep diary (when you go to bed, how long it takes to fall asleep, awakenings, when you get up, naps, caffeine). Polysomnography is only done if another sleep disorder, such as apnoea, is suspected.

Treatment

The first-choice treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which is more effective in the long term than medicines. It includes:

  • Sleep restriction to consolidate sleep.
  • Stimulus control: the bed is only for sleep; if you do not fall asleep within about 20 minutes, get up and go back when you feel sleepy.
  • Restructuring beliefs about sleep.
  • Relaxation and sleep hygiene.

It can be done in a clinic, in a group or through digital programmes.

Medicines: only in the short term or in specific situations. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs (zolpidem) are recommended for a maximum of 2-4 weeks because of the risk of tolerance, dependence, falls and memory problems. Orexin antagonists (daridorexant) and prolonged-release melatonin in people over 55 are other options. See treatment of insomnia and narcolepsy.

Tips for sleeping better

  • Always get up at the same time, including at weekends.
  • Get natural light in the morning and exercise during the day.
  • Avoid caffeine after midday and alcohol in the evening.
  • Avoid naps longer than 20-30 minutes.
  • Cut down on screens and bright light in the hour before bed.
  • A cool, dark, quiet bedroom.
  • Do not look at the clock during the night.

See sleeping well.
