Peripheral facial palsy

Half of the face stops moving overnight. The most common form, Bell's palsy, usually recovers. How to tell it apart from a stroke and how to protect the eye.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Peripheral facial palsy is weakness or paralysis of one whole half of the face, due to damage to the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve. The most common form is Bell's palsy, of unknown cause, which affects about 20-30 people per 100,000 each year. It is named after the Scottish anatomist Charles Bell, who described the nerve in 1821.

Symptoms

They appear over hours, peaking at 2-3 days:

  • Drooping of one half of the face: the corner of the mouth droops, liquid escapes when drinking, it is hard to whistle or blow.
  • The eye on that side cannot close fully, and the forehead cannot be wrinkled.
  • A dry eye or, conversely, a watery eye.
  • Pain behind the ear, often the day before.
  • Altered taste at the front of the tongue.
  • Sensitivity to noise (hyperacusis).

Peripheral or central: the forehead is the key

In peripheral palsy, the whole half of the face is paralysed, including the forehead. In a stroke (central facial palsy), mainly the lower half of the face is affected and the forehead still moves, and there are usually other symptoms such as arm weakness or difficulty speaking. If in any doubt, go to A&E or call 112 (or your local emergency number).

Causes

  • Bell's palsy (idiopathic): most cases. It is thought to be due to reactivation of the herpes simplex virus, which inflames the nerve inside its narrow bony canal.
  • Ramsay Hunt syndrome: caused by the varicella-zoster virus; there are also blisters in the ear or mouth, earache and sometimes hearing loss and vertigo. It has a worse outlook.
  • Lyme disease, after a tick bite.
  • Ear infections, tumours of the parotid gland or ear, injuries, sarcoidosis, Guillain-Barré.
  • More common in pregnancy, diabetes and high blood pressure.

Diagnosis

It is clinical. The ear and parotid gland are examined and other neurological symptoms are ruled out. Tests (MRI, blood tests, electromyography) are only done if there are atypical features: both sides paralysed, slow progression over weeks, no improvement at three months, or recurrences.

Treatment

  • Corticosteroids (prednisone or prednisolone) for 10 days, starting within the first 72 hours: they increase the chance of full recovery. It is the only treatment with proven efficacy.
  • Antivirals (valaciclovir or aciclovir): doubtful benefit in Bell's palsy; they are added in severe forms and always in Ramsay Hunt syndrome.
  • Protecting the eye: essential to prevent corneal ulcers.
  • Artificial tears by day and ointment at night.
  • Cover the eye at night with a patch or suitable tape.
  • Sunglasses in the wind.
  • Facial exercises in front of a mirror and physiotherapy, in some cases.

Outlook

About 70-85% of patients recover completely, most within 3 weeks to 3 months. Complete palsies, people over 60 and Ramsay Hunt syndrome have a worse outlook. Some people are left with after-effects such as synkinesis (the eye closes when smiling) or "crocodile tears" when eating, which can be treated with physiotherapy and botulinum toxin. Reconstructive surgery exists for severe after-effects.
