Transverse myelitis

Inflammation of a segment of the spinal cord that causes weakness, loss of sensation and bladder and bowel problems over hours or days. Causes, diagnosis and urgent treatment.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

  3. Causes

What it is

Transverse myelitis is inflammation of the spinal cord affecting the whole thickness of one or more segments. It interrupts the pathways running up and down the cord, causing symptoms below the affected area. It is uncommon (1-8 cases per million a year) and can occur at any age.

Symptoms

They develop over hours or days (within 3 weeks at most):

  • Weakness of the legs (and arms if the lesion is in the neck), sometimes paralysis.
  • Sensory changes: tingling, numbness or loss of sensation below a level on the trunk, often with a feeling of a tight band.
  • Bladder and bowel problems: urinary retention or incontinence, constipation.
  • Back pain at the level of the lesion.

It is an emergency: with leg weakness that is progressing quickly together with problems passing urine, go to A&E, because it can also be due to spinal cord compression requiring immediate surgery.

Causes

  • Multiple sclerosis: usually causes partial myelitis, short and asymmetrical.
  • Neuromyelitis optica and MOGAD: extensive myelitis, spanning three or more vertebral segments.
  • Infections: viruses (herpes, varicella-zoster, enteroviruses, HIV), bacteria (syphilis, tuberculosis, Mycoplasma), or as a post-infectious reaction.
  • Autoimmune diseases: lupus, Sjögren's syndrome, sarcoidosis.
  • Paraneoplastic, associated with cancer.
  • Idiopathic: no cause is found in a third.

Non-inflammatory causes that look similar must also be ruled out: compression by a herniated disc or tumour, spinal cord infarction, dural arteriovenous fistula, or vitamin B12 or copper deficiency.

Diagnosis

  • Urgent MRI of the spinal cord (with contrast) and the brain.
  • Lumbar puncture: signs of inflammation, oligoclonal bands, infections.
  • Blood tests: anti-AQP4 and anti-MOG antibodies, autoimmunity, vitamin B12, copper, infections.

Treatment

  • High-dose intravenous corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) for 3-5 days.
  • Plasma exchange if there is no improvement or in severe cases.
  • Antivirals or antibiotics if there is an infection.
  • Treatment of the underlying disease to prevent relapses: multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica.
  • Early rehabilitation, bladder and bowel management, prevention of thrombosis and pressure sores, treatment of pain and spasticity.

Outlook

About a third of patients recover well, a third are left with moderate after-effects and a third with significant after-effects. Recovery starts within weeks and can continue for up to two years. Most idiopathic myelitis does not recur.
