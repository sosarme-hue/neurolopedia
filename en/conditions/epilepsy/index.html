Epilepsy

A tendency of the brain to produce seizures through abnormal electrical discharges of neurons. Types of seizure, causes, tests and treatment. Seven in ten people become seizure-free with medication.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Epilepsy is a brain disease that causes repeated seizures without an immediate trigger. A seizure is an excessive, synchronised electrical discharge of a group of neurons, which shows itself in many ways depending on the area of the brain affected. It affects about 50 million people worldwide and about 400,000 in Spain. It can start at any age, although it is more common in childhood and in older people.

Having one seizure does not mean having epilepsy: one in ten people will have a seizure in their lifetime, for example from a high fever in childhood, low blood sugar or alcohol. Epilepsy is diagnosed after two unprovoked seizures, or after just one if the risk of recurrence is high (for example, because of a lesion on MRI or an abnormal electroencephalogram).

Types of seizure

Focal seizures, which start in one area of the brain:

  • With preserved awareness: the person notices strange sensations (a smell, a rising feeling in the stomach, déjà vu, intense fear), jerking of one hand or visual disturbances. These used to be called "auras".
  • With impaired awareness: the person switches off, stares, and may make automatic movements (chewing, lip-smacking, fiddling with objects). Afterwards they do not remember it.
  • They can spread to the whole brain and become a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.

Generalised seizures, which involve both hemispheres from the start:

  • Tonic-clonic: loss of consciousness, stiffening and jerking of the whole body, sometimes with tongue-biting and incontinence, followed by confusion and sleepiness.
  • Absences: blank spells of a few seconds, typical in children, which can happen many times a day and be mistaken for daydreaming.
  • Myoclonic: brief jerks, like electric shocks, especially on waking.
  • Atonic: sudden loss of muscle tone with a fall to the ground.

Causes

  • Genetic: many epilepsies of childhood and adolescence.
  • Structural: the after-effects of a stroke (the most common cause in older people), head injuries, tumours, malformations of cortical development, hippocampal sclerosis.
  • Infectious: meningitis, encephalitis, neurocysticercosis (the main preventable cause in the world).
  • Metabolic and immune.
  • Unknown in a significant proportion of cases.

Diagnosis

  • A witness's account is essential: a video recorded on a mobile phone helps a great deal.
  • Electroencephalogram (EEG), sometimes after sleep deprivation or with video over several days.
  • MRI to look for a lesion.
  • Blood tests and, in some cases, genetic testing.

It must be distinguished from syncope (a faint), psychogenic non-epileptic seizures and other disorders.

Treatment

  • Antiseizure medicines: with the first or second well-chosen medicine, about 70% of patients become seizure-free. There are more than twenty, chosen according to the type of epilepsy, age, sex, other illnesses and possible side effects.
  • Epilepsy surgery: if seizures continue after two appropriate medicines (drug-resistant epilepsy, a third of patients), a specialised unit should assess whether the area where they start can be removed. In temporal lobe epilepsy, surgery leaves two in three patients seizure-free.
  • Neuromodulation: vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation or responsive neurostimulation.
  • Ketogenic diet, especially in children with severe epilepsies.
  • New medicines for specific syndromes, such as cannabidiol or fenfluramine in Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes.

Living with epilepsy

  • Take your medication every day and never stop it suddenly.
  • Get enough sleep and avoid excess alcohol: lack of sleep is one of the main triggers.
  • Driving: in Spain you can obtain or renew your licence after one year without seizures, with a favourable report from your neurologist. Rules vary between countries.
  • Sport: recommended, with precautions in water and at heights. Never swim alone.
  • Pregnancy: should be planned. Some medicines, especially valproic acid, increase the risk of malformations and neurodevelopmental problems and should not be used in women who could become pregnant unless there is no alternative. Folic acid is recommended.
  • Work and study: most people with epilepsy lead a normal life. Stigma remains one of the biggest problems.
  • SUDEP: sudden unexpected death in epilepsy is rare, but the risk rises with uncontrolled tonic-clonic seizures, especially at night. Controlling seizures is the best prevention.

Status epilepticus

A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without regaining consciousness in between, is an emergency: call 112. It is treated with benzodiazepines (buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam, which relatives can give if prescribed) and, in hospital, with intravenous medicines.
