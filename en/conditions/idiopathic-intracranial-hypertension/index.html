Idiopathic intracranial hypertension

Raised pressure inside the skull with no tumour or other visible cause, mainly in young women who are overweight. Headache and a risk to eyesight.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. What it is
  2. Symptoms
  3. Risk factors
  4. Diagnosis
  5. Treatment

What it is

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (formerly called "pseudotumour cerebri") is a rise in the pressure of the cerebrospinal fluid inside the skull with no visible cause on MRI, such as a tumour or hydrocephalus. It mainly affects women of childbearing age who are overweight or obese: in that group, up to 20 in every 100,000 a year. Its frequency is increasing in parallel with obesity.

The raised pressure compresses the optic nerves and can cause permanent loss of vision if untreated, so it is important to diagnose it in time.

Symptoms

  • Daily headache, often pressing, which can get worse when lying down, coughing or straining. It sometimes looks like migraine.
  • Visual obscurations lasting seconds, when standing up or bending down.
  • Pulsatile tinnitus: hearing your heartbeat in your ears ("like a heart inside my head").
  • Horizontal double vision.
  • Progressive loss of visual field, which the patient may not notice at first.
  • Neck or back pain.

Risk factors

  • Obesity and, above all, recent weight gain.
  • Some medicines: tetracyclines (minocycline, doxycycline), excess vitamin A and its derivatives (isotretinoin for acne), growth hormone, lithium, stopping corticosteroids.
  • Polycystic ovary syndrome, sleep apnoea.

Diagnosis

  • Fundoscopy: shows papilloedema (swelling of the optic nerve), the key sign.
  • Visual field testing and optical coherence tomography to measure optic nerve damage.
  • MRI with venography, to rule out tumours, hydrocephalus and cerebral venous thrombosis. It may show indirect signs such as an empty sella or narrowing of the venous sinuses.
  • Lumbar puncture: raised opening pressure (more than 25 cm of water in adults) with normal fluid.

Treatment

  • Weight loss: the treatment that modifies the disease. Losing 5-15% of body weight can lead to remission. Bariatric surgery, in selected cases, is very effective, and the new obesity medicines (GLP-1 agonists) are promising.
  • Acetazolamide: reduces production of cerebrospinal fluid. It can cause tingling, tiredness and a metallic taste. Topiramate is an alternative that also helps weight loss and prevents headache.
  • Stopping medicines that can cause it.
  • Treatment of headache, avoiding painkiller overuse.
  • Urgent surgery if vision is getting worse quickly: a stent in the narrowed venous sinus, optic nerve sheath fenestration or a cerebrospinal fluid shunt.

It requires joint follow-up by neurology and ophthalmology, with regular visual field checks.
