Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease

A very rapid dementia caused by prions, misfolded proteins that pass on their abnormal shape. Types, symptoms, diagnosis and surveillance.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is the most common prion disease in humans. Prions are misfolded forms of a normal brain protein, the prion protein, which can turn healthy proteins into abnormal ones in a chain reaction. They build up, destroy neurons and leave the brain looking spongy (spongiform encephalopathy).

It is a rare disease: 1-2 cases per million people per year worldwide, which means about 70-100 cases a year in Spain. It is always fatal, and progresses very quickly.

Types

  • Sporadic (85%): appears with no known cause, at around 60-70. The protein is thought to misfold by chance.
  • Genetic or familial (10-15%): due to mutations in the PRNP gene. This also includes fatal familial insomnia and Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome.
  • Acquired (less than 1%):
  • Iatrogenic: transmitted by dura mater or corneal transplants, growth hormone extracted from cadavers or contaminated neurosurgical instruments; now exceptional.
  • Variant CJD: linked to eating beef from cows with bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") in the 1980s and 1990s, mainly in the United Kingdom. It affected young people. Thanks to food chain control measures, it has practically disappeared.

Symptoms

  • Very rapid cognitive decline: over weeks or a few months, memory loss, confusion, behavioural changes.
  • Myoclonus: sudden muscle jerks, often triggered by a noise or touch.
  • Ataxia: unsteadiness and lack of coordination.
  • Visual disturbances, stiffness, abnormal movements.
  • It progresses to a state of mutism and immobility. Average survival is 4 to 6 months from the first symptoms.

Diagnosis

  • Diffusion MRI: typical changes in the cortex ("cortical ribboning") and the basal ganglia.
  • The RT-QuIC test in cerebrospinal fluid: detects prions with a specificity close to 100%. It has transformed diagnosis.
  • 14-3-3 and tau proteins in the cerebrospinal fluid (see lumbar puncture).
  • Electroencephalogram: characteristic periodic complexes.
  • Genetic testing of the PRNP gene.

It is important to rule out treatable diseases that can mimic it, such as autoimmune encephalitis.

Treatment

There is no treatment that slows the disease. Care focuses on relieving symptoms (clonazepam or levetiracetam for myoclonus) and providing palliative care and support for the family. Antisense oligonucleotides and other therapies that reduce the prion protein are being researched, especially for mutation carriers.

It is not spread by contact

CJD is not transmitted through everyday contact, kissing or living together. Special precautions are taken with surgical instruments and tissues. In Spain there is a national surveillance registry, coordinated by the Carlos III Health Institute, and it is a notifiable disease.
