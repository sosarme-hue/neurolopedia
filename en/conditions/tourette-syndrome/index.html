Tourette syndrome

Motor and vocal tics that start in childhood and usually improve in adolescence. What tics are, what they are associated with, when to treat them and how behavioural therapy helps.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Tourette syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by motor and vocal tics that start in childhood, change over time and last more than a year. Tics are sudden, rapid, repetitive, non-rhythmic movements or sounds. It is named after the French neurologist Georges Gilles de la Tourette, a pupil of Charcot, who described it in 1885.

Transient tics are very common: up to one in five children have them at some point. Tourette syndrome affects about 0.5-1% of school-age children, three or four times more often boys. It usually starts between 5 and 7, is most intense around 10-12 and improves greatly in adolescence: in adulthood, most people have mild tics or none.

What tics are like

Simple Complex
Motor Blinking, winking, wrinkling the nose, shrugging, jerking the head Touching objects, jumping, twirling, imitating other people's gestures
Vocal Throat clearing, coughing, sniffing, grunting, clicking the tongue Words or phrases out of context, repeating what others say (echolalia) or what you yourself say

Coprolalia, involuntarily saying obscene words, is the symptom best known from film and television, but only a minority have it (about 10-15%).

Characteristic features:

  • Before the tic there is a premonitory urge, like an itch or tension that is relieved by doing it.
  • Tics can be held back for a while, with effort, but then "rebound".
  • They fluctuate: they come and go, change type, get worse with stress, excitement, tiredness or boredom, and improve when concentrating on an activity (sport, music).

Associated conditions

Up to 85% of people with Tourette's have other problems, which often affect them more than the tics themselves:

  • ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder), in half.
  • Obsessive-compulsive disorder or obsessive traits.
  • Anxiety, depression, anger control problems, learning and sleep difficulties.

Cause

It is a disorder with a strong genetic component, involving many genes. It is linked to changes in the circuits connecting the cortex with the basal ganglia and to excess dopamine activity. It is not caused by poor upbringing or psychological problems.

Diagnosis

It is clinical: multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic, for more than a year, starting before 18, with no other cause. No tests are needed unless there are atypical features.

Treatment

Many children do not need treatment for their tics: it is enough to inform the family, the school and the child, and not to draw attention to the tics, because that increases tension and tics. Treatment is given when tics cause pain or social or school problems.

  • Behavioural therapy: the first-choice treatment. Comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics (CBIT), which includes habit reversal training, teaches people to recognise the premonitory urge and make a response incompatible with the tic. Exposure and response prevention is also used.
  • Medicines, if therapy is not enough: clonidine or guanfacine (also useful if there is ADHD), aripiprazole, tiapride or risperidone, and topiramate.
  • Botulinum toxin for isolated, troublesome motor tics.
  • Deep brain stimulation in adults with very severe, resistant tics.
  • Treating ADHD, OCD and anxiety, which often improves quality of life more than reducing the tics.

Living with Tourette's

The syndrome does not affect intelligence. With information, understanding at school and support, most people with Tourette's lead full lives. There are athletes, musicians and professionals in every field with the syndrome.
