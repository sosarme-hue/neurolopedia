Neuromyelitis optica

An autoimmune disease that mainly attacks the optic nerves and the spinal cord, with severe relapses. It differs from multiple sclerosis by an antibody, anti-aquaporin-4.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Neuromyelitis optica (NMO), or neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), is a rare and serious autoimmune disease of the central nervous system that mainly attacks the optic nerves and the spinal cord. For a long time it was considered a form of multiple sclerosis, but in 2004 it was discovered that most patients have antibodies against aquaporin-4 (AQP4), a protein found in astrocytes, and it is now known to be a separate disease with different treatment.

It affects between 1 and 10 people in every 100,000, far more often women (up to 9 in 10), with an average age at onset of about 40, and is more common in people of Asian and African descent.

Symptoms

The disease progresses in relapses, which are usually more severe than in multiple sclerosis and leave more lasting damage:

  • Optic neuritis: loss of vision in one or both eyes, often severe, with pain on moving the eye.
  • Longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis: inflammation of a long segment of the spinal cord, with weakness or paralysis of the legs (or all four limbs), loss of sensation and bladder and bowel problems.
  • Area postrema syndrome: intractable hiccups or vomiting for days, with no other cause.
  • Others: sleepiness, hormonal changes, brainstem symptoms.

MOG antibody-associated disease

Some patients without AQP4 antibodies have antibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG). MOGAD is now considered another distinct disease, more common in children, which also causes optic neuritis, myelitis and encephalomyelitis, generally with better recovery.

Diagnosis

  • Blood tests for anti-AQP4 and anti-MOG antibodies, using cell-based assays.
  • MRI: long spinal cord lesions (three or more vertebral segments) and optic nerve lesions.
  • Lumbar puncture: unlike MS, oligoclonal bands are usually negative.

Distinguishing it from multiple sclerosis is crucial, because some MS medicines (interferon beta, natalizumab, fingolimod) can worsen neuromyelitis optica.

Treatment

Of the relapse (as early as possible):

  • High-dose intravenous corticosteroids.
  • Early plasma exchange if there is no rapid improvement, or straight away in severe relapses.

Preventive, to avoid new relapses, which is essential because each relapse can leave permanent damage:

  • Traditional: rituximab, azathioprine or mycophenolate.
  • Medicines specifically approved for AQP4 antibody-positive NMOSD: eculizumab and ravulizumab (which block complement), inebilizumab (against B cells) and satralizumab (which blocks interleukin-6). They reduce relapses by more than 75-90%.

Rehabilitation and treatment of symptoms: spasticity, neuropathic pain, bladder.
