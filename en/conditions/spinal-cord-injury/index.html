Spinal cord injury

Damage to the spinal cord that cuts off communication between the brain and the body below the injury. Causes, levels, rehabilitation and research advances.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A spinal cord injury is damage to the spinal cord, the cord of nerve tissue that runs inside the spine and connects the brain with the rest of the body. It cuts off communication below the injury, causing paralysis, loss of sensation and disturbances of functions such as bladder, bowel, sexual function, blood pressure or breathing.

More than 15 million people worldwide live with a spinal cord injury. In Spain there are about 1,000 new cases a year, and some 50,000 people live with one.

Causes

  • Traumatic (just over half): road traffic accidents, falls (the main cause in older people), diving into shallow water (especially young people in summer), sports and work accidents, assaults.
  • Non-traumatic: tumours, herniated discs or osteoarthritis compressing the cord, infections, inflammatory myelitis (multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica), vascular problems, vitamin B12 deficiency.

Types

By the level of the injury:

  • Tetraplegia: a cervical injury; the arms, trunk and legs are affected. The highest (C1-C4) can affect breathing.
  • Paraplegia: a thoracic or lumbar injury; the trunk and legs are affected, with the arms preserved.

By its extent (ASIA scale):

  • Complete (ASIA A): no movement or sensation is preserved below the injury, including the sacral area.
  • Incomplete (ASIA B-D): some sensation or movement is preserved. The outlook for recovery is much better.

Symptoms and consequences

  • Paralysis and loss of sensation below the injury.
  • Neurogenic bladder and bowel: loss of control of urine and stools, requiring catheterisation and bowel programmes.
  • Sexual dysfunction and fertility problems.
  • Spasticity and spasms.
  • Neuropathic pain, very common.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia in injuries above T6: a sudden, dangerous rise in blood pressure, with headache and sweating, triggered by a stimulus below the injury (a full bladder, constipation, a wound). It is an emergency.
  • Low blood pressure when sitting up, problems regulating temperature.
  • Pressure ulcers, thrombosis, osteoporosis, urinary and respiratory infections.

Initial care

After an accident with a possible spinal injury, do not move the injured person unless there is immediate danger, and call 112 (or your local emergency number). The emergency services immobilise the spine. In hospital:

  • CT and MRI scans are done.
  • Good blood pressure and oxygenation are maintained to protect the cord.
  • Surgery to decompress the cord and stabilise the spine, ideally within the first 24 hours.

Rehabilitation

Neurorehabilitation in specialist units is essential. In Spain, the National Hospital for Paraplegics in Toledo has been a reference centre since 1974. It includes physiotherapy, occupational therapy, wheelchair training, learning to manage bladder and bowel, sexuality, psychological support and social and work reintegration. Many people with spinal cord injury work, drive adapted vehicles, play sport and have children.

Advances and research

  • Electrical stimulation of the spinal cord: in 2018 and 2023, teams in Switzerland and the USA enabled people with paraplegia to take steps again with a programmed epidural stimulator, and in 2023 it was connected to a brain implant (a "digital bridge") to control movement by thought. See brain-computer interfaces.
  • Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation, which in 2024 improved hand and arm function in cervical injuries.
  • Robotic exoskeletons.
  • Cell therapies and neuroprotective medicines, still under research.
