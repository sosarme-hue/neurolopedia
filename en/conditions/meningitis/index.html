Meningitis

Inflammation of the meninges, almost always due to an infection. Bacterial meningitis is an emergency that can kill within hours. Symptoms, vaccines and treatment.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, the membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord, almost always due to an infection. Viral meningitis is the most common and is usually mild. Bacterial meningitis is much less common, but it is an emergency: it can kill within hours, and about one in five survivors are left with after-effects. Each year it causes about 2.5 million cases and more than 200,000 deaths worldwide, according to WHO, which has a plan to defeat it by 2030.

Causes

Bacteria:

  • Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcus): causes outbreaks in teenagers and young adults, and sometimes overwhelming sepsis.
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus): the most common cause in adults.
  • Listeria: in newborns, people over 50, pregnant women and people with weakened immunity; it is transmitted through food.
  • Haemophilus influenzae type b, now rare thanks to the vaccine.
  • Group B streptococcus and E. coli in newborns.
  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis: meningitis that develops more slowly.

Viruses: enteroviruses (the most common cause, in summer and autumn), herpes, chickenpox, HIV, mumps virus.

Others: fungi (such as Cryptococcus in people with weakened immunity), some medicines, tumours or autoimmune diseases.

Symptoms

The classic triad is:

  • Fever.
  • Severe headache.
  • Neck stiffness: difficulty bringing the chin down to the chest.

Also: vomiting, sensitivity to light, confusion or drowsiness, and sometimes seizures.

Spots that do not fade

In meningococcal sepsis, red or purple spots appear on the skin (petechiae or purpura) that do not fade when pressed with a glass. This is a sign of the utmost urgency, although it does not always appear.

In babies

Symptoms are less clear: fever or low temperature, irritability or inconsolable crying, drowsiness, refusing feeds, vomiting, a bulging fontanelle, stiffness or floppiness.

If you suspect meningitis, call 112 (or your local emergency number) or go to A&E immediately.

Diagnosis

  • Lumbar puncture: the key test. It analyses the cerebrospinal fluid (cells, glucose, protein), with culture, staining and PCR to identify the germ.
  • Blood cultures and blood tests.
  • A CT head scan before the puncture only if there is reduced consciousness, neurological deficits, seizures or weakened immunity. It must never delay antibiotics.

Treatment

  • Bacterial meningitis: intravenous antibiotics within the first hour, before the diagnosis is confirmed, plus dexamethasone, which reduces deafness and mortality. See treatment of nervous system infections.
  • Viral meningitis: rest, painkillers, fluids; aciclovir if herpes is suspected.
  • Contacts: in meningococcal and Haemophilus meningitis, household and close contacts receive preventive antibiotics. Public health services identify them.

After-effects

After bacterial meningitis there may be deafness (which is why a hearing test is always done), problems with memory and learning, epilepsy, hydrocephalus, paralysis or amputations after meningococcal sepsis.

Prevention: vaccines

  • Meningococcal C and ACWY in childhood and adolescence, and meningococcal B, included in the Spanish vaccination schedule.
  • Pneumococcal: in childhood, in people over 60-65 and in risk groups.
  • Haemophilus influenzae type b: included in the babies' hexavalent vaccine.
  • Pregnant women: screening and antibiotics during labour if they carry group B streptococcus; avoid foods with a risk of Listeria (raw milk cheeses, uncooked cured meats, smoked fish).
