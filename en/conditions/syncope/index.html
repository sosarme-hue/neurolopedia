Syncope

A faint is a brief loss of consciousness because too little blood reaches the brain. It is almost always harmless, but it needs to be told apart from a seizure or a heart problem.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Syncope is a brief loss of consciousness caused by a temporary drop in blood flow to the whole brain, with spontaneous and complete recovery within seconds or a few minutes. It is what is commonly called a faint. Almost half of people will have at least one in their lifetime, and it accounts for about 1% of A&E visits.

Most are harmless, but some are due to heart disease and warn of a risk of sudden death. That is why every syncope deserves a medical assessment.

Types

Type Frequency What happens
Reflex or vasovagal The most common A reflex suddenly lowers pulse and blood pressure
Orthostatic hypotension Common in older people Blood pressure drops on standing up
Cardiac Less common, but the most serious Arrhythmias or structural heart disease

Vasovagal syncope

It is triggered by standing for a long time, heat, stuffy rooms, pain, the sight of blood or a needle, or strong emotions. Also by coughing, passing urine or swallowing (situational syncope). It usually gives warning: light-headedness, feeling hot, cold sweat, nausea, pallor, blurred or tunnel vision and ringing in the ears. It is very common in teenagers and young adults.

Orthostatic hypotension

Blood pressure drops on getting up, mainly because of medicines (for blood pressure, diuretics, for the prostate, antidepressants), dehydration, alcohol, or diseases that damage the autonomic nervous system, such as diabetes, Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies.

Cardiac syncope: the warning signs

  • Fainting during exercise or while lying down.
  • Palpitations just before.
  • No warning symptoms, with a sudden fall and injury.
  • Known heart disease or an abnormal electrocardiogram.
  • Relatives who died suddenly before 40.

In these cases, go to A&E.

Syncope or seizure?

During a faint, brief jerks of the arms and legs are common and can be mistaken for a seizure. Some differences help:

Syncope Epileptic seizure
Trigger Standing, heat, pain Often none
Before Light-headedness, sweating, pallor Aura (smell, rising stomach sensation)
Duration Seconds 1-2 minutes
Afterwards Quick recovery, tiredness Confusion for minutes
Tongue-biting Rare, at the tip Common, at the side

See epilepsy.

Diagnosis

  • A detailed clinical history and witnesses' accounts.
  • Blood pressure lying and standing.
  • An electrocardiogram for everyone.
  • Depending on the case: blood tests, echocardiogram, Holter monitor, an implantable loop recorder for infrequent syncope of uncertain origin, exercise test or tilt-table test.

Treatment

  • Vasovagal syncope: understand the mechanism and recognise the warning symptoms. When you notice them, lie down with your legs raised or, if that is not possible, sit down and use counter-pressure manoeuvres (crossing and squeezing the legs, clenching the fists or pulling on interlocked hands). Drink enough water and avoid triggers. In selected cases, medicines or a pacemaker.
  • Orthostatic hypotension: review medicines, get up slowly, drink water, increase salt if your doctor advises, use compression stockings and sometimes medicines such as fludrocortisone or midodrine.
  • Cardiac syncope: treat the cause (pacemaker, implantable defibrillator, ablation, surgery).

What to do if someone faints

  1. Lay them on their back and raise their legs.
  2. Loosen tight clothing and let in fresh air.
  3. If they do not regain consciousness within a minute, are not breathing normally or there are warning signs, call 112 and start resuscitation if they are not breathing.
  4. Do not give them anything to drink until they are fully awake, and do not let them get up suddenly.
