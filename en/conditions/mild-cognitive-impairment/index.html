Mild cognitive impairment

Lapses in memory or other abilities greater than expected for age, but that do not prevent an independent life. What it means, its causes and why it does not always progress to dementia.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an intermediate state between normal ageing and dementia. The person, or those around them, notice that memory or other mental abilities (language, attention, orientation or planning) have got worse than before, and tests confirm it, but they remain independent in daily life. They may need more time or effort, or rely on diaries and reminders, but they manage on their own.

It affects 10 to 20% of people over 65, and becomes more common with age.

Types

  • Amnestic: loss of memory for recent events predominates. This is the type that most often progresses to Alzheimer's disease.
  • Non-amnestic: mainly affects language, attention, speed of thinking or visuospatial ability. It can herald other dementias, such as vascular or Lewy body dementia.

Does it always lead to dementia?

No. Each year, 10 to 15% of people with MCI progress to dementia, compared with 1-2% of the general population of the same age. But many remain stable for years, and up to one in four improve or return to normal, especially when the impairment was due to a treatable cause. See is forgetfulness Alzheimer's?.

Causes

  • Neurodegenerative diseases in their early stages, especially Alzheimer's.
  • Cerebrovascular disease: small infarcts, damage from high blood pressure.
  • Treatable or reversible causes, which should always be looked for:
  • Depression and anxiety.
  • Medicines: benzodiazepines, antihistamines, drugs with anticholinergic effects, some painkillers.
  • Vitamin B12 deficiency and hypothyroidism.
  • Sleep apnoea and lack of sleep.
  • Uncorrected hearing and vision loss.
  • Alcohol use.
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus.

Diagnosis

  • An interview with the person and with a relative who knows them well.
  • Brief cognitive tests (such as the MoCA or the Mini-Mental) and, if needed, a full neuropsychological assessment.
  • Blood tests: vitamin B12, thyroid, glucose, kidney and liver function.
  • Brain MRI.
  • In specialist units, Alzheimer's biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluid, amyloid PET or, more recently, blood tests (such as p-tau217), which show whether the cause is Alzheimer's disease. This matters when deciding whether someone may be eligible for the new anti-amyloid antibodies.

Treatment

No medicine is approved for MCI in general. Dementia medicines such as donepezil have not been shown to prevent progression. What does work:

  • Treating reversible causes and reviewing medication.
  • Controlling vascular risk factors: blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking. See the 14 risk factors for dementia.
  • Regular physical exercise: the measure with the best evidence for improving cognitive function.
  • Cognitive stimulation and an active social life. See cognitive reserve.
  • Hearing aids if there is hearing loss.
  • A Mediterranean diet and good sleep.

The Finnish FINGER trial showed that a combined intervention (diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular monitoring) improves cognitive function in older people at risk.

In MCI due to Alzheimer's, with positive biomarkers, the antibodies lecanemab and donanemab can slow progression somewhat, with strict requirements and a risk of side effects.

Planning ahead

MCI is a good time to plan for the future: talk about care preferences, make an advance directive and organise financial and legal matters while the person can decide with full capacity. It is also worth reviewing driving and the handling of money and medication.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What could be causing my impairment? Is anything reversible?
  • Am I taking any medicine that could make my memory worse?
  • Should I have biomarker tests?
  • How often should I have check-ups?
