Wilson's disease

An inherited disease in which copper builds up in the liver and brain. It causes tremor, dystonia or psychiatric symptoms in young people and has an effective treatment.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Wilson's disease is a genetic disease in which the body cannot get rid of excess copper through the bile. Copper builds up first in the liver and then in the brain, especially in the basal ganglia, the eyes and other organs. It is caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene and is inherited in an autosomal recessive way. It affects 1 in 30,000 people. It was described in 1912 by the British neurologist Samuel Kinnier Wilson.

It is a very important disease to recognise: without treatment it is fatal, but with treatment most patients lead a normal life.

Symptoms

They usually appear between 5 and 35, although it can start later.

Liver (more common in children): raised liver enzymes, hepatitis, cirrhosis or acute liver failure.

Neurological (more common in young adults):

  • Tremor, sometimes a "wing-beating" tremor when the arms are held out.
  • Dystonia, with a characteristic forced smile.
  • Parkinsonism, clumsiness, difficulty speaking (dysarthria), drooling, difficulty swallowing.
  • Ataxia.

Psychiatric: personality changes, depression, irritability, falling school performance or psychosis. Sometimes these are the first symptoms and are mistaken for psychological problems of adolescence.

Kayser-Fleischer ring: a brownish-green ring around the cornea, caused by copper deposits, visible with a slit lamp. It is present in almost all patients with neurological symptoms.

Diagnosis

It should be considered in anyone under 40-50 with a movement disorder without a clear cause, or with unexplained liver disease.

  • Low blood caeruloplasmin.
  • Raised 24-hour urinary copper.
  • Slit lamp examination.
  • MRI: lesions in the basal ganglia and brainstem (the "face of the giant panda" in the midbrain).
  • Genetic testing of the ATP7B gene.
  • Sometimes a liver biopsy to measure copper.

Once diagnosed, siblings must be tested, as they have a 25% chance of having the disease.

Treatment

Treatment is lifelong and must never be stopped:

  • Copper chelators, which remove it in the urine: D-penicillamine and trientine. When starting, neurological symptoms can temporarily worsen, so treatment begins at low doses.
  • Zinc salts, which prevent copper absorption in the gut; used for maintenance and in patients without symptoms.
  • A low-copper diet at first: avoid shellfish, liver, nuts, chocolate and mushrooms.
  • Liver transplant in severe liver failure; it cures the disease.

With treatment, neurological symptoms improve slowly over months or years, and many disappear.
