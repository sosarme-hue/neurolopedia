Stroke

A blood vessel in the brain becomes blocked or bursts and part of the brain is left without blood. It is an emergency: every minute counts. Types, symptoms, the stroke code, treatments, rehabilitation, prevention and life after stroke.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

A stroke happens when blood stops reaching part of the brain, because an artery becomes blocked or because it bursts. It is also called a cerebrovascular accident (CVA), cerebral infarction or, in older usage, apoplexy. Neurons have no energy reserves: without oxygen and glucose they begin to die within minutes. In a large untreated stroke, an average of about 1.9 million neurons are lost every minute. Hence the motto "time is brain".

A stroke is a medical emergency as serious as a heart attack, and today it has very effective treatments if they are given early. That is why the stroke code exists, activated by calling 112 (or your local emergency number).

Stroke in numbers

  • About 11.9 million strokes occur worldwide every year, and some 94 million people are living after having had one (Global Burden of Disease study 2021).
  • It causes about 7.3 million deaths a year: it is the second leading cause of death in the world and one of the leading causes of disability.
  • One in four adults will have a stroke during their lifetime.
  • In Spain there are between 110,000 and 120,000 strokes a year, according to the Spanish Society of Neurology. It is the leading cause of acquired disability in adults and one of the leading causes of death, especially in women.
  • Around 10-15% of strokes occur in people under 50.
  • More than 80% of the burden of stroke is attributed to risk factors that can be changed, with high blood pressure at the top of the list.

Types of stroke

Type Frequency What happens
Ischaemic 80-85% A clot or plaque blocks an artery and an area of the brain loses its blood supply.
Intracerebral haemorrhage 10-15% An artery bursts and bleeds inside the brain. See intracerebral haemorrhage.
Subarachnoid haemorrhage About 5% Bleeding into the space around the brain, almost always from a ruptured aneurysm. See subarachnoid haemorrhage.

When symptoms are brief and recover completely, with no lesion on MRI, it is called a transient ischaemic attack (TIA). It is not a minor warning: it is an emergency, because the risk of a full stroke in the following days is high.

Causes of ischaemic stroke

Neurologists classify ischaemic stroke by its cause (the TOAST classification), because that determines how to prevent another one:

  • Large-artery atherosclerosis: a cholesterol plaque in a large artery, such as the carotid, ruptures and forms a clot.
  • Cardioembolic: the clot forms in the heart and travels to the brain. The most common cause is atrial fibrillation, an irregular heart rhythm that increases the risk of stroke fivefold and often causes no symptoms.
  • Lacunar: a very thin, deep artery becomes blocked, almost always because of long-standing high blood pressure. It produces small infarcts, less than 1.5 cm across.
  • Other determined cause: dissection (a tear) of the carotid or vertebral artery, blood disorders, vasculitis, or certain medicines and drugs such as cocaine. These are more common in young people.
  • Cryptogenic or undetermined: in one in four strokes no cause is found despite full investigation. In some cases a hidden atrial fibrillation is sought with a long-term heart monitor.

How to recognise it

Symptoms appear suddenly, within seconds or minutes, and almost always on one side of the body. Remember the FAST rule:

  • Face: ask the person to smile. Does the mouth droop to one side?
  • Arm: ask them to raise both arms with their eyes closed. Does one drift down or not move?
  • Speech: ask them to repeat a simple sentence. Is their speech strange or slurred, do they use meaningless words, or do they not understand you?
  • Time: if any of the three is abnormal, call 112 and note the time it started or when the person was last seen well.

Some services add two letters, BE-FAST: balance (unsteadiness, sudden severe dizziness, inability to walk) and eyes (sudden loss of vision in one eye or half of the visual field, or double vision).

Other possible symptoms are a sudden, explosive headache unlike any other (more typical of haemorrhage), loss of sensation or tingling down one side of the body, sudden confusion, or sudden difficulty swallowing.

Symptoms by the area affected

Area Typical symptoms
Left hemisphere Weakness of the right side and language impairment (aphasia)
Right hemisphere Weakness of the left side, "ignoring" the left half of space (neglect) and lack of awareness of the problem
Brainstem and cerebellum Severe vertigo, double vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, unsteadiness, weakness of all four limbs
Occipital lobe Loss of half of the visual field in both eyes

Call 112 even if the symptoms pass within a few minutes. Do not wait to see if it gets better, do not lie down "to see if it goes away" and do not drive yourself: the emergency team activates the stroke code and takes the patient to the right hospital. We explain it step by step in Stroke code.

What not to do

  • Do not give food or drink: swallowing may be affected and there is a risk of choking.
  • Do not give aspirin or other medicines: if it is a haemorrhage, it may make it worse.
  • Do not lower the blood pressure yourself: in the first hours, a somewhat high pressure helps carry blood to the brain.
  • Do not "wait and see" or call your GP first.

Risk factors

Modifiable (the INTERSTROKE study estimated that ten of them explain about 90% of the risk):

  • High blood pressure: the most important, present in more than half of strokes. See blood pressure and the brain.
  • Atrial fibrillation and other heart diseases.
  • Smoking: doubles the risk. After quitting, the risk falls considerably within a few years.
  • Diabetes, high LDL cholesterol, abdominal obesity and physical inactivity.
  • Excess alcohol and drugs, especially cocaine and amphetamines.
  • Poor diet, high in salt and ultra-processed foods and low in fruit and vegetables.
  • Psychological stress and untreated sleep apnoea.
  • Air pollution.

Non-modifiable: age (the risk doubles every decade after 55), family history, a previous stroke or TIA, and some genetic diseases such as CADASIL.

Stroke in women and young people

Women have more strokes over their lifetime than men, because they live longer, and their strokes tend to be more severe. They also have their own risks: pre-eclampsia and high blood pressure in pregnancy, migraine with aura combined with smoking and oestrogen-containing contraceptives, and early menopause.

In young people, unusual causes weigh more heavily: arterial dissection, sometimes after an injury or a sudden neck movement; patent foramen ovale (a small opening between the upper chambers of the heart, which in selected cases is closed with a device); drugs; and clotting disorders.

Diagnosis

In hospital, the neurologist assesses severity with the NIHSS scale (0 to 42 points), and the following are done immediately:

  • CT scan of the head: distinguishes an ischaemic stroke from a haemorrhage within minutes.
  • CT angiography: shows whether a large artery is blocked and could be reopened with thrombectomy.
  • CT perfusion or MRI: show how much brain is already lost and how much can be saved (the "penumbra"). They make it possible to treat patients outside the usual time window or who wake up with a stroke.
  • Blood glucose, blood tests and an electrocardiogram.

Then the cause is investigated: heart rhythm monitoring (sometimes for weeks with an implantable loop recorder), Doppler ultrasound of the neck arteries, an echocardiogram, and tests for cholesterol, diabetes and clotting.

Treatment in the first hours

  • Intravenous thrombolysis: a medicine (alteplase or tenecteplase) that dissolves the clot. It works within the first 4.5 hours, and in some patients selected with MRI or CT perfusion up to 9 hours or on waking with a stroke. The sooner the better: every 15 minutes gained improves the outlook.
  • Mechanical thrombectomy: a neuroradiologist passes a catheter from the groin or wrist and removes the clot from a large artery. It is effective up to 24 hours in selected patients. It is one of the most effective treatments in medicine: for every 3 patients treated, one is left with less disability.
  • Admission to a stroke unit, which reduces death and dependency.
  • In haemorrhage: rapid control of blood pressure, reversal of anticoagulants if taken and, in some cases, surgery.

We explain this in detail in thrombolysis and thrombectomy.

Complications

In the following days and months, the following may appear:

  • Aspiration pneumonia, if food passes into the lungs because of swallowing problems. That is why swallowing is assessed before eating.
  • Venous thrombosis in the legs from immobility.
  • Depression: affects one in three people after a stroke and is treatable. Anxiety, emotional lability (crying or laughing easily) and apathy are also common.
  • Fatigue, very common and often under-recognised.
  • Spasticity, stiffness of the muscles on the affected side, and a painful shoulder.
  • Seizures in about 5-10%, especially after large or haemorrhagic strokes.
  • Cognitive decline and vascular dementia: after a stroke the risk of dementia rises, and even more so if it recurs.
  • Falls and fractures.
  • Urinary incontinence, almost always temporary.

Rehabilitation and recovery

Neurorehabilitation begins in hospital as soon as the patient is stable. Recovery is fastest in the first three to six months, but can continue for years thanks to brain plasticity.

  • Physiotherapy: strength, balance and walking.
  • Occupational therapy: getting dressed, washing, cooking or using a computer again.
  • Speech and language therapy: language, speech and swallowing.
  • Neuropsychology: attention, memory and executive functions.
  • Psychological support for the person and their family.

About half of survivors are left with some lasting effect. Initial severity, age, speed of treatment and intensity of rehabilitation are the factors that matter most.

Preventing another stroke

After a first stroke, the risk of another is high, especially in the first weeks: about one in four strokes happens to someone who has already had one. Stroke prevention is based on:

Measure Usual target
Blood pressure Below 130/80 mmHg
LDL cholesterol Below 70 mg/dL, and in many cases below 55 mg/dL
Diabetes HbA1c around 7%
Smoking Stop completely
Exercise At least 150 minutes a week, adapted to any disability
Alcohol The less, the better
  • Antiplatelet drugs (aspirin or clopidogrel) in non-cardioembolic ischaemic stroke. After a minor stroke or high-risk TIA, the two are sometimes combined for 21 days.
  • Anticoagulants if there is atrial fibrillation, generally the direct-acting ones, started in the first days.
  • Statins to lower cholesterol.
  • Carotid surgery or stenting if the artery is severely narrowed and was the cause, ideally within the first two weeks.

Living after a stroke

  • Driving: traffic regulations require a period without driving and a favourable medical report. Ask your neurologist before getting behind the wheel again.
  • Work: many people return to work, sometimes with workplace adjustments or a gradual return.
  • Sex life: can be resumed as soon as the person feels well. Some medicines can affect it; mention it at your appointment.
  • Carers: caring for someone after a stroke is demanding. Patient associations offer information and support.
  • Disability and dependency assessment: if there are lasting effects, you can ask social services to assess them.

Common myths

  • "Stroke only happens to old people." False: one in ten occurs before 50, and children can have strokes too.
  • "If the symptoms go away, there's no need to go to A&E." False: a TIA warns of an imminent stroke and is an emergency.
  • "Nothing can be done." False: thrombolysis, thrombectomy and stroke units have changed the outlook.
  • "You can't improve after a few months." False: improvement is fastest at first, but rehabilitation remains useful afterwards.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What type of stroke did I have and what was the cause?
  • What blood pressure and cholesterol targets should I have, and how do I check them at home?
  • How long should I take each medicine and what do I do if I need an operation?
  • Can I drive? When can I go back to work?
  • Where and for how long will I have rehabilitation?
  • Which symptoms should make me go back to A&E?
