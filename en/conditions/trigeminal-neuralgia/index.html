Trigeminal neuralgia

Electric shocks lasting seconds on one side of the face when talking, eating or brushing your teeth. Why it happens and how it is treated, with medicines or surgery.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

What it is

Trigeminal neuralgia causes electric shocks of excruciating pain in part of the face, lasting from a fraction of a second to two minutes. It is due to irritation of the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve, which carries sensation from the face. It mainly affects people over 50 and is somewhat more common in women. It has been called "the suicide disease" because of the intensity of the pain, although today it has effective treatments.

Symptoms

  • Sudden, very intense pain, like an electric shock or a stab, almost always on one side.
  • Mainly in the cheek, jaw, teeth or gums (second and third branches of the nerve); less often in the forehead.
  • Triggered by trivial stimuli: talking, chewing, brushing your teeth, shaving, putting on make-up, a draught or touching certain points on the face ("trigger" zones).
  • Between shocks the person is usually pain-free, although some have a continuous background pain.
  • There may be periods of weeks or months with attacks and others without pain.

Many patients go to the dentist first, and healthy teeth are sometimes extracted.

Causes

  • Classical: in most people, an artery (or a vein) compresses the nerve where it leaves the brainstem and causes it to lose myelin at that point.
  • Secondary: multiple sclerosis (especially under 40 or if on both sides) and, rarely, a tumour.
  • Idiopathic: no visible cause.

Diagnosis

It is clinical. An MRI scan with special sequences is done to see whether a vessel is compressing the nerve and to rule out multiple sclerosis or a tumour.

Treatment with medicines

  • Carbamazepine: the treatment of choice, and it works in most people at first. It is started at a low dose and increased gradually. It can cause dizziness, drowsiness, low sodium and, rarely, serious skin reactions (more often in people of Asian origin with the HLA-B*1502 gene) or blood disorders, so blood tests are done.
  • Oxcarbazepine: just as effective and better tolerated in many cases.
  • As an add-on or alternative: lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin or pregabalin. See antiseizure medicines.
  • For a very severe attack in A&E, intravenous phenytoin or lacosamide, or anaesthetic blocks, can be used.

Surgery

If medicines do not work or are not tolerated:

  • Microvascular decompression: the neurosurgeon separates the vessel from the nerve with a small Teflon pad. It is the most effective and long-lasting technique: more than 70% of patients remain pain-free at 10 years. It requires an operation behind the ear under general anaesthesia.
  • Radiosurgery with the Gamma Knife: no incision, with an effect after weeks or months.
  • Percutaneous techniques (radiofrequency thermocoagulation, balloon compression, glycerol): simple and quick, useful in older people, but they usually leave some numbness and the pain can return.

See neurosurgery and treatment of neuropathic pain.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is there a vessel compressing the nerve on the MRI?
  • What blood tests do I need while taking carbamazepine?
  • Am I a candidate for surgery, and what are the risks of each technique?
