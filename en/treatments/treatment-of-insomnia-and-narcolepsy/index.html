Treatment of insomnia and narcolepsy

Cognitive behavioural therapy is the first treatment for chronic insomnia; sleeping pills only in the short term. In narcolepsy, medicines that keep you awake and control cataplexy.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Chronic insomnia: therapy first

Chronic insomnia (difficulty sleeping at least three nights a week for three months, with daytime consequences) affects about 10% of adults. All guidelines agree that the first treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which is more effective in the long term than medicines and has no side effects. It takes four to eight sessions, also available digitally or in groups, and includes:

  • Sleep restriction: spending in bed only the time you actually sleep, to consolidate sleep, and extending it gradually.
  • Stimulus control: using the bed only for sleep; if you do not fall asleep within about 20 minutes, getting up and going back when sleepy; always getting up at the same time.
  • Cognitive restructuring of the thoughts that keep insomnia going ("if I don't sleep eight hours, tomorrow will be a disaster").
  • Relaxation and good sleep habits. See sleeping well.

Medicines for insomnia

Group Examples Comments
Benzodiazepines Lorazepam, lormetazepam Only for 2 to 4 weeks; tolerance, dependence, falls and memory lapses
"Z-drugs" Zolpidem, zopiclone Similar; possible complex behaviours during sleep
Orexin antagonists Daridorexant Newer; less tolerance and dependence; can be used for longer
Prolonged-release melatonin Melatonin Modest effect; approved for people over 55
Sedating antidepressants Low-dose trazodone, mirtazapine, doxepin Often used off-label; limited evidence

Over-the-counter antihistamines (doxylamine, diphenhydramine) are not indicated for chronic insomnia and can cause confusion in older people. Stopping a benzodiazepine after years of use should be done gradually, with a plan agreed with the doctor.

Narcolepsy

Type 1 narcolepsy is caused by the loss of the neurons that make orexin (hypocretin). It causes intense daytime sleepiness, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotions), sleep paralysis and hallucinations when falling asleep. There is no cure, but it can be well controlled:

  • Short scheduled naps and regular hours.
  • Modafinil or armodafinil, which keep you awake.
  • Pitolisant, which acts on brain histamine and also reduces cataplexy.
  • Solriamfetol, which increases dopamine and noradrenaline.
  • Sodium oxybate, taken at night, which improves night-time sleep, sleepiness and cataplexy.
  • Antidepressants (venlafaxine, clomipramine) for cataplexy.
  • Under research: orexin receptor agonists, which replace the missing substance and have shown very promising results.

Narcolepsy affects driving: treatment and medical follow-up are essential to be allowed to drive.

Other sleep disorders

  • Sleep apnoea: CPAP, weight loss and, in selected cases, mandibular advancement devices or surgery.
  • Restless legs syndrome: correcting iron deficiency, gabapentinoids and, with caution, dopamine agonists.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder (moving and "acting out dreams"): melatonin or clonazepam, and making the bedroom safe. It can herald Parkinson's disease years in advance.
