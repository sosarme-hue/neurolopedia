Migraine treatments

From ibuprofen and triptans to anti-CGRP antibodies and gepants: how to treat an attack, when to start preventive treatment and what has changed in the last decade.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Two types of treatment

Migraine is treated with two complementary strategies:

  • Acute treatment (of the attack): to stop each attack.
  • Preventive treatment: so that there are fewer attacks and they are milder.

Treating the attack

The golden rule is to take treatment early, at the start of the pain, and at the right dose.

Step Medicines Comments
Mild or moderate attacks Anti-inflammatories (ibuprofen, naproxen, dexketoprofen, aspirin), paracetamol, metamizole Paracetamol is the least effective, but useful in pregnancy
Moderate or severe attacks Triptans: sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, eletriptan, almotriptan, naratriptan, frovatriptan As tablets, melts, nasal spray or injection; can be combined with an anti-inflammatory
If triptans cannot be used Gepants (rimegepant) and ditans (lasmiditan) No blood vessel-narrowing effect; useful in people with cardiovascular disease
Nausea and vomiting Metoclopramide, domperidone They also improve absorption

Triptans must not be used in people with coronary heart disease, stroke or poorly controlled high blood pressure. They can cause a feeling of pressure in the chest or neck, tingling and drowsiness.

Avoid opioids (codeine, tramadol) and frequent combinations with caffeine or ergots. And do not take painkillers on more than 10-15 days a month: medication overuse headache may develop.

Preventive treatment

It is considered if there are 4 or more migraine days a month, if attacks are very disabling, or if acute treatment does not work or is not tolerated. It is considered effective if it reduces migraine days by at least 50%, and you need to wait 2-3 months to judge it.

Traditional oral medicines:

  • Beta blockers: propranolol, metoprolol.
  • Topiramate (not in pregnancy; can cause tingling and word-finding problems), valproic acid (not in women of childbearing age).
  • Amitriptyline: useful if there is insomnia or associated tension-type headache.
  • Flunarizine: can cause drowsiness, weight gain and, in older people, parkinsonism.
  • Candesartan or lisinopril.

Specific treatments:

  • Botulinum toxin (OnabotulinumtoxinA): for chronic migraine, at 31 points on the head and neck every 12 weeks.
  • Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies: erenumab, galcanezumab, fremanezumab (monthly or quarterly injection under the skin) and eptinezumab (quarterly intravenous). They block CGRP or its receptor, the key molecule in migraine. They are very well tolerated and substantially reduce migraine days in many patients. In Spain they are funded when at least three preventive treatments have failed.
  • Preventive gepants: atogepant daily and rimegepant every other day.

Menstrual migraine and pregnancy

  • In menstrual migraine, "mini-prevention" can be used during the days at risk with a long-acting triptan (frovatriptan, naratriptan) or an anti-inflammatory.
  • In pregnancy, migraine often improves. Paracetamol is the painkiller of choice; triptans such as sumatriptan have reassuring data. Most preventive medicines are avoided.

Without medicines

  • Regular schedules for sleep and meals, good hydration.
  • Regular aerobic exercise (about 40 minutes three times a week is as effective as some preventive medicines).
  • Relaxation, biofeedback, cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness.
  • Non-invasive neuromodulation: vagus nerve, supraorbital nerve or transcranial magnetic stimulation, and remote electrical neuromodulation devices worn on the arm. See non-invasive neuromodulation.
  • A headache diary to identify triggers and assess treatments.
  • Supplements with some evidence: magnesium, riboflavin (vitamin B2), coenzyme Q10.
