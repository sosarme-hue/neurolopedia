Stroke prevention

How to avoid a first stroke and, above all, another one after the first. Blood pressure, cholesterol, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, carotid surgery and habits, with the targets set by the guidelines.

Up to nine in ten strokes can be prevented

The international INTERSTROKE study estimated that ten modifiable risk factors explain about 90% of strokes. That is why prevention works: in countries that have controlled blood pressure and smoking better, age-adjusted stroke mortality has fallen to less than half in thirty years.

Primary prevention means avoiding a first stroke, and secondary prevention means preventing another one after a stroke or a transient ischaemic attack. In the second case the risk of recurrence is high, especially in the first days and weeks, and treatment is more intensive.

The targets

Factor Usual target after a stroke How to achieve it
Blood pressure Below 130/80 mmHg Less salt, exercise, weight loss, and medicines (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics, calcium channel blockers)
LDL cholesterol Below 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L), and often below 55 (1.4 mmol/L) Statins, ezetimibe and, if that is not enough, PCSK9 inhibitors
Diabetes HbA1c around 7% Diet, exercise and medicines; some (GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors) also protect the cardiovascular system
Smoking Zero Professional support, nicotine replacement, varenicline or cytisine
Physical activity 150 minutes a week of moderate intensity Adapted to any after-effects
Alcohol The less, the better Avoid heavy drinking and binges
Weight Reduce abdominal fat Mediterranean diet and exercise

Blood pressure comes first

High blood pressure is the most important risk factor: it is present in more than half of strokes. Lowering systolic pressure by 10 mmHg reduces the risk of stroke by around 30-40%. It is worth measuring it at home with a validated monitor, seated, after five minutes' rest and on several days. See blood pressure and the brain.

Antiplatelets: aspirin and clopidogrel

In ischaemic stroke that is not caused by atrial fibrillation, an antiplatelet medicine is used to stop platelets forming new clots:

  • Low-dose aspirin.
  • Clopidogrel, an alternative that is equally effective or slightly better.
  • After a minor stroke or high-risk TIA, the two are combined for 21 days and then just one is continued (the CHANCE and POINT trials). Prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding with no extra benefit.

In primary prevention, aspirin is no longer recommended routinely for healthy people, because the risk of bleeding outweighs the benefit.

Anticoagulants in atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation multiplies the risk of stroke by five, and the strokes it causes are more severe. It often causes no symptoms: checking your pulse and having an electrocardiogram from the age of 65 helps detect it.

  • Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran) reduce the risk of stroke by about two thirds and cause fewer brain haemorrhages than the older vitamin K antagonists (warfarin, acenocoumarol).
  • After a stroke due to atrial fibrillation they can be started in the first few days if the stroke is mild or moderate (the ELAN trial).
  • For people who cannot take anticoagulants, the left atrial appendage can be closed with a device.

Statins

Statins (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin) lower LDL cholesterol and stabilise plaques in the arteries. After an ischaemic stroke caused by atherosclerosis, reaching an LDL below 70 mg/dL reduces new cardiovascular events (the TST trial). Muscle aches blamed on statins are less common than people think, and there is almost always an alternative.

The carotid artery

If a stroke or TIA is caused by a 70-99% narrowing of the carotid artery, endarterectomy (surgery that removes the plaque) greatly reduces the risk of recurrence, especially if it is done within the first two weeks. In selected cases a stent is placed. Narrowings that have not caused symptoms are treated mainly with intensive medication. They are assessed with Doppler ultrasound.

Other situations

  • Patent foramen ovale: in people under 60 with a stroke and no other cause, closing it with a device reduces recurrences.
  • Arterial dissection: treated with antiplatelets or anticoagulants for a few months.
  • Brain haemorrhage: the key is very good blood pressure control. Restarting antithrombotic medicines is decided case by case.
  • Sleep apnoea: treated with CPAP if significant.

Habits that add up

  • A Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil and nuts: in the PREDIMED study it reduced strokes by about a third. See diet.
  • Less salt: replacing it with a potassium-enriched salt substitute reduced strokes by 14% in the SSaSS trial.
  • Regular exercise, including brisk walking. See physical exercise.
  • Stopping smoking: the extra risk halves within a few years. See alcohol, tobacco and drugs.
  • Sleeping well and treating apnoea.

Adherence: taking the treatment

One in three patients stops at least one preventive medicine in the first year. If a medicine disagrees with you, discuss it with your doctor before stopping it: there are almost always alternatives. Weekly pill organisers, phone alarms and the polypill (several medicines in one tablet) all help.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What caused my stroke and which treatment prevents it?
  • What blood pressure and cholesterol figures should I aim for?
  • How long should I take the antiplatelet or anticoagulant?
  • What should I do if I need surgery or dental treatment?
