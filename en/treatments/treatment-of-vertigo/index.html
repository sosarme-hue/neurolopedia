Treatment of vertigo

Repositioning manoeuvres for positional vertigo, vestibular rehabilitation, medicines for only a few days, and treatment of Ménière's disease and vestibular migraine.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

First, find the cause

Vertigo is the false sensation that you or your surroundings are spinning. Treatment depends entirely on the cause, so the first step is a good diagnosis. Sudden vertigo with double vision, difficulty speaking or swallowing, weakness or inability to stand may be a stroke of the brainstem or cerebellum: it is an emergency and you should call 112 (or your local emergency number).

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo

This is the most common cause: tiny crystals in the inner ear (otoliths) move into a semicircular canal and cause seconds-long bouts of vertigo when lying down, turning over in bed or looking up. It is cured in the clinic, in most cases in one or two sessions, with repositioning manoeuvres (such as the Epley manoeuvre), which return the crystals to their place by moving the head through a sequence of positions. Medicines do not help and can delay recovery. It can come back over the years, and some people learn to do the manoeuvre themselves at home.

Vestibular neuritis

Inflammation, probably viral, of the balance nerve causes intense vertigo lasting days, with nausea and vomiting. It is treated with:

  • Vestibular sedatives (dimenhydrinate, prochlorperazine, benzodiazepines) for only 2-3 days, for the acute phase. Taking them for longer slows the brain's compensation.
  • Corticosteroids early on in some cases.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation as soon as possible: moving, walking and head and eye exercises.

Vestibular rehabilitation

This is a programme of exercises, guided by a physiotherapist, that trains the brain to compensate for damaged balance. It is useful in neuritis, after ear surgery, in loss of balance function on both sides and in chronic functional dizziness (persistent postural-perceptual dizziness), which is also treated with cognitive behavioural therapy and sometimes antidepressants.

Ménière's disease

Attacks of vertigo lasting hours with hearing loss, tinnitus and a feeling of fullness in one ear. Treatment aims to reduce attacks:

  • Cutting down on salt, caffeine and alcohol.
  • Betahistine, very widely used, although recent trials have not shown a clear benefit.
  • Diuretics in some cases.
  • Corticosteroid or gentamicin injections through the eardrum, and surgery in resistant cases.

Vestibular migraine

This is a common and under-recognised cause of recurrent vertigo in people with migraine. It is treated like migraine: avoiding triggers, sleeping well and, if attacks are frequent, preventive treatment with propranolol, flunarizine, amitriptyline or topiramate. See migraine treatments.
