Antiseizure medicines

More than twenty medicines that prevent seizures. How they are chosen, which side effects to watch for, why they must not be stopped suddenly and what happens in pregnancy.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

What they are for

Antiepileptic drugs, now usually called antiseizure medicines, prevent epileptic seizures by reducing the excitability of neurons. They do not cure the cause of epilepsy, but they control it: with a well-chosen first or second medicine, about 70% of patients become seizure-free. More than 25 are available. From phenobarbital (1912) and phenytoin (1938) to the medicines of recent years, each generation has been better tolerated and has had fewer interactions.

How they work

Mechanism Examples
Block the sodium channels of neurons Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, eslicarbazepine, lamotrigine, lacosamide, phenytoin
Act on the SV2A protein of synaptic vesicles Levetiracetam, brivaracetam
Strengthen the GABA brake Benzodiazepines (clobazam, clonazepam), phenobarbital, vigabatrin
Block calcium channels Ethosuximide (absences), gabapentin, pregabalin
Block glutamate AMPA receptors Perampanel
Several mechanisms Valproic acid, topiramate, zonisamide, cenobamate

The most widely used

  • Levetiracetam: widely used because it is easy to manage and has few interactions. It can cause irritability or mood changes.
  • Lamotrigine: well tolerated, the choice for many women of childbearing age. The dose must be increased very slowly to avoid serious skin reactions.
  • Lacosamide, oxcarbazepine, eslicarbazepine and carbamazepine: for focal seizures. Carbamazepine has many interactions.
  • Valproic acid: very effective in generalised epilepsies, but it should not be used in girls or in women who could become pregnant unless there is no alternative, because of the risk of malformations and developmental problems in the baby.
  • Ethosuximide: in childhood absence epilepsy.
  • Cenobamate: one of the most effective in drug-resistant focal epilepsy.
  • Clobazam, perampanel, topiramate, zonisamide, brivaracetam as add-ons.
  • For specific syndromes: vigabatrin (West syndrome, tuberous sclerosis), stiripentol, fenfluramine and cannabidiol (Dravet, Lennox-Gastaut). See childhood epilepsies.

How they are chosen

The neurologist takes into account:

  • The type of epilepsy: some medicines useful in focal epilepsies worsen certain generalised ones (carbamazepine can worsen absences and myoclonus).
  • Age, sex and plans for pregnancy.
  • Other conditions (depression, migraine, overweight, kidney, liver) and other medicines.
  • Possible side effects.

Treatment starts with a single medicine, at a low dose that is increased gradually. If it does not work, it is changed or another is added. If two appropriate medicines fail, this is called drug-resistant epilepsy, and surgery or other options should be assessed in an epilepsy unit.

Side effects

  • Common: drowsiness, dizziness, tiredness, unsteadiness, double vision, especially when starting or increasing the dose.
  • Mood and behaviour: irritability or depression (levetiracetam, perampanel, topiramate).
  • Skin: rashes, rarely serious (lamotrigine, carbamazepine, phenytoin). A rash with fever or involvement of the mouth or eyes needs urgent advice.
  • Weight: gain (valproic acid, pregabalin) or loss (topiramate, zonisamide).
  • Low sodium (oxcarbazepine, carbamazepine), memory and word-finding problems (topiramate), osteoporosis with long-term use of some.
  • Many interact with other medicines, including hormonal contraceptives, whose effectiveness they can reduce.

Pregnancy

Women with epilepsy can have healthy children, but pregnancy must be planned:

  • Review treatment before pregnancy, and never stop it on your own.
  • Lamotrigine and levetiracetam have the lowest known risk. Valproate carries the highest risk, followed by topiramate.
  • Take folic acid.
  • Monitor levels: some medicines fall during pregnancy.
  • Breastfeeding is recommended in most cases.

Tips

  • Take them every day, at the same time. Phone alarms and pill organisers help.
  • Do not stop them suddenly: this can trigger severe seizures.
  • If you miss a dose, check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist: in general, take it as soon as you remember unless the next dose is due soon.
  • Ask before taking other medicines, herbal remedies (such as St John's wort) or contraceptives.
  • After two or more years without seizures, gradual withdrawal can be considered, weighing up the risk of relapse. See can epilepsy be cured?.
