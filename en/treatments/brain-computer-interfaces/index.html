Brain-computer interfaces

Devices that read brain activity and turn it into commands for a computer, a robotic arm or a synthetic voice. Real advances in people with paralysis, and what is still a promise.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. What they are
  2. Types
  3. What has already been achieved
  4. Challenges and ethical questions
  5. Sources

What they are

A brain-computer interface (BCI) is a system that records brain activity and translates it into commands for an external device: a cursor, a keyboard, a synthetic voice, a robotic arm or the person's own muscles. It allows people who have lost that ability through ALS, spinal cord injury, a brainstem stroke (locked-in syndrome) or other diseases to communicate or move.

Types

Type How it records Pros and cons
Non-invasive Electroencephalogram with an electrode cap No surgery, but an imprecise, slow signal
Semi-invasive Electrodes on the surface of the brain (electrocorticography) or inside a blood vessel (stentrode) Better signal, minor surgery
Invasive Microelectrodes implanted in the cortex that record individual neurons The best signal; requires neurosurgery

What has already been achieved

  • Writing by thought: people with paralysis have written more than 90 characters a minute by imagining handwriting (2021).
  • Speaking again: in 2023, two US teams translated in real time the attempted speech of people with ALS and with a stroke into text and synthetic voice, at about 60-80 words a minute, with an avatar reproducing facial expression. In 2024 a patient with ALS regained communication with 97% accuracy, and in 2025 an almost instantaneous synthetic voice reproducing intonation was achieved.
  • Moving a robotic arm to drink or eat.
  • Walking again: in 2023, a "digital bridge" between a brain implant and a spinal cord stimulator allowed a man with paraplegia to walk by controlling his legs with his thoughts.
  • Communicating in complete locked-in syndrome, with no voluntary movement at all.
  • Since 2024, several companies (Neuralink, Synchron, Blackrock, Precision, Paradromics) have implanted their devices in clinical trials.

Challenges and ethical questions

  • The durability of implants and the need to recalibrate them.
  • Access: today they are only available in trials and are very expensive.
  • The privacy of brain data ("neurorights"): in 2021 Chile became the first country to protect brain activity in its Constitution, and Spain includes neurorights in its Charter of Digital Rights.
  • Identity, autonomy and responsibility for actions mediated by the machine.
  • Expectations: they are not "mind reading", but the decoding of specific intentions after training.
