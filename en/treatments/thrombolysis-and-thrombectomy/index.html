Thrombolysis and thrombectomy

The two treatments that can reverse an ischaemic stroke if given in time: a medicine that dissolves the clot and a catheter that removes it. Time windows, results and risks.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Unblocking the artery as soon as possible

In ischaemic stroke, a clot blocks an artery in the brain. Around the area that has already died (the infarct core) there is an area that receives little blood but can still be saved: the ischaemic penumbra. As the minutes pass, the penumbra turns into infarct. Reperfusion treatments aim to restore blood flow before that happens. There are two, which are often combined: thrombolysis and thrombectomy.

Both depend on a very fast chain of events, the stroke code, which starts with a call to 112 (or your local emergency number).

Intravenous thrombolysis

A medicine that dissolves the clot by activating plasminogen in the blood is given into a vein:

  • Alteplase (rt-PA), approved since 1995 after the NINDS trial: 10% of the dose as a bolus and the rest as a one-hour infusion.
  • Tenecteplase: given as a single injection, it is at least as effective and makes transfers easier. Since 2023-2024 it has been the first choice in many guidelines and hospitals.

When it can be given

  • Within the first 4.5 hours of symptom onset or of when the patient was last seen well.
  • Later, up to 9 hours, or in wake-up stroke, if an MRI or CT perfusion scan shows salvageable tissue (WAKE-UP and EXTEND trials).
  • A CT head scan to rule out haemorrhage, and a blood glucose check, are mandatory first.

When it cannot be given

Brain haemorrhage, recent major surgery or serious injury, active bleeding, very high blood pressure that does not come down, very low platelets, or treatment with anticoagulants at effective doses (although with direct oral anticoagulants it is sometimes possible after measuring their levels or reversing them).

Benefits and risks

  • It increases the chance of ending up with no disability or minimal after-effects: for every 10 patients treated within the first 3 hours, one more becomes independent.
  • The sooner, the better: the benefit is greatest in the first hour (the "golden hour") and disappears at around 4.5 hours.
  • Its main risk is symptomatic brain haemorrhage (about 2-6%), so the patient is closely monitored for 24 hours and blood pressure is strictly controlled.
  • Rarely, it causes swelling of the tongue and lips (angioedema).

Mechanical thrombectomy

When the clot blocks a large artery (the internal carotid, the middle cerebral or the basilar), thrombolysis alone opens the artery in fewer than half of cases. In thrombectomy, an interventional neuroradiologist:

  1. Inserts a catheter through the femoral artery (in the groin) or the radial artery (in the wrist).
  2. Guides it to the blocked brain artery using X-rays.
  3. Removes the clot with a stent retriever, a mesh that traps it, or by aspiration with a wide-bore catheter, or by combining both techniques.

It is done under sedation or general anaesthesia and takes about 30-60 minutes on average.

A revolution since 2015

In 2015, five large trials (MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME and REVASCAT, the latter from Spain) showed an enormous benefit: for every 3 patients treated, one more ends up with less disability, and for every 5, one more becomes independent. It is one of the most effective treatments in all of medicine.

Extended windows

  • Up to 24 hours in selected patients whose imaging shows salvageable tissue (DAWN and DEFUSE-3 trials, 2018).
  • In large infarcts, previously excluded (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, TENSION and other trials, 2023).
  • In basilar artery occlusion (ATTENTION and BAOCHE, 2022).
  • Its value in medium-sized arteries is being researched.

Risks

Brain haemorrhage, injury to the artery or emboli to other areas, and complications at the puncture site, all uncommon in expert hands.

Thrombolysis and thrombectomy together

If the patient is a candidate for both, thrombolysis must not delay thrombectomy: it is given while the transfer or the angiography suite is being prepared. When the hospital does not offer thrombectomy, the "drip and ship" model is used: thrombolysis followed by transfer to the reference centre.

After treatment

The patient is admitted to a stroke unit or intensive care. Blood pressure is controlled, imaging is repeated at 24 hours, and investigation of the cause, prevention and rehabilitation begin.

The great inequality

In Spain, the proportion of patients receiving these treatments has increased greatly, but there are still differences between regions because of the distance to hospitals offering thrombectomy. Telemedicine (telestroke), pre-hospital scales that identify large artery occlusion and transfer networks aim to reduce them.
