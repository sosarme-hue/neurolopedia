Deep brain stimulation

Electrodes implanted in the brain connected to a generator, like a brain pacemaker. It reduces the symptoms of advanced Parkinson's, essential tremor and dystonia.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

What it is

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) involves implanting fine electrodes in deep areas of the brain, connected to a generator similar to a pacemaker, placed under the skin of the chest. The generator sends continuous electrical pulses that modulate faulty brain circuits. It is reversible and adjustable: it can be programmed from outside and switched off if needed.

The modern technique was developed in 1987 in Grenoble by the neurosurgeon Alim-Louis Benabid and the neurologist Pierre Pollak. Since then more than 250,000 people have been treated worldwide.

What it is used for

Disease Usual target What improves
Parkinson's disease Subthalamic nucleus or internal globus pallidus Fluctuations, dyskinesias, tremor, stiffness; allows medication to be reduced
Essential tremor Thalamus (ventral intermediate nucleus) Hand tremor
Dystonia Internal globus pallidus Generalised dystonia, especially genetic (DYT1), and cervical dystonia
Drug-resistant epilepsy Anterior or centromedian thalamus Reduces seizures
Severe obsessive-compulsive disorder Internal capsule, nucleus accumbens Approved under humanitarian exemption in some countries
Severe Tourette syndrome Thalamus, globus pallidus Tics

It is being researched in treatment-resistant depression, chronic pain, Alzheimer's disease and addictions.

Who is a good candidate in Parkinson's

  • A definite diagnosis of Parkinson's disease, present for at least 4-5 years.
  • A good response to levodopa: the symptoms that improve with levodopa are the ones that improve with stimulation (except tremor, which improves even if it responds little).
  • Fluctuations or dyskinesias that are not well controlled with medication.
  • No dementia or severe psychiatric illness, and in good general health.

Symptoms that do not respond to levodopa, such as unsteadiness, resistant freezing of gait or cognitive problems, do not usually improve either.

How it is done

  1. Assessment in a specialist unit: neurology, neurosurgery, neuropsychology, psychiatry and imaging.
  2. Surgery: using a stereotactic frame or robotic systems, and guided by MRI, the electrodes are inserted through small holes in the skull. The patient is often awake so that neuronal activity can be recorded and the effect checked. It takes several hours.
  3. Generator: implanted under the collarbone, during the same operation or days later.
  4. Programming: the settings are adjusted over the following weeks. Current systems are directional and some are adaptive (approved in 2025): they record brain activity and adjust the stimulation by themselves.

What to expect

  • In Parkinson's, it improves "off" periods and dyskinesias, increases the time with good mobility and allows medication to be reduced by 30 to 50%. The EARLYSTIM study showed that it improves quality of life more than medication alone in patients with early complications.
  • The benefit on tremor is immediate.
  • In dystonia, improvement takes weeks or months.
  • It does not cure or slow the disease, but the effect lasts for many years.

Risks

  • Brain haemorrhage (1-2%), infection of the system (3-5%), hardware problems.
  • Stimulation side effects, which can usually be corrected by reprogramming: tingling, difficulty speaking, mood changes.
  • Batteries last 3 to 5 years (or more than 15 for rechargeable ones) and are replaced with a simple operation.
  • Precautions with some MRI scans, diathermy and security scanners.

An alternative without implants: focused ultrasound

MRI-guided focused ultrasound creates a small lesion in the thalamus or globus pallidus without opening the skull. It is an option for essential tremor and some cases of Parkinson's, especially on one side. See neurosurgery.
