Multiple sclerosis treatments

From the interferons of the 1990s to high-efficacy monoclonal antibodies: how disease-modifying medicines work, how they are chosen and what is new.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Three fronts

Treatment of multiple sclerosis has three aims:

  1. Treat relapses when they occur.
  2. Modify the course of the disease: reduce relapses, new lesions and the progression of disability.
  3. Relieve symptoms and maintain quality of life.

Thirty years ago there was no treatment. Today there are more than twenty approved medicines, and the outlook has changed radically.

Treating a relapse

  • High-dose methylprednisolone, intravenous or oral, for 3-5 days. It speeds up recovery, although it does not change the long-term course.
  • Plasma exchange if a severe relapse does not respond.
  • Not all mild relapses need treatment.
  • A pseudo-relapse must first be ruled out: a temporary worsening due to fever, infection (often urinary) or heat.

Disease-modifying treatments

Efficacy Medicines How given
Moderate Interferon beta, glatiramer acetate Subcutaneous or intramuscular injections
Teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate, diroximel fumarate Oral, daily
High S1P modulators: fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, ponesimod Oral, daily
Cladribine Oral, in two short courses over two years
Anti-CD20: ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, ublituximab Six-monthly infusion or monthly injection
Natalizumab Monthly infusion (or subcutaneous)
Alemtuzumab Infusion in two yearly courses

How the choice is made

  • Disease activity (relapses, MRI lesions), age, plans for pregnancy, other illnesses and the patient's preferences.
  • The current trend is to start early with a high-efficacy medicine in many patients, rather than escalating step by step, because observational studies show less long-term disability.
  • It is reviewed with regular MRI scans: if relapses or new lesions appear, treatment is changed.

Risks that are monitored

  • Infections, including progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a serious brain infection caused by the JC virus. Its risk is higher with natalizumab in people with antibodies against the virus, so these are monitored.
  • With anti-CD20 drugs, low immunoglobulins and a weaker response to some vaccines. It is best to get vaccinated before starting.
  • With fingolimod, a slow heartbeat when starting; with alemtuzumab, autoimmune diseases of the thyroid, platelets and kidney.
  • Regular blood tests depending on the medicine.

Progressive forms

  • Ocrelizumab: the first medicine approved for the primary progressive form, in young patients with inflammatory activity.
  • Siponimod: for active secondary progressive MS.
  • BTK inhibitors (tolebrutinib, fenebrutinib) are being researched; they act on microglia and "smouldering" inflammation inside the brain. In 2024-2025 tolebrutinib reduced progression in non-relapsing secondary progressive MS.

Pregnancy

Many women with multiple sclerosis have children. It needs to be planned: some medicines must be stopped in advance, others can be continued until conception or during pregnancy. Relapses decrease during pregnancy and increase somewhat in the three months after birth.

Treating symptoms

  • Spasticity: stretching, baclofen, tizanidine, cannabinoid oral spray, botulinum toxin. See treatment of spasticity.
  • Fatigue: exercise, pacing tasks, treating sleep problems and depression; amantadine or modafinil in some cases.
  • Walking: fampridine improves walking speed in some patients.
  • Bladder: anticholinergics, mirabegron, catheterisation, botulinum toxin into the bladder.
  • Pain, depression, cognitive and sexual problems.
  • Neurorehabilitation and exercise: improve strength, balance, fatigue and mood.

Habits that help

Stop smoking (smoking speeds up progression), keep vitamin D at normal levels, control weight and vascular risk factors, and sleep well.
