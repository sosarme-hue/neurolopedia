Treatment of neuropathic pain

Pain that comes from a damaged nerve does not respond to ordinary painkillers. First-line medicines, patches, options if they fail and non-drug measures.

What neuropathic pain is

Neuropathic pain occurs when the damage is in the nervous system itself, not in a muscle or a joint. It is described as burning, electric shocks, stabbing, painful tingling or pain when clothes brush the skin (allodynia). It affects between 7% and 10% of the population. Its most common causes are diabetic neuropathy, neuralgia after shingles, sciatica, trigeminal neuralgia, pain after a stroke or a spinal cord injury, and multiple sclerosis.

Common painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen barely work for this type of pain. It is treated with medicines that reduce nerve excitability. See also pain.

First line

Medicine Type Comments
Gabapentin Gabapentinoid The dose is increased gradually; causes drowsiness and dizziness
Pregabalin Gabapentinoid Faster effect; same side effects; risk of misuse
Duloxetine SNRI antidepressant Especially useful in diabetic neuropathy; nausea at first
Amitriptyline Tricyclic antidepressant Low dose at night; dry mouth, constipation, drowsiness; caution in older people and those with heart disease

None is clearly better than the others. The choice depends on age, other illnesses and side effects. You need to wait several weeks at an effective dose before deciding it does not work, and the realistic goal is to halve the pain, not to remove it completely. If one alone is not enough, they are combined.

Local treatments

When the pain is in a specific area (for example, after shingles):

  • 5% lidocaine plasters, with very few side effects.
  • 8% capsaicin patch, applied in hospital for 30-60 minutes, which brings relief for months.
  • Botulinum toxin injected into the skin, in selected cases. See botulinum toxin.

If they do not work

  • Tramadol and other opioids, as second or third line and for a limited time: their long-term benefit is doubtful and they carry a risk of dependence.
  • Carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine: the treatment of choice for trigeminal neuralgia, but not for other neuropathic pain.
  • Spinal cord stimulation and other neuromodulation techniques, in pain clinics.

Non-drug measures

  • Treat the cause: in diabetes, good blood sugar control slows neuropathy.
  • Tailored exercise and physiotherapy.
  • Psychological therapy (cognitive behavioural therapy or acceptance and commitment therapy), which reduces the impact of pain on daily life.
  • Sleeping well: lack of sleep amplifies pain.
  • Looking after your feet in neuropathy, because loss of sensation makes injuries more likely.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is causing my pain and can it be treated?
  • How long should I wait to know whether the medicine works?
  • Can I drive on this treatment?
  • What should I do if it makes me drowsy or dizzy?
