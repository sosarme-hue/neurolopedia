Stroke code

What the stroke code is, how it is activated by calling 112, what happens minute by minute from the call to thrombolysis or thrombectomy, and what you can do to save time.

What the stroke code is

The stroke code is an emergency protocol that coordinates ambulance services, hospitals and neurologists so that a person with symptoms of stroke receives the right treatment as quickly as possible. It works as a "fast track": once it is activated, every link in the chain knows a stroke patient is on the way and gets ready before they arrive.

It was created because the treatments that unblock the artery, thrombolysis and thrombectomy, only work if they are given in the first hours, and their benefit falls minute by minute. In a large untreated stroke about 1.9 million neurons are lost every minute. In Spain every region has had a stroke code since the National Health System Stroke Strategy (2008), and Europe works to the Stroke Action Plan for Europe 2018-2030, which asks for 90% of patients to be treated in stroke units.

The most important thing: if a face droops, an arm stops working or speech becomes strange all of a sudden, call 112 (or your local emergency number). Do not drive to A&E yourself or call your GP first.

Step by step: from the call to treatment

1. Recognise the stroke and call 112 (minute 0)

The chain starts with whoever is nearby. Use the FAST rule: face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty, time to call. The emergency service will tell you what to do, and it helps to have this information ready:

  • The exact time the symptoms started or, if the person woke up like this or nobody saw it happen, the last time they were seen well. This is the most important fact for deciding treatment.
  • What symptoms they have and whether they have improved.
  • What medicines they take, especially anticoagulants (warfarin, acenocoumarol, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban). If you can, take the list or the boxes.
  • Whether they had any disability before, and their main illnesses.
  • A contact phone number for a relative who can come to the hospital: the doctors may need information or consent.

While you wait for the ambulance: lay the person on their side if they vomit or are drowsy, do not give them food, drink or medicines, and open the front door.

2. Assessment by the ambulance crew (minutes 10-30)

The ambulance team confirms the suspicion with a simple scale, checks the blood sugar (low blood sugar can mimic a stroke), blood pressure and heart rhythm, and activates the stroke code: it alerts the hospital that a patient who may need treatment is on the way.

Many services use scales that estimate whether a large artery is blocked, such as the RACE scale developed in Catalonia. If the score is high, the patient can go straight to a hospital that performs thrombectomy, even if it is not the nearest one. This is the decision of "which hospital to go to", made at the scene in minutes.

3. Arrival at hospital ("door" minute 0)

The stroke team waits at the A&E door or in the CT room. The international target is a door-to-needle time under 60 minutes, and the best centres bring it below 30. Within a few minutes:

  1. The neurologist examines the patient using the NIHSS scale.
  2. A head CT scan is done to rule out a bleed and, almost always at the same time, a CT angiogram to see whether a large artery is blocked, and sometimes a CT perfusion scan.
  3. Blood is taken, although treatment is generally not delayed for the results, except in people taking anticoagulants.

4. Thrombolysis (if possible, within 4.5 hours)

If there is no bleeding and no contraindication, a medicine that dissolves the clot is given: tenecteplase as a single injection or alteplase as a one-hour infusion. In patients who wake up with a stroke or whose onset time is unknown, MRI or perfusion imaging allows some of them to be treated. See thrombolysis and thrombectomy.

5. Thrombectomy (if a large artery is blocked, up to 24 hours)

If the CT angiogram shows a large artery blocked, the patient goes to the interventional neuroradiology suite. Through a catheter inserted in the groin or wrist, the clot is removed with a stent retriever or by aspiration. It is done even if thrombolysis has been given, and can be performed up to 24 hours later in patients selected by imaging (the DAWN and DEFUSE-3 trials). Since 2023 we know it also benefits patients with large established infarcts.

When the first hospital cannot perform thrombectomy, the "drip and ship" model is used: thrombolysis is given and the patient is transferred to the referral centre. Some places do the opposite and bring the neuroradiologist to the patient.

6. Stroke unit (the first 48-72 hours)

Afterwards the patient is admitted to a stroke unit, where blood pressure, blood sugar, temperature, heart rhythm and the ability to swallow are monitored, the search for the cause begins and rehabilitation starts.

The times that are measured

Interval Quality target
Symptom onset → emergency call As soon as possible: this is the longest delay and depends on the public
Call → arrival at hospital Under 60 minutes
Door → CT Under 20-25 minutes
Door → thrombolysis (door-to-needle) Under 60 minutes, ideally under 30
Door → groin puncture (thrombectomy) Under 90 minutes

For every 15 minutes that thrombolysis is brought forward, more patients end up independent and fewer die. In thrombectomy, every hour of delay clearly reduces the chance of recovering without after-effects.

Telestroke

In district hospitals without a neurologist on call, telestroke allows a neurologist at a referral hospital to see the patient by video link, review the CT remotely and decide on thrombolysis. It has brought treatment closer to rural areas and reduced differences between regions.

Who can activate it

  • Anyone, by calling 112: this is the fastest way.
  • Primary care centres and the A&E departments of hospitals that receive the patient directly. Arriving on your own usually delays treatment, because nobody is warned in advance and it may not be the right hospital.
  • Hospitals themselves, when a patient admitted for another reason has a stroke ("in-hospital stroke code").

If the symptoms go away

A transient ischaemic attack is also an emergency. Even if the person feels well, they must go to hospital the same day: up to one in ten has a full stroke in the following days, and early investigation and treatment greatly reduce that risk.

Myths and mistakes that delay the stroke code

  • Waiting to see if it passes or for a relative to wake up.
  • Driving yourself to the nearest hospital instead of calling 112.
  • Thinking it is not serious because it does not hurt: an ischaemic stroke is almost never painful.
  • Calling your GP first or a relative who is a doctor.
  • Giving an aspirin: if it is a bleed, it can make it worse.

What to ask at the hospital

  • Was it possible to give thrombolysis or perform a thrombectomy? If not, why not?
  • What type of stroke was it and which artery was affected?
  • How long will they stay in the stroke unit?
  • When does rehabilitation start?
