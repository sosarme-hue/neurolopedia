Medicines for dementia

Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine and memantine: what they are for, how much they help, what side effects they have and which other medicines are used for the symptoms of dementia.

What to expect

The classic dementia medicines do not cure or halt the disease, but in many people they improve or stabilise for a while memory, attention and the ability to carry out daily tasks. The effect is modest and varies greatly from person to person: some patients and families notice a clear change, others none. The new anti-amyloid antibodies are a different family: they clear amyloid from the brain, but are used only in very early stages and in selected patients.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors

In Alzheimer's disease the neurons that make acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter vital for memory and attention, are lost. These medicines block the enzyme that breaks it down and so increase the amount available at the synapse.

Medicine Form Details
Donepezil Tablet, once a day The most widely used; usually taken at night
Rivastigmine Capsules or a daily skin patch The patch causes less nausea
Galantamine Tablets or prolonged-release capsules Also acts on nicotinic receptors

They are used in mild and moderate Alzheimer's dementia, and are particularly helpful in dementia with Lewy bodies and in Parkinson's disease dementia, where the acetylcholine deficit is even greater. They are not recommended in frontotemporal dementia, where they can worsen agitation.

Common side effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, loss of appetite and weight, vivid dreams, cramps. They are reduced by increasing the dose gradually and taking the medicine with food. They can slow the pulse and cause dizziness or fainting, so an ECG should be checked in people with heart rhythm problems.

Memantine

Memantine works differently: it modulates the NMDA glutamate receptors and protects neurons from overstimulation. It is used in moderate and severe Alzheimer's disease, alone or together with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. It is usually well tolerated; it can cause dizziness, headache, constipation or drowsiness. The dose is adjusted if the kidneys are not working well.

When they are stopped

There is no fixed point. Stopping treatment is considered when dementia is very advanced and it no longer helps, or if it causes side effects. It is best done gradually and with monitoring: in some people a decline is noticeable when the medicine is withdrawn.

Treating other symptoms

Dementia is not just about memory. Many patients have behavioural and psychological symptoms: apathy, depression, anxiety, insomnia, agitation, delusions or hallucinations. The first step is to look for treatable causes (pain, urinary infection, constipation, medicines) and to use non-drug measures: routines, daylight, activity, a calm environment.

  • Antidepressants such as sertraline or citalopram for depression and anxiety.
  • Antipsychotics at low doses and for as short a time as possible, only if agitation or hallucinations cause distress or risk: they increase the risk of stroke and death. In dementia with Lewy bodies many antipsychotics are contraindicated because of the risk of severe reactions; quetiapine or clozapine are used with great caution.
  • Insomnia: good sleep habits and daylight; benzodiazepines are avoided because of the risk of falls and confusion.

Medicines best avoided

Some commonly used medicines worsen memory and confusion in older people, especially those with an anticholinergic effect: some first-generation antihistamines, medicines for urinary incontinence such as oxybutynin, tricyclic antidepressants and some muscle relaxants. Benzodiazepines too. Reviewing medication with a doctor or pharmacist is one of the most useful steps.

What about supplements?

No supplement (ginkgo, vitamin E, omega-3, "nootropics") has been shown to prevent or treat dementia. We explain this in Do memory supplements work?.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Which medicine suits me and what benefit can I expect?
  • How will we know if it is working?
  • What side effects should I watch for?
  • Is any medicine I take likely to make my memory worse?
