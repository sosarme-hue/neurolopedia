Neurorehabilitation

Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy and neuropsychology to recover functions after a stroke or injury, or in chronic diseases. The brain can reorganise itself, especially if training starts early.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. What it is
  2. A team
  3. Principles
  4. Techniques and technologies
  5. Where it takes place
  6. Sources

What it is

Neurorehabilitation is the set of therapies that help people with an injury or disease of the nervous system to recover functions or adapt to those that have been lost, to achieve the greatest possible independence and quality of life. It is based on brain plasticity: the brain's capacity to reorganise itself, create new connections and let other areas take over lost functions. Plasticity is stimulated above all by repeated, intensive and meaningful practice.

It is essential after a stroke, a traumatic brain injury or a spinal cord injury, and in diseases such as multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, ALS or cerebral palsy.

A team

Professional What they work on
Rehabilitation physician and neurologist Coordinate, treat spasticity, pain and other complications
Physiotherapist Strength, balance, walking, trunk control
Occupational therapist Activities of daily living (dressing, washing, cooking), home adaptation, assistive devices
Speech and language therapist Language (aphasia), speech (dysarthria), voice and swallowing
Neuropsychologist Attention, memory, executive functions, behaviour
Nursing, psychology and social work Care, emotional support, resources and reintegration

Principles

  • Start early, as soon as the patient is stable, although very intensive sessions in the first 24 hours after a stroke have not shown benefit (AVERT study).
  • Intensity: more hours of practice are associated with better results.
  • Meaningful tasks, aimed at specific goals that matter to the patient.
  • Repetition and gradually increasing difficulty.
  • Involve the family and continue the work at home.
  • Recovery is fastest in the first 3-6 months, but improvement can continue years later.

Techniques and technologies

  • Constraint-induced movement therapy: forcing use of the affected arm.
  • Gait training on a treadmill with body-weight support, robots and exoskeletons.
  • Virtual reality and therapeutic video games.
  • Mirror therapy for the arm and pain.
  • Functional electrical stimulation, which activates muscles during walking.
  • Vagus nerve stimulation combined with arm rehabilitation, approved in the USA in 2021 for chronic stroke.
  • Non-invasive neuromodulation and brain-computer interfaces, under research.
  • Telerehabilitation from home.

Where it takes place

  • In the stroke unit and on the hospital ward.
  • In brain injury or spinal cord injury units, as an inpatient or day patient.
  • In outpatient centres, at home or in day centres.
  • In Spain, brain injury and spinal cord injury associations offer long-term programmes and support.
