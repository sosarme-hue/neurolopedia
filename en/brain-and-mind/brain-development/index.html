Brain and mind

Brain development

From a tube of cells in the embryo to the adult brain, through the explosion of connections in childhood and the remodelling of adolescence. Why the first years matter so much.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

From a tube to a hundred billion neurons

The brain starts to form at three weeks of pregnancy, when many women do not yet know they are pregnant. A sheet of cells in the embryo folds to form the neural tube, which will become the brain and spinal cord. If the tube does not close properly, defects such as spina bifida or anencephaly appear. Taking folic acid from before pregnancy greatly reduces the risk.

The stages before birth

Stage What happens When
Neurulation The neural tube forms and closes Weeks 3-4
Proliferation Neurons are produced at up to 250,000 a minute Mainly weeks 5-25
Migration Neurons travel to their place, guided by radial glia, and form the layers of the cortex Weeks 8-25
Differentiation Each neuron takes on its shape and function From mid-pregnancy
Synaptogenesis Neurons form connections From the third trimester, explosively after birth
Myelination Axons are coated with myelin From late pregnancy to age 25

Disorders of migration cause malformations of the cortex (lissencephaly, dysplasias) that lead to epilepsy and intellectual disability. Nerve growth depends on factors such as nerve growth factor, discovered by Rita Levi-Montalcini (Nobel Prize 1986).

After birth

At birth the brain weighs about 350-400 grams, 25% of its adult weight. By age two it reaches 75%, and by six, 90%. It does not grow through new neurons but through the explosion of connections, myelin and glia.

  • In the first years more than a million new connections a second are formed.
  • Those that are not used are later pruned: the brain of a 2-3-year-old has far more synapses than an adult's. "Use it or lose it".

Sensitive periods

There are windows when the brain is especially sensitive to certain inputs: vision in the first years, language (babies can tell apart all the sounds of all languages until 8-10 months, then specialise in their own), attachment. Severe deprivation during these periods, as seen in Romanian orphanages in the 1990s, leaves lasting effects, although they improve if the child is raised in a family as early as possible. See brain plasticity.

What helps most

A baby's brain is built through interaction: talking to them, looking at them, responding, playing, reading stories. Harvard experts call this "serve and return", as in tennis. Nutrition (breastfeeding, iron, iodine), sleep and an environment free of toxic stress also matter. Screens do not replace interaction.

Adolescence: a brain under construction

The teenage brain is not an immature adult brain but one in the middle of a reorganisation:

  • The reward and emotional systems mature earlier and are very sensitive.
  • The prefrontal cortex, which controls impulses and plans, is the last to mature, around age 25.

This mismatch explains sensation-seeking and risk-taking, especially in front of friends. It also makes the teenage brain more vulnerable to alcohol, cannabis and other drugs. See alcohol, tobacco and drugs.

Ageing

From the age of 30-40 the brain slowly loses volume (0.2-0.5% a year, more after 60). Processing speed and working memory decline, but vocabulary and accumulated knowledge are maintained or improve. Lifestyle has a big influence on how the brain ages. See cognitive reserve.

Developmental disorders

  • Cerebral palsy: damage to the developing brain before, during or shortly after birth.
  • Neurodevelopmental disorders: autism, ADHD, intellectual disability, learning disorders.
  • Malformations: Chiari, congenital hydrocephalus, microcephaly (for example, due to Zika virus).
  • Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability.
  • Genetic conditions such as Tourette syndrome or tuberous sclerosis.
