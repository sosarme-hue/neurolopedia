Spatial navigation

How the brain knows where we are: place cells, grid cells and others form an inner "GPS" discovered by O'Keefe and the Mosers. Why getting lost can be an early symptom of Alzheimer's.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The brain's GPS

Knowing where we are, remembering how to get somewhere or finding the way back home seems simple, but it requires a very sophisticated system. The brain has its own positioning system, whose discovery earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared by John O'Keefe and May-Britt and Edvard Moser.

The navigation cells

Cell type Where What they do Who discovered them
Place cells Hippocampus Fire when the animal is in a particular place John O'Keefe, 1971
Grid cells Entorhinal cortex Fire at points forming a hexagonal grid, like the coordinates of a map May-Britt and Edvard Moser, 2005
Head direction cells Several areas Fire when the head points in a given direction, like a compass James Ranck, 1984
Border cells Entorhinal cortex Fire near walls or boundaries
Speed cells Entorhinal cortex Encode how fast we are moving

Together they form a system that calculates position from movement, like a ship's navigator without GPS, and corrects it using visual landmarks. These cells have also been found in humans, in patients with electrodes implanted to study their epilepsy.

Two strategies for finding your way

  • Cognitive map (spatial strategy): having a representation of the environment and being able to find shortcuts. It depends on the hippocampus.
  • Learned route (response strategy): following a sequence of turns ("right at the chemist's"). It depends on the caudate nucleus, in the basal ganglia.

We use both, and each person tends to prefer one.

London taxi drivers

To get their licence, London taxi drivers must memorise about 25,000 streets and thousands of places, a test known as "The Knowledge" that takes years of study. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire showed that their posterior hippocampus is larger than other people's, that it grows during their training and that its size relates to years of experience. It is one of the best-known examples of brain plasticity.

And satnav? Some studies suggest that always relying on a phone's navigation app engages the hippocampus less and is associated with poorer spatial memory. Finding your own way now and then is good exercise.

When it fails

  • Alzheimer's: the entorhinal cortex is among the first areas to be damaged. That is why one of the first symptoms can be getting lost in familiar places or in a new place while travelling. Virtual reality navigation tests (such as the game Sea Hero Quest, played by millions of people) are being researched to detect it early.
  • Right parietal lobe damage from a stroke: difficulty finding one's way and neglect of the left side of space.
  • Developmental topographical disorientation: healthy people who have got lost constantly since childhood, with no visible lesion.
  • Head injuries, transient global amnesia and dementia with Lewy bodies, where visuospatial problems are very marked.

Differences and training

People vary widely in their sense of direction, which depends on experience, culture and practice more than on sex. Studies with Sea Hero Quest found that people who grew up outside cities, or in cities with irregular street layouts, navigate better. Like memory, navigation improves with practice.
