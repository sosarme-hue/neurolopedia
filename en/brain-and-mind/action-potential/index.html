The action potential

The nerve impulse: an all-or-nothing electrical wave that travels along the axon as ions flow in and out. How Hodgkin and Huxley cracked it using the squid giant axon.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The electrical language of neurons

Neurons communicate through brief electrical impulses called action potentials. Each lasts about 1 millisecond and always has the same shape and size: it is an all-or-nothing signal. Information is not coded in the strength of each impulse, but in how many fire per second (up to hundreds) and which neurons fire.

The neuron as a battery

At rest, the inside of the neuron is negatively charged relative to the outside: about −70 millivolts. This is the resting potential, and it is maintained because:

  • There is more potassium (K⁺) inside and more sodium (Na⁺) outside.
  • The sodium-potassium pump pushes out 3 sodium ions for every 2 potassium ions it brings in, using energy (ATP). This pump uses a large share of the brain's energy.
  • The membrane lets potassium through more easily than sodium.

How an action potential is produced

Phase What happens
1. Stimulus The neuron receives signals from others; if the charge rises to the threshold (about −55 mV), it fires
2. Depolarisation Voltage-gated sodium channels open; sodium floods in and the inside becomes positive (up to +40 mV)
3. Repolarisation The sodium channels close and potassium channels open; potassium flows out and the charge becomes negative again
4. Hyperpolarisation For an instant, the charge drops lower than at rest
5. Refractory period For 1-2 ms the neuron cannot fire again; this stops the impulse going backwards

The impulse travels along the axon like a wave: each depolarised area triggers the next. When it reaches the end, it causes the release of neurotransmitters.

Speed

Speed depends on the thickness of the axon and, above all, on myelin. In myelinated axons the impulse jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next (saltatory conduction). See glia and myelin.

Fibre type Speed
Thin unmyelinated fibres (slow pain) 0.5-2 m/s
Thin lightly myelinated fibres (fast pain) 5-30 m/s
Thick myelinated fibres (movement, touch) 50-120 m/s

That is why, when you stub your toe, you first feel a sharp, well-located pain and a second later a dull ache. See pain.

The squid experiment

The mechanism was discovered by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley in Plymouth in the late 1940s, using the squid giant axon, almost a millimetre across, into which electrodes could be inserted. Their mathematical equations, published in 1952, describe the action potential precisely and are still used today. They received the Nobel Prize in 1963.

When it goes wrong

Many diseases and medicines act on the channels that generate the action potential:

  • Epilepsy: neurons fire excessively and in synchrony. Many antiseizure medicines (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin) block sodium channels. Some childhood epilepsies, such as Dravet syndrome, are caused by mutations in the genes for these channels (channelopathies).
  • Local anaesthetics (lidocaine): block the sodium channels of pain nerves.
  • Tetrodotoxin: pufferfish poison blocks sodium channels and causes paralysis.
  • Multiple sclerosis: loss of myelin slows or blocks conduction. Heat worsens symptoms (Uhthoff's phenomenon) because it hampers conduction in damaged axons.
  • Some kinds of neuropathic pain are due to overactive sodium channels; people who lack one particular channel (Nav1.7) feel no pain.
  • Changes in blood potassium and calcium cause weakness or cramps.

The action potential is what the electroencephalogram and the electromyogram record.
