Brain and mind

Consciousness

Being awake and aware of yourself and your surroundings. Which structures sustain it, what coma and the vegetative state are, and why it remains one of the great mysteries of science.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The great mystery

Consciousness is the subjective experience of ourselves and the world: seeing red, feeling pain, knowing that we exist. It is one of the greatest mysteries in science. The philosopher David Chalmers distinguished between the "easy" problems (which brain areas are active when we are aware of something) and the "hard" problem: why and how the activity of neurons produces a subjective experience.

Medicine distinguishes two components:

  • Level of consciousness (wakefulness): being awake. It depends on the brainstem and thalamus.
  • Content of consciousness: what we are aware of. It depends on the cortex and its connections.

The basis in the brain

  • The reticular formation of the brainstem keeps the cortex awake. It was discovered by Giuseppe Moruzzi and Horace Magoun in 1949. A small lesion here can cause coma.
  • The thalamus: relay and synchronisation of cortical activity.
  • The cortex, especially the posterior areas (parietal, occipital, temporal) and the frontoparietal networks.
  • The claustrum, a thin sheet of grey matter, has been proposed as a "conductor", although this is a hypothesis.

The theories

Theory Main idea
Global workspace (Baars, Dehaene) Information becomes conscious when it is broadcast across the whole brain and made available to many systems at once ("ignition")
Integrated information (Tononi) Consciousness depends on how much information a system integrates; it is measured by a value called Φ
Higher-order theories We are conscious of something when the brain "represents" that it is perceiving it
Predictive processing The brain constantly makes predictions and consciousness reflects its best guesses

Between 2019 and 2025 "adversarial" experiments were run to test the first two theories against each other, and neither came out fully confirmed.

Altered states of consciousness

State Eyes Consciousness Outlook
Coma Closed No response and no sleep-wake cycle Usually lasts days or weeks; progresses to another state
Unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (vegetative state) Open; sleep-wake cycles present No signs of awareness Variable; less recovery over time
Minimally conscious state Open Inconsistent but real signs (tracks with the eyes, sometimes obeys) Better than the previous one
Locked-in syndrome Open Fully conscious, but paralysed except for the eyes Not a disorder of consciousness
Brain death — Irreversible loss of all brain function It is legal and medical death

In 2006 Adrian Owen showed that some patients thought to be in a vegetative state activated the same areas as healthy people on functional MRI when asked to imagine playing tennis. It is estimated that up to 15-25% of these patients have covert consciousness. This has changed the way they are assessed.

How it is assessed

  • The Glasgow Coma Scale (eye opening, verbal response, motor response; from 3 to 15) in emergency care and head injuries.
  • Specific scales such as the CRS-R for minimally conscious states.
  • The electroencephalogram, MRI and evoked potentials.

Other disturbances

  • Syncope: brief loss of consciousness due to lack of blood flow. See fainting.
  • Seizures: many alter consciousness. See epilepsy.
  • Delirium: acute, fluctuating confusion, very common in older people in hospital.
  • General anaesthesia and sleep: reversible states studied closely to understand consciousness.
