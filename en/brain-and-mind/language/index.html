Brain and mind

Language

Speaking and understanding depend on a network of areas, mainly in the left hemisphere, that Broca and Wernicke began to map in the 19th century. What aphasia is and how it is rehabilitated.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A human ability

Language is one of the abilities that most sets us apart as a species: it lets us express an infinite number of ideas by combining a limited number of sounds and words. It depends on a network of brain areas, which in most people lies in the left hemisphere. See corpus callosum and hemispheres.

The classic areas

In 1861 Paul Broca studied a patient, Leborgne, nicknamed "Tan" because it was the only thing he could say. He understood what was said to him but could not speak. At post-mortem he had a lesion in the lower part of the left frontal lobe: Broca's area. In 1874 Carl Wernicke described patients who spoke fluently but meaninglessly and could not understand, with lesions in the back of the left temporal lobe: Wernicke's area. The two areas are joined by a bundle of fibres, the arcuate fasciculus.

The current model

Today we know that language depends not on just two areas but on wide networks with two main routes:

  • Dorsal stream: links sounds with articulation; it allows us to repeat and produce words.
  • Ventral stream: links sounds with meaning; it allows us to understand.

The anterior temporal lobe (word meaning), the motor cortex (articulation), the cerebellum (fluency), the basal ganglia and the right hemisphere (intonation, irony, metaphor) also take part. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

Aphasias

Aphasia is the loss or disturbance of language due to brain damage. It is not a problem of intelligence or of the speech muscles: it is a failure of the ability to handle language.

Broca's Non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic Fairly preserved Impaired Left inferior frontal
Wernicke's Fluent but meaningless, invented words Severely impaired Impaired Left posterior temporal

The most common cause is stroke: up to a third of patients have aphasia. Sudden aphasia is a warning sign: call 112 (or your local emergency number). It also occurs in tumours, head injuries and primary progressive aphasia, a form of frontotemporal dementia or Alzheimer's.

Other speech disorders

  • Dysarthria: difficulty articulating because of weakness or poor coordination of the muscles (stroke, ALS, Parkinson's, ataxias). Language itself is intact.
  • Stammering, dyslexia, developmental language disorders.

Rehabilitation

Intensive speech and language therapy improves aphasia, even months or years after a stroke. It harnesses brain plasticity: other areas of the left and right hemispheres can take over functions. Melodic intonation therapy, which uses singing (more linked to the right hemisphere), helps some patients. For families, it helps to speak slowly in short sentences, give time to answer and use gestures or drawings. See neurorehabilitation.

Language and bilingualism

Bilingual people largely use the same networks for both languages. Speaking more than one language may increase cognitive reserve. Sign languages use the same left hemisphere areas as spoken languages: language is an ability of the brain, not of the ear or mouth.
