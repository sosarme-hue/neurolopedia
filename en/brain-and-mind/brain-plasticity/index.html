Brain and mind

Brain plasticity

The brain changes with everything we learn and can reorganise after an injury. The basis of rehabilitation, of learning at any age and of cognitive reserve.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A brain that changes

Brain plasticity (or neuroplasticity) is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning or injury. For a long time the adult brain was believed to be fixed. We now know it changes throughout life, although more easily in childhood.

Santiago Ramón y Cajal sensed this as early as 1894, when he proposed that learning was based on the formation of new connections: the brain as a "garden" where branches are pruned and grow. Although he also wrote that in the adult "everything may die, nothing may be regenerated", he left the door open for the science of the future to change that.

How the brain changes

Level What happens
Synapses Strengthened or weakened (long-term potentiation and depression); the basis of memory
Dendritic spines Appear and disappear within hours or days
New connections Axons and dendrites grow and form new synapses
Myelin Circuits used more get more myelin and conduct better
Cortical maps Areas of cortex devoted to a function expand or shrink
New neurons In the adult hippocampus, in small numbers (neurogenesis)

Examples that prove it

  • London taxi drivers: to get their licence they must memorise about 25,000 streets. Their posterior hippocampus is larger than other people's, and it grows while they study. See spatial navigation.
  • Musicians: the areas of cortex that control the fingers of the left hand are larger in violinists, the more so the earlier they started.
  • Jugglers: learning to juggle for three months visibly increased the grey matter of visual areas; when they stopped, it shrank again.
  • Blind people: the visual cortex is "recycled" to process touch when reading Braille, or hearing.
  • Phantom limb: after an amputation, the area of cortex that represented the hand is taken over by the face area. See pain.

Critical periods

In childhood there are critical periods when the brain is especially sensitive to certain inputs. If a child has a lazy eye (amblyopia) that is not treated before the age of 7-8, vision in that eye may never develop properly. The same happens with acquiring a native accent in a language. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel studied these periods in the visual system (Nobel Prize 1981). See brain development.

Plasticity after an injury

After a stroke or a head injury, nearby healthy areas and the other hemisphere can take over lost functions. Recovery is fastest in the first three to six months, but can continue afterwards. Neurorehabilitation is based on these principles:

  • Intensive repetition of meaningful tasks.
  • Specificity: you improve what you practise.
  • "Use it or lose it": constraint-induced therapy restrains the healthy arm to force use of the weak one.
  • Techniques such as non-invasive neuromodulation try to boost plasticity.

The downside

Plasticity does not always help. Chronic pain, musician's dystonia, tinnitus, addictions and some epilepsies are partly due to misdirected plasticity.

How to encourage it

Physical exercise, learning new and demanding things, sleep, social life and avoiding chronic stress are the best allies. They are the basis of cognitive reserve.
