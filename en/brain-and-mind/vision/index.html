Brain and mind

Vision

Seeing is not taking a photograph: the brain devotes almost a third of the cortex to building what we see. The journey from the retina to the occipital lobe, and what happens when it is damaged.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

We see with the brain

The eyes capture light, but we see with the brain. About a third of the cerebral cortex is involved in some way in processing visual information. The image we perceive is not a faithful copy of the world but a construction by the brain, which fills in, interprets and sometimes gets it wrong, as optical illusions show.

From the eye to the cortex

  1. Retina: cones (about 6 million, for colour and detail, concentrated in the macula) and rods (about 120 million, for vision in dim light) turn light into electrical signals. The retina already processes the image, detecting contrast and edges.
  2. Optic nerve: carries the signals from each eye. See cranial nerves.
  3. Optic chiasm: the fibres from the inner half of each retina cross over. So everything on our left reaches the right hemisphere, and vice versa.
  4. Thalamus (lateral geniculate nucleus): a relay station. See thalamus.
  5. Optic radiations: run through the temporal and parietal lobes.
  6. Primary visual cortex (V1): in the occipital lobe, at the back of the head.

Two processing streams

From the primary visual cortex, information is split into two main streams:

Stream Direction Question it answers If it is damaged
Ventral Towards the temporal lobe What is it? Shapes, colours, objects, faces Objects or faces are not recognised (agnosia, prosopagnosia)
Dorsal Towards the parietal lobe Where is it and how do I grab it? Position, movement Difficulty reaching for objects or seeing movement

There are specialised areas: V4 for colour, V5 for motion and the fusiform area for faces. Damage to these areas causes surprising disorders, such as not seeing colour or seeing the world as a series of still photographs. Oliver Sacks described many of them in his books, such as The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat. See Oliver Sacks.

In the 1960s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel found that neurons in the visual cortex respond to lines of a particular orientation, and that the visual system needs input in childhood to develop (Nobel Prize 1981). See brain plasticity.

Visual fields

Because the pathways cross and are arranged in an orderly way, the pattern of vision loss shows where the lesion is:

  • One eye only: damage to the eye or optic nerve, as in optic neuritis or a TIA of the ophthalmic artery (amaurosis fugax).
  • Outer halves of both eyes (bitemporal hemianopia): compression of the chiasm, typical of pituitary tumours.
  • The same half in both eyes (homonymous hemianopia): a lesion behind the chiasm, often a stroke of the posterior cerebral artery. The person may not notice and bump into things on one side. It affects driving.

Diseases and vision

  • Migraine with aura: flashes, zigzag lines or blind spots that spread over 5-60 minutes. They are caused by a wave of activity moving across the occipital cortex.
  • Intracranial hypertension: swelling of the optic nerve (papilloedema) and risk of vision loss.
  • Multiple sclerosis: optic neuritis, double vision.
  • Myasthenia gravis: double vision and a drooping eyelid that get worse through the day.
  • Posterior cortical atrophy: a variant of Alzheimer's that starts with visual problems despite healthy eyes.
  • Charles Bonnet syndrome: people with vision loss who see visual hallucinations while knowing they are not real. It is not a mental illness.

How it is examined

Testing the visual fields and fundoscopy in the neurological examination, perimetry, optical coherence tomography (OCT) and visual evoked potentials. See also hearing and sight.
