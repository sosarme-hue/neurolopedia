Brain and mind

Sleep

We spend a third of our lives asleep, and it is not wasted time: the brain consolidates memories, clears waste and regulates mood. Sleep stages, the body clock and how much sleep we need.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

An active state

We spend almost a third of our lives asleep. For a long time sleep was thought to be a passive "switched-off" state. The invention of the electroencephalogram by Hans Berger and, in 1953, the discovery of REM sleep by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman showed that the brain is very active while we sleep.

Sleep stages

Stage Features Share in an adult
N1 Transition; very light sleep; jerks on falling asleep 5%
N2 Light sleep; sleep spindles and K-complexes on the EEG 45-55%
N3 (deep sleep) Slow delta waves; very hard to wake; restorative 15-25%
REM Rapid eye movements, vivid dreams, paralysed muscles, very active brain 20-25%

The stages follow one another in cycles of about 90 minutes, four or five a night. Deep sleep predominates in the first half of the night and REM in the second. That is why the dreams we remember tend to come in the early morning.

During REM, the brainstem paralyses the muscles so that we do not "act out" our dreams. When this mechanism fails, REM sleep behaviour disorder appears, which can herald Parkinson's or dementia with Lewy bodies years in advance.

What regulates sleep

Two processes combine:

  • Sleep pressure (homeostatic process): the longer we are awake, the more adenosine builds up in the brain and the sleepier we become. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors.
  • Circadian clock: the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus sets a rhythm of about 24 hours, adjusted by light. At night, the pineal gland releases melatonin. Teenagers' clocks run late, and older people's run early.

In addition, orexin (hypocretin) neurons in the hypothalamus keep wakefulness stable. Their loss causes narcolepsy.

What sleep is for

  • Memory: during deep sleep the hippocampus "replays" what has been learned and transfers it to the cortex. REM sleep helps integrate and regulate emotional memory.
  • Clearing waste: the glymphatic system removes waste such as beta-amyloid mainly during deep sleep. See cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Plasticity: sleep "prunes" and fine-tunes synapses.
  • Emotions, the immune system, metabolism and growth (growth hormone is released in deep sleep).

Lack of sleep

After a night without sleep, attention and reflexes are similar to those of someone with a blood alcohol level of 0.08-0.1%. Chronic short sleep is linked to a higher risk of high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, depression, accidents and dementia. Rats totally deprived of sleep die within a few weeks.

Sleep disorders

  • Chronic insomnia: the most common.
  • Sleep apnoea: pauses in breathing, snoring, sleepiness.
  • Restless legs syndrome.
  • Narcolepsy: sleep attacks and cataplexy.
  • Parasomnias: sleepwalking, night terrors (from deep sleep), nightmares and REM behaviour disorder.
  • Circadian rhythm disorders: delayed sleep phase, shift work, jet lag.

They are studied with polysomnography, which records the EEG, eye movements, muscles, breathing and oxygen through the night. See sleeping well and treatment of insomnia and narcolepsy.

Dreams

We dream mainly in REM sleep, although also in other stages. Nobody knows for sure what dreams are for. Theories include processing emotions, rehearsing threatening situations or consolidating memory. We all dream every night, even if we often do not remember it.
