Brain and mind

Pain

Pain is not in the damaged tissue: the brain builds it. How the signal travels, why pain can persist without an injury and what types of pain there are.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A necessary alarm

Pain is an unpleasant experience that warns us of actual or potential damage. It is essential for survival: the rare people born unable to feel pain suffer burns, fractures and wounds without noticing, and usually live shorter lives. But pain is not a simple signal travelling from the body to the brain: it is a construction of the brain, shaped by attention, emotions, expectations and past experience.

The pain pathway

  1. Detection: free nerve endings called nociceptors detect harmful stimuli: heat, extreme cold, pressure, inflammatory chemicals.
  2. Transmission: the signal travels along thin fibres. Lightly myelinated Aδ fibres carry fast pain, sharp and well localised. Unmyelinated C fibres carry slow pain, dull and diffuse. See action potential.
  3. Spinal cord: the fibres enter through the posterior horn and connect with neurons that cross to the other side and travel up the spinothalamic tract. See spinal cord.
  4. Brain: the signal reaches the thalamus and from there a network of areas: - The somatosensory cortex says where and how much. - The insula and cingulate add the emotional component: the unpleasantness of pain. - The prefrontal cortex evaluates it and decides what to do.

The brain can turn the volume up or down

In 1965 Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall proposed the gate control theory: in the spinal cord there is a "gate" that lets more or fewer pain signals through. Rubbing a knocked area activates touch fibres that partly close the gate, which is why it helps.

The brain also has its own pain-relief system: from the periaqueductal grey matter of the brainstem it sends signals that dampen pain in the spinal cord, using endorphins (natural opioids), serotonin and noradrenaline. This explains why an injured soldier or an athlete in mid-competition may not feel pain until later, and the placebo effect, which releases real endorphins.

Conversely, anxiety, fear, lack of sleep or focusing on the pain make it worse. See stress and the brain and emotions.

Types of pain

Type Origin Examples
Nociceptive Tissue damage; the alarm system works properly Fracture, burn, osteoarthritis
Inflammatory Inflammatory substances sensitise nociceptors Arthritis, infected wound
Neuropathic Damage to the nervous system itself Diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, pain after a stroke
Nociplastic Oversensitive pain system without clear damage Fibromyalgia, some chronic headaches, irritable bowel

Neuropathic pain is described as burning, electric shocks, tingling or pain when the skin is brushed (allodynia). It does not respond well to ordinary painkillers and needs other medicines. See treatment of neuropathic pain.

From acute to chronic pain

Pain is called chronic when it lasts more than three months. It affects one in five adults in Europe. Over time the nervous system becomes sensitised: neurons in the spinal cord and brain respond more and more (central sensitisation), and the pain continues even after the original injury has healed. It is no longer a symptom but a disease in its own right.

Pain and the head

Headaches are among the most common kinds of pain. In migraine the trigeminovascular system is activated and CGRP is released, the target of modern treatments. See migraine treatments.

Phantom limb

Many people who have had an amputation still feel, and sometimes feel pain in, the limb they no longer have. The brain keeps its "map" of the body, which reorganises. Techniques such as mirror therapy can relieve it, an example of brain plasticity.
