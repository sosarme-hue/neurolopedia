Brain and mind

Memory

There is not one memory but several, in different brain circuits. How a memory forms, why we forget, what sleep does and when forgetfulness is a reason to see a doctor.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

There is not just one memory

Memory is not a single system but several, which depend on different areas of the brain and can fail separately. This was shown by the case of H. M., who after losing both hippocampi could not remember anything new, yet learned to draw while looking in a mirror, even though he did not remember practising. He was studied by Brenda Milner.

Types of memory

Type What it holds What it depends on Example
Working memory Information for seconds while it is being used Prefrontal cortex Remembering a phone number while dialling
Episodic Personal experiences with their when and where Hippocampus, cortex What you had for dinner yesterday
Semantic Knowledge and meanings Anterior temporal lobe That Paris is the capital of France
Procedural Skills and habits Basal ganglia, cerebellum Riding a bicycle
Emotional The emotional charge of memories Amygdala Fear of dogs after a bite
Priming Unconscious influence of what was seen before Cortex Recognising a recently seen word faster

Episodic and semantic memory make up declarative (explicit) memory: the kind we can put into words. Procedural, emotional and priming are non-declarative (implicit).

The three stages

  1. Encoding: information goes in. It depends on attention: what is not attended to is not recorded. Many "memory lapses" are really lapses of attention.
  2. Consolidation: the memory becomes stable. The hippocampus holds it at first and, especially during sleep, gradually transfers it to the cortex. That is why sleeping after studying improves recall.
  3. Retrieval: the memory is called up. Each time something is recalled it is reconstructed and can be changed (reconsolidation). That is why memories are not exact recordings, and we can have false memories with complete confidence.

What happens in neurons

A memory is stored as a change in the strength of the connections between a group of neurons (an "engram"). Synapses that are active together are strengthened (long-term potentiation): "neurons that fire together wire together", as Donald Hebb put it in 1949. Short-term memory modifies existing synapses; long-term memory requires making new proteins and new connections. Eric Kandel showed this in the sea slug Aplysia (Nobel Prize 2000). See brain plasticity.

Forgetting is normal

Forgetting is necessary: the brain discards what is not useful. With age it is normal to find names harder to recall, to take longer to learn or to forget where you left your keys. What is not normal is forgetting whole recent conversations, repeating the same questions or getting lost in familiar places. See Is forgetfulness Alzheimer's?.

Memory disorders

  • Alzheimer's: starts by damaging the hippocampus and recent episodic memory.
  • Mild cognitive impairment.
  • Transient global amnesia: a sudden, temporary loss of the ability to form memories, lasting a few hours.
  • Korsakoff syndrome from lack of vitamin B1 in alcoholism; encephalitis; head injuries; temporal lobe epilepsy.
  • Depression and anxiety affect attention and can look like memory problems.

How to remember better

  • Active recall (testing yourself, explaining it) rather than rereading.
  • Spacing study over time rather than cramming in one day.
  • Linking new material to what you already know; using images and emotions.
  • Good sleep, exercise and avoiding multitasking.
