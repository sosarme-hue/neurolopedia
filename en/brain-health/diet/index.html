Brain health

Diet and the brain

There are no superfoods for the brain, but there are eating patterns that protect it, such as the Mediterranean diet. What the evidence says about supplements, omega-3 and vitamins.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

No superfoods, just patterns

No single food protects the brain on its own. What matters is the eating pattern over the years. The diets most closely linked to a healthy brain are the Mediterranean diet and the MIND diet, which combines the Mediterranean diet with DASH (designed to lower blood pressure).

The Mediterranean diet

It is based on:

  • Extra virgin olive oil as the main fat.
  • Vegetables and fruit every day.
  • Pulses several times a week.
  • Wholegrains.
  • Nuts: a handful a day.
  • Fish, especially oily fish, two or three times a week.
  • Little red and processed meat, few sweets and ultra-processed foods.

In the Spanish PREDIMED trial, a Mediterranean diet with olive oil or nuts reduced cardiovascular events, including stroke, by 30%, and a sub-study showed better cognitive function.

The MIND diet

It highlights ten "brain-healthy" food groups and five to limit:

More Less
Green leafy vegetables (almost daily) Red meat
Other vegetables Butter and margarine
Berries (2 or more times a week) Cheese
Nuts Pastries and sweets
Olive oil Fried and fast food
Wholegrains, pulses, fish, poultry

Observational studies linked it to slower cognitive ageing. A 2023 trial in people who were also losing some weight found no difference compared with a control diet that was also healthy, which suggests the benefit lies in eating well in general rather than in an exact formula.

What to limit

  • Salt: it raises blood pressure, the main risk factor for stroke. Less than 5 g a day (a teaspoon), counting what is already in bread, cured meats and ready meals.
  • Sugars and ultra-processed foods: linked to obesity, diabetes and a higher risk of dementia.
  • Alcohol: see alcohol, tobacco and drugs.

Coffee and tea

Coffee in moderation (2-4 cups a day) is safe and linked to a lower risk of Parkinson's and possibly of stroke and depression. It improves attention in the short term. It is best avoided in the afternoon so as not to disturb sleep. Green tea is also linked to modest benefits.

Vitamins and supplements

In a person with a varied diet, supplements do not improve memory or prevent dementia. See memory supplements. The exceptions are genuine deficiencies:

  • Vitamin B12: deficiency causes cognitive decline, neuropathy and spinal cord problems. It is common in older people, vegans and people who take metformin or omeprazole for years.
  • Folic acid in pregnancy: prevents neural tube defects (spina bifida).
  • Vitamin D, if deficient.
  • Iodine in pregnancy, for the baby's brain development.

Weight and the brain

Obesity in midlife increases the risk of dementia, and poorly controlled diabetes damages blood vessels and brain tissue itself. Keeping a healthy weight and controlling blood sugar is one of the best investments for the brain. In old age, by contrast, the risk lies more in malnutrition and weight loss.
