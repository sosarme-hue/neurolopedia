Brain health

Physical exercise and the brain

Moving is probably the best thing you can do for your brain: it improves blood flow, stimulates new connections, lowers the risk of stroke and dementia, and improves mood and sleep.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The best "medicine" for the brain

If there were a medicine that reduced the risk of stroke, dementia and depression, improved memory and mood, and had almost no side effects, we would all take it. That medicine exists and it is physical exercise. Active people have around a 30% lower risk of dementia and a clearly lower risk of stroke, and the effect is seen at any age.

What exercise does in the brain

  • Improves blood flow: it increases blood flow to the brain, lowers blood pressure and protects the small arteries.
  • Stimulates BDNF: a protein that acts as "fertiliser" for neurons, encouraging new connections and brain plasticity.
  • Makes the hippocampus grow: in a classic study, walking for 40 minutes three times a week for a year increased hippocampal volume by 2% in older people, the equivalent of reversing one or two years of ageing.
  • Promotes neurogenesis in the hippocampus, at least in animals.
  • Reduces inflammation, improves insulin sensitivity and sleep.
  • Lifts mood: its antidepressant effect is comparable to medication in mild or moderate depression, and it reduces anxiety and stress.

How much exercise

The World Health Organization recommends for adults, including older adults:

Type Amount per week Examples
Moderate aerobic 150-300 minutes Brisk walking, cycling, swimming, dancing
or vigorous aerobic 75-150 minutes Running, team sports, hill climbing
Muscle strengthening 2 or more days Weights, resistance bands, climbing stairs
Balance (older adults) 3 or more days Tai chi, yoga, standing on tiptoe

"Moderate" means you can talk but not sing. But any amount is better than none: the greatest benefits come from going from doing nothing to doing something. Even 4,000-7,000 steps a day are linked to a lower risk of dementia and death.

Exercise and neurological diseases

  • Parkinson's: vigorous exercise improves walking and balance and may slow progression. It is recommended for all patients from diagnosis.
  • Multiple sclerosis: it reduces fatigue and improves strength without worsening the disease.
  • After a stroke: it is part of rehabilitation and of preventing a second stroke.
  • Migraine: regular aerobic exercise reduces the frequency of attacks.
  • Alzheimer's and mild cognitive impairment: it improves function and mood and reduces falls.

Less time sitting

As well as moving, it matters not to sit for many hours at a stretch. Sitting for more than 10 hours a day is linked to a higher risk of dementia, even in people who exercise. Get up every half hour, walk while you are on the phone, take the stairs.

Exercise for mind and body at once

Activities that combine movement with coordination, memory and social contact (dancing, team sports, tai chi, learning a routine) seem especially beneficial.

Precautions

Start gently and build up gradually. If you have heart disease, chest pain or dizziness, or have not exercised for years, check with your doctor first.
