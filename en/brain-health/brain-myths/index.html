Brain health

Brain myths

"We only use 10% of our brain", "there are left-brained and right-brained people", "neurons never regenerate"… What science says about the most widespread neuromyths.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. What gets repeated but is not true
  2. How to spot a neuromyth
  3. Sources

What gets repeated but is not true

Many false ideas about the brain circulate, some in self-help books and even in schools. In a survey of teachers in several countries, most believed at least one of these "neuromyths".

"We only use 10% of our brain"

False. Neuroimaging shows that we use the whole brain, although not every area at once or with the same intensity. Any lesion, however small, has some effect. Besides, the brain uses 20% of the body's energy while weighing 2%: evolution would not keep such an expensive organ if 90% of it were useless.

"There are left-brained (logical) and right-brained (creative) people"

False. Some functions are more localised in one hemisphere, such as language in the left in most people. But all complex tasks, creativity included, use both hemispheres, connected by the corpus callosum. A study of more than a thousand people found no one who was more "one-sided" than another.

"Neurons do not regenerate; we are born with all we will ever have"

Partly false. That was believed for decades, but we now know that some new neurons form in adults, at least in the hippocampus, although how many in humans is still debated. More importantly, the brain changes throughout life by creating and strengthening connections: this is brain plasticity.

"Each person learns best in their own style: visual, auditory or kinaesthetic"

False. This is one of the most widespread myths in education. Studies find that teaching to a "preferred style" does not improve learning. What works for everyone is combining formats, practising recall (retrieval) and spacing study over time.

"Listening to Mozart makes you more intelligent"

False. The "Mozart effect" was based on a small 1993 study with a brief improvement in one specific task, which has not been consistently replicated. Learning to play an instrument, however, is good stimulation, like any complex activity.

"Brain-training games prevent dementia"

Not proven. They improve the game being practised but barely carry over to anything else. See cognitive reserve.

"Alcohol kills brain cells"

It depends. Moderate drinking does not directly "kill" neurons as people say, but heavy drinking damages connections, white matter and the cerebellum, and reduces brain volume. See alcohol, tobacco and drugs.

"Sugar makes children hyperactive"

False. Controlled trials found no difference in behaviour after eating sugar. Parties, excitement and what parents expect explain the impression.

"A function lost to a stroke never comes back"

False. Thanks to plasticity and neurorehabilitation, many people regain functions, especially in the first months, and further improvement is possible later.

"A stroke is painful"

False in most cases. Ischaemic stroke almost never hurts, which is why it is underestimated. See how to recognise a stroke.

"The adult brain stops developing at 18"

False. The prefrontal cortex matures until about age 25, and the brain keeps changing throughout life. See brain development.

How to spot a neuromyth

Be wary of claims that promise quick results, use images of lit-up brains to sell a product, or reduce the brain to two kinds of people or a single substance.
