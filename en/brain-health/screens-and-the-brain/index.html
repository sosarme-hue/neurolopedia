Screens and the brain

What we know and do not know about the effect of phones, tablets and social media on the brains of children, teenagers and adults. Sensible advice without alarmism.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Neither demon nor harmless

We spend many hours a day in front of screens: phones, computers, tablets, television. A lot has been said about their effects on the brain, some of it exaggerated and some well founded. What the studies say is that the effect depends mainly on age, time, content and, very importantly, what is not being done while in front of the screen: sleeping, moving, playing, talking to other people.

Young children

The brain develops very fast in the early years, and it does so through interaction with real people: looks, words, play. See brain development.

Age Recommendation (WHO and the Spanish Paediatric Association)
Under 2 No screens, except video calls with family
2-5 years Less than 1 hour a day, good-quality content, watched together
6-12 years Less than 2 hours a day of leisure use, with clear rules
Teenagers Responsible use, no screens in the bedroom at night

Too much screen time in early childhood is linked to delayed language, poorer attention and worse sleep. A key reason is that it reduces conversation with adults ("technoference", which also applies when it is the parents looking at their phones).

Teenagers

Heavy use of social media is linked to more anxiety, depression, poorer body image and bullying, especially in girls, although the relationship is complex and studies disagree on the size of the effect. Designs built to hook users (notifications, infinite scroll, "likes") exploit the reward circuits of the teenage brain, which still has little braking from the prefrontal cortex. Delaying the age of the first phone and of social media is a measure more and more experts recommend.

Sleep

This is the best-established effect. Screens at night:

  • Delay sleep, because the content is stimulating and hard to put down.
  • Blue light lowers melatonin, although this effect is smaller than that of the content itself.
  • Notifications interrupt sleep.

Recommendation: switch them off an hour before bed and charge the phone outside the bedroom. See sleeping well.

Attention and memory

  • Multitasking (studying with the phone beside you, jumping between apps) worsens performance. The brain does not do two tasks at once; it switches between them at a cost. Even having the phone in sight reduces concentration.
  • The "Google effect": we remember less of what we know we can look up, although we remember where to find it.
  • Constantly navigating with GPS reduces the use of spatial navigation.

In older people

For older people, the internet and screens can be beneficial: they keep them in touch with family, give access to information and new activities, and some studies link their use to a lower risk of dementia. What matters is using them actively.

Other effects

  • Eyes: prolonged close work favours short-sightedness in children. Time outdoors prevents it.
  • Sedentary behaviour and posture.
  • Screens can trigger seizures in some people with photosensitive epilepsy and worsen migraine.

Practical tips

  • Screen-free zones and times: meals, the bedroom.
  • Turn off notifications you do not need.
  • The 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet (6 metres) away for 20 seconds.
  • Set an example: children copy what they see at home.
