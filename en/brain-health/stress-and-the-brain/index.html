Brain health

Stress and the brain

Acute stress prepares us to act; chronic stress wears down the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, disturbs sleep and increases vascular risk. What happens in the brain and what helps.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  3. Stress and neurological diseases
  4. What helps

A response that saves us

Stress is the body's response to a threat or a demand. Faced with danger, the amygdala raises the alarm and the hypothalamus sets two systems in motion:

  • The sympathetic nervous system, which releases adrenaline within seconds: the heart races, blood pressure rises, the pupils dilate, more blood reaches the muscles.
  • The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which releases cortisol within minutes and mobilises energy.

This is the fight or flight response, which helps us react. Brief, manageable stress even improves attention and memory. The problem comes when it becomes chronic: when the alarm does not switch off.

What chronic stress does to the brain

  • Hippocampus: sustained cortisol reduces connections and the formation of new neurons, and can shrink its volume. This shows in memory and concentration.
  • Prefrontal cortex: it loses connections, which worsens planning, impulse control and decision-making.
  • Amygdala: it becomes more active and larger, which keeps the cycle of anxiety and vigilance going.
  • Inflammation: chronic stress increases inflammatory substances.

Many of these changes are reversible when stress eases, thanks to brain plasticity.

Stress and neurological diseases

  • Tension-type headache and migraine: stress is one of the most common triggers, and so is the let-down after a stressful period ("weekend" migraine).
  • Stroke: psychosocial stress is linked to higher risk, through blood pressure and the habits that come with it (smoking, poor diet, little sleep).
  • Insomnia: stress and insomnia feed each other.
  • Functional neurological disorders: real symptoms (weakness, tremor, seizures) caused not by a lesion but by a problem in how the brain works, where stress can be a factor.
  • Takotsubo syndrome ("broken heart"): heart dysfunction after intense stress.
  • Its role in dementia risk is being researched, especially through depression, which is an established risk factor.

What helps

Strategy Why it works
Physical exercise Lowers cortisol, releases endorphins, improves sleep
Sleeping well A sleep-deprived brain reacts more to stress
Social relationships Support from others cushions the stress response
Slow breathing Activates the parasympathetic system and slows the heart
Meditation and mindfulness Reduce anxiety; some studies show changes in the amygdala
Time in nature Reduces rumination and tension
Organising your time Setting limits, saying no, separating work and rest

A simple exercise: breathe in for 4 seconds, out for 6, for a few minutes. The long out-breath activates the vagus nerve and calms the alarm response.

When to ask for help

If stress or anxiety lasts for weeks, stops you sleeping, working or seeing people, or comes with persistent sadness, loss of interest or thoughts of harming yourself, see your GP or a psychologist. In Spain the 024 line helps people with suicidal thoughts 24 hours a day; in the UK and Ireland, Samaritans can be reached on 116 123.
