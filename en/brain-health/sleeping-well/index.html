Brain health

Sleeping well

Practical, evidence-based tips for better sleep: schedules, light, screens, caffeine, alcohol, naps and what to do if you cannot get to sleep.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  3. Sleep hygiene

Why the brain sleeps

Sleeping is not "switching off". During sleep the brain works hard:

  • It consolidates memory: the hippocampus "replays" what was learned during the day and transfers it to the cortex for long-term storage. Sleeping after studying improves recall.
  • It clears waste: during deep sleep the glymphatic system kicks in, increasing the flow of fluid between neurons and flushing out waste, including the beta-amyloid of Alzheimer's. A single sleepless night increases the amount in the brain.
  • It regulates emotions: lack of sleep makes the amygdala react more strongly to negative stimuli.
  • It restores energy and regulates hormones, appetite and the immune system.

How much to sleep

Age Recommended hours
Newborns 14-17
Children aged 6 to 12 9-12
Teenagers 8-10
Adults 7-9
Over 65s 7-8

Regularly sleeping less than 6 hours in midlife is linked to a higher risk of dementia, high blood pressure, obesity and depression. Sleeping more than 9-10 hours is also linked to poorer health, although it is often a sign of another illness.

With age, sleep becomes lighter and more broken, and people tend to go to bed and wake up earlier. That is normal; what is not normal is feeling persistently sleepy during the day.

Sleep hygiene

  1. A regular schedule: go to bed and get up at the same time, weekends included. This is the most effective measure.
  2. Daylight in the morning and little light at night, especially blue light from screens.
  3. Caffeine only in the morning; its effect lasts up to 6-8 hours.
  4. Alcohol: it helps you fall asleep, but it fragments sleep and worsens apnoea.
  5. Regular exercise, preferably not in the 2-3 hours before bed.
  6. Light evening meals, not too late.
  7. A dark, quiet, cool bedroom (about 18-20 °C).
  8. If you have not fallen asleep in about 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light until you feel sleepy again.
  9. Short naps (20-30 minutes), before 4 pm.
  10. Use the bed only for sleep and sex.

When to see a doctor

  • Insomnia three or more nights a week for more than three months. The treatment of choice is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, more effective in the long term than pills. See treatment of insomnia and narcolepsy.
  • Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, waking up choking or daytime sleepiness: it may be sleep apnoea, which increases the risk of stroke and cognitive decline and can be treated effectively.
  • An urge to move your legs at night: restless legs syndrome.
  • Irresistible sleep attacks during the day: narcolepsy.
  • Violent movements or shouting while dreaming ("acting out" dreams): this is REM sleep behaviour disorder, which can precede Parkinson's or dementia with Lewy bodies by years. It is worth mentioning to a neurologist.

Sleeping pills

Benzodiazepines and "Z-drugs" (zolpidem) are useful only for short periods. Taken for months or years they cause tolerance and dependence, increase falls and fractures in older people and are linked to memory problems. They should never be stopped suddenly after long-term use: they need to be tapered off gradually with the doctor's help. Melatonin is safe and useful especially in older people and for jet lag, although its effect is modest.
