Brain health

Cognitive reserve

Why two people with the same brain damage can have very different symptoms. Education, stimulating work, reading, languages, music and social life build reserve.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

A post-mortem puzzle

In the late 1980s researchers noticed something surprising: some people who had died without any symptoms of dementia had as many Alzheimer's plaques and tangles in their brains as patients with advanced dementia. Why do some people have symptoms and others not, with the same damage? The proposed explanation is cognitive reserve: the brain's ability to tolerate damage without it showing, by compensating with other networks or using them more efficiently.

The famous Nun Study, which followed hundreds of American nuns for decades, showed that those who wrote in rich, complex sentences when young had a much lower risk of dementia in old age.

Brain reserve and cognitive reserve

  • Brain reserve: the "hardware". More neurons, more synapses, a bigger brain.
  • Cognitive reserve: the "software". How those networks are used: strategies, flexibility, the ability to find alternative routes.

Both are built throughout life and are related to brain plasticity.

What increases it

Factor Examples
Education More years of study, especially in childhood and youth
Complex work Jobs involving decisions, dealing with people, solving problems
Leisure activities Reading, board games, learning an instrument, volunteering
Languages Speaking more than one language
Social life Frequent and varied relationships
Physical exercise Improves the "hardware" and blood supply

Studies in bilingual people suggest that dementia symptoms appear a few years later, although the results are not consistent.

What matters: novelty and challenge

The most beneficial activity is one that is new, demanding and varied. Doing the same crossword every day becomes automatic and gives little stimulation. Learning something different (a language, an instrument, a dance, a craft) forces the brain to make new connections. If it is also social and physical, so much the better.

"Brain training" games

"Brain training" apps and games improve the specific skill practised, but the improvement barely carries over to other functions or daily life. A group of scientists warned about this in 2014. The ACTIVE trial found that processing-speed training was associated with slightly less dementia at ten years, but the other interventions were not. They do no harm, but they are no better than reading, talking or genuinely learning something.

It is never too late

Although reserve is built mainly in youth, it keeps forming throughout life. Retiring should not mean stopping learning. Courses for older learners, workshops, volunteering or looking after grandchildren are all ways to maintain it.

The other side

People with a lot of reserve may take longer to show symptoms, but when they do appear the disease is already advanced and sometimes progresses faster. That is why cognitive tests take education into account: a "normal" score may be low for that person.
