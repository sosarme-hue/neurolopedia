Brain health

Protecting your head

Helmets, seat belts, child car seats and fall prevention: simple measures that prevent thousands of head injuries every year. And what to do about knocks in sport.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  3. In sport

Why it matters

Traumatic brain injuries are one of the leading causes of death and disability in young people, and in older people they are increasingly common because of falls. Even mild knocks, if repeated, can leave lasting effects, and the Lancet Commission counts head injuries among the 14 risk factors for dementia. Most can be prevented.

On the road

  • A seat belt in every seat, including the back seats and on short journeys. It halves the risk of death.
  • Child car seats suited to the child's height and weight, rear-facing for as long as possible.
  • A helmet on motorbikes, mopeds, bicycles and e-scooters. On motorbikes it reduces serious brain injuries by almost 70%; on bicycles, also substantially. It must be certified, fit well and be replaced after a hard impact.
  • Do not drive under the influence of alcohol, drugs or sedating medicines, or when sleepy, and do not use your phone at the wheel.

In sport

  • A helmet for cycling, skiing, snowboarding, climbing, horse riding, skating and hockey.
  • In contact sports (rugby, football, boxing, martial arts), follow the rules and safe techniques.
  • After a concussion during a game: take the player off for the rest of that day, even if they say they are fine, and do not let them return until a doctor allows it, gradually. A second blow before recovery can be very serious, especially in teenagers.
  • Repeated blows over years, even without concussion, are linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy, described in boxers, American football and rugby players. Some countries now limit heading in children's football.

At home: preventing falls in older people

Falls are the leading cause of head injury in people over 65, and those on anticoagulants have a higher risk of bleeding. To prevent them:

  • Strength and balance exercise (tai chi, for example). This is the most effective measure. See physical exercise.
  • Review medication: sleeping pills, sedatives and some blood pressure medicines increase falls.
  • A safe home: good lighting, removing loose rugs and cables, grab rails in the bathroom, a walk-in shower, a night light.
  • Have your eyes checked and wear the right glasses. See hearing and sight.
  • Closed shoes with non-slip soles.
  • Treat low blood pressure on standing, dizziness and foot problems.

In children

  • Never shake a baby: shaken baby syndrome causes severe brain haemorrhages. If the crying becomes overwhelming, put the baby down safely in the cot and step away for a few minutes.
  • Stair gates, guards on windows and balconies.
  • Never leave a baby alone on a changing table or bed.
  • A helmet on bikes, skates and scooters from an early age.

Protecting the spine too

  • Do not dive into shallow or unfamiliar water: it is an important cause of spinal cord injury in young people in summer.
  • A car head restraint set correctly, at head height.

After a knock: when to go to A&E

Go to A&E if, after a blow to the head, there is:

  • Loss of consciousness, however brief.
  • A headache that gets worse, repeated vomiting.
  • Drowsiness, confusion, changes in behaviour.
  • Weakness, tingling, difficulty speaking or seeing.
  • A seizure.
  • Clear fluid coming from the nose or ear.
  • Any knock in people taking anticoagulants or aged over 65.
