Blood pressure and the brain

High blood pressure is the main risk factor for stroke and one of the most important for dementia. Controlling it is the single measure that most protects the brain. Figures, home readings and targets.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

The brain's silent enemy

High blood pressure (hypertension) is the main modifiable risk factor for stroke: it lies behind roughly half of all strokes. It is also an important risk factor for vascular dementia and Alzheimer's. And it almost never causes symptoms: many people do not know they have it until they have a stroke or heart attack. In Spain about a third of adults have high blood pressure, and a large share either do not know it or are not well controlled.

What high blood pressure does to the brain

  • It damages the small arteries: their walls thicken and stiffen (small vessel disease). Small silent infarcts (lacunes), white matter lesions and microbleeds appear, visible on MRI. Over the years they cause slowness, problems with attention and walking and, eventually, dementia.
  • It speeds up atherosclerosis in the carotids and large arteries, which narrow or form clots.
  • It bursts arteries: it is the main cause of brain haemorrhage, and makes aneurysms more likely to rupture.
  • It contributes to atrial fibrillation, another cause of stroke.

The figures

Category Systolic (top number) Diastolic (bottom number)
Optimal < 120 < 70
High-normal 120-139 70-89
Hypertension ≥ 140 ≥ 90
Hypertension on home readings ≥ 135 ≥ 85

The 2024 European guidelines recommend, for most treated adults, a systolic target of 120-129 if well tolerated. In very old or frail people the target is individualised.

Lowering blood pressure protects the brain

  • Every 10 mmHg reduction in systolic pressure cuts the risk of stroke by about 27%.
  • In the SPRINT trial, tighter control (a target below 120) reduced cases of mild cognitive impairment.
  • Blood pressure control is the most effective way to prevent a second stroke. See stroke prevention.

How to measure it properly

  • Seated, after 5 minutes' rest, with your back supported and your arm at heart level.
  • Without having smoked, had coffee or exercised in the previous 30 minutes.
  • With a validated upper-arm monitor (wrist monitors are less reliable).
  • Two readings a minute apart, morning and evening, for several days before your appointment.
  • Home monitoring avoids "white coat hypertension" and detects masked hypertension (normal in the clinic, high at home).

From the age of 40, it is worth having it checked at least once a year.

How to lower it

Without medicines, it can be lowered by 5 to 10 mmHg with:

  • Less salt: under 5 g a day. This is the most effective measure. See diet.
  • More potassium: fruit, vegetables and pulses. Potassium-enriched salt substitutes reduced stroke in a large trial.
  • Regular aerobic exercise.
  • Losing weight if overweight.
  • Limiting alcohol.
  • Treating sleep apnoea.

If that is not enough, there are effective and cheap medicines. Many people need two or three, often combined in a single tablet. Do not stop them once your blood pressure is normal: it is normal precisely because you are taking them.
