Brain health

Alcohol, tobacco and drugs

How alcohol (there is no safe dose), tobacco, cannabis and other drugs affect the brain. Stroke, dementia, seizures and damage to the developing brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

In this article

  1. Tobacco
  2. Alcohol
  3. Drugs
  4. Getting help
  5. Sources

Tobacco

Smoking doubles the risk of ischaemic stroke and increases the risk of subarachnoid haemorrhage. Smoke damages artery walls, speeds up atherosclerosis, thickens the blood and raises blood pressure. It also increases the risk of dementia and many other diseases.

The good news is that stopping works fast: the risk of stroke starts to fall within weeks and, after 2-5 years, approaches that of someone who has never smoked. It is never too late: stopping after a stroke reduces the risk of a second one.

  • E-cigarettes and heated tobacco are not harmless: they contain nicotine, which is addictive and constricts blood vessels, and other substances whose long-term effects are unknown.
  • Passive smoking also increases the risk of stroke.
  • To quit, support from your GP or practice nurse and treatments (nicotine patches or gum, cytisine, varenicline) greatly increase the chances of success.

Alcohol

There is no level of drinking that is good for the brain. The most recent studies, which correct errors in older ones, show that risk increases with any amount, and the idea that a glass of wine a day is protective does not hold up.

Heavy drinking:

  • Increases the risk of brain haemorrhage, raises blood pressure and triggers atrial fibrillation.
  • Reduces brain volume: even moderate drinking is linked to shrinkage.
  • Damages the cerebellum and causes ataxia (unsteady walking).
  • Causes neuropathy and Wernicke's encephalopathy, due to lack of vitamin B1 (thiamine), which if untreated leaves a severe memory disorder (Korsakoff syndrome).
  • Causes seizures, especially on stopping drinking suddenly.
  • Is a cause of dementia in itself and increases the risk of head injuries.
  • During pregnancy, causes fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. In pregnancy, no alcohol at all.

If you drink, the less the better. The Lancet Commission identifies drinking more than 21 units a week as clearly harmful (one UK unit is 8 g of alcohol, roughly half a pint of ordinary beer or a small glass of wine).

Drugs

Substance Effects on the brain
Cannabis In teenagers, poorer memory and attention; higher risk of psychosis, especially with strong cannabis and daily use
Cocaine Ischaemic strokes and haemorrhages, even in young people; vasospasm; seizures
Amphetamines and ecstasy Haemorrhages, overheating, damage to serotonin neurons
Opioids Overdose with respiratory arrest and brain damage from lack of oxygen
Nitrous oxide ("laughing gas") Damage to the spinal cord and nerves due to vitamin B12 deficiency; increasingly common in young people
Inhalants (solvents) White matter damage, ataxia, dementia

The brain keeps developing until about age 25, and the teenage brain is especially vulnerable to drugs and alcohol. See brain development.

Getting help

Addictions are brain diseases, not a lack of willpower, and they can be treated. In Spain, each region's addiction services are free and confidential; elsewhere, your GP can point you to local services.
