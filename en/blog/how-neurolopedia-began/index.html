How Neurolopedia began

An encyclopedia about the brain and nervous system, their diseases, tests and treatments, first written in Spanish and now also in English, with sources on every page.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician

Today sees the launch of Neurolopedia, an encyclopedia about the brain and the nervous system: how they work, how they become diseased and how to look after them.

Neurological diseases are the leading cause of disability in the world. According to the Global Burden of Disease study published in The Lancet Neurology in 2024, more than 3.4 billion people, four in ten, live with a condition affecting the nervous system. Stroke, migraine, dementia, epilepsy and Parkinson's disease touch almost every family.

What you will find

  • Conditions: 42 articles, from stroke to migraine, from Alzheimer's disease to ALS, with their symptoms, causes and treatments.
  • Diagnosis: what each test is and how it is done, from the neurological examination to MRI, the EEG or lumbar puncture.
  • Treatments: thrombectomy, antiseizure medicines, levodopa, deep brain stimulation, anti-CGRP medicines, anti-amyloid antibodies, gene therapies and brain-computer interfaces.
  • Anatomy: from the neuron to the hemispheres, explained without jargon.
  • Brain and mind: memory, sleep, pain, language, emotions, consciousness and plasticity.
  • People and history: from Neolithic trepanation to Cajal, Charcot, Alzheimer, Levi-Montalcini and the Mosers.
  • Brain health: the 14 risk factors for dementia, exercise, sleep, blood pressure, alcohol, hearing and sight, and the most widespread myths.
  • A glossary, a quiz, a history of neurology and a library of sources.

Neurolopedia began in Spanish, and the English edition is now complete too.

How it is made

Every article cites its sources, separates what has been proven from what is sold without evidence, and highlights the warning signs that need urgent attention. The images come from Wikimedia Commons and Unsplash, with the author and licence shown next to each one.

Neurolopedia does not replace your healthcare team. But it aims to help you arrive at your appointment understanding better what is happening to you and with the right questions. And if someone near you has a drooping face, an arm that will not respond or difficulty speaking, call 112 in Europe, or your local emergency number.
