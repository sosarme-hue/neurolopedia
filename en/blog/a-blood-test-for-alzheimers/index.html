A blood test for Alzheimer's: what it detects and when it helps

New blood tests help find out whether memory problems are due to Alzheimer's disease. They are a major advance, but they are not a test to have out of curiosity.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician

Until a few years ago, there were two ways to find out whether a person with memory problems had Alzheimer's disease in their brain: an amyloid PET scan, expensive and available in few hospitals, or a lumbar puncture to analyse the cerebrospinal fluid. Today there is a third way: a blood test. In July 2025 the Alzheimer's Association published the first clinical practice guideline on how to use them. We explain what they measure, what they are for and what they are not for.

What they measure

In Alzheimer's disease two abnormal proteins build up in the brain: beta-amyloid, which forms plaques, and tau, which forms tangles inside neurons. Small amounts of these proteins, or fragments of them, pass into the blood.

The most reliable tests measure a specific form of tau, p-tau217, on its own or combined with the ratio between two types of amyloid (Aβ42/Aβ40). When p-tau217 is high, it is very likely that there are amyloid plaques in the brain.

What the 2025 guideline says

The Alzheimer's Association guideline, developed with the GRADE method from a systematic review, makes two main recommendations:

  • A test with a sensitivity of at least 90% and a specificity of at least 75% can be used as a triage test: if it is negative, the symptoms are unlikely to be due to Alzheimer's disease; if it is positive, it must be confirmed with PET or lumbar puncture.
  • A test with sensitivity and specificity of at least 90% can serve as a confirmatory test, instead of PET or lumbar puncture.

And it sets an important condition: these recommendations are for people with symptoms (problems with memory or other abilities) seen in specialist clinics, such as a memory clinic. Not every test on the market reaches these levels of accuracy.

What they are for

  • Diagnosing earlier and better. Many people with mild cognitive impairment do not have Alzheimer's disease but other causes: depression, sleep apnoea, side effects of medicines, vascular problems. Knowing whether amyloid is present guides the diagnosis.
  • Avoiding unpleasant or expensive tests. A negative result can spare someone a PET scan or a lumbar puncture.
  • Selecting treatments. The new anti-amyloid antibodies can only be used if amyloid in the brain is confirmed.

What they are not for

  • They are not a test for people without symptoms. Many older people have some amyloid in their brain and never develop dementia. A positive result without symptoms causes distress, has no approved treatment and can have consequences, for example with insurance. For now, they are used in research in people without symptoms, not in the clinic.
  • They do not make the diagnosis on their own. The diagnosis is made by a professional who weighs up the history, the examination and cognitive tests. The blood test is one more piece.
  • They do not predict exactly when symptoms will start or how the disease will progress.

Can I ask for one?

If you or a relative notice memory problems that are getting worse, the first step is to talk to your family doctor, who will look for other causes and, if needed, refer you to neurology. These tests are starting to reach memory clinics in some hospitals; availability depends on each health system and centre. It is not a good idea to have one of these tests privately, outside a consultation, with nobody to interpret the result.

And remember that the best strategy known today for protecting memory is not a blood test but looking after the 14 modifiable risk factors: hearing, blood pressure, exercise, smoking, cholesterol and the rest.
