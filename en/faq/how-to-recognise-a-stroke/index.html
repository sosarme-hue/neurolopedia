How do I recognise a stroke and what should I do?

A drooping mouth, an arm that will not lift, strange speech: call 112 (or your local emergency number) and note the time. What not to do while help is on the way.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. Symptoms come on suddenly
  2. What to do
  3. What not to do
  4. If the symptoms have passed
  5. Why the hurry?

Symptoms come on suddenly

A stroke causes sudden symptoms, within seconds or minutes, almost always on one side of the body, and usually without pain. That is why it is sometimes underestimated. Check three things with the FAST rule:

  1. Face: ask the person to smile or show their teeth. Does the mouth droop to one side?
  2. Arms: ask them to raise both arms and hold them for ten seconds with their eyes closed. Does one drift down or not move?
  3. Speech: ask them to repeat a simple sentence, such as "it's a nice day today". Is their speech strange or slurred, do they use words that make no sense, or do they not understand you?

If any of the three is abnormal, it is an emergency.

Other sudden symptoms

  • Loss of vision in one eye or in half the visual field, or double vision.
  • Unsteadiness or severe vertigo with difficulty walking.
  • Numbness or tingling of the face, arm or leg on one side.
  • Sudden confusion.
  • An explosive headache, the worst of your life, especially with vomiting or drowsiness: it may be a haemorrhage.

What to do

  1. Call 112 (or your local emergency number) straight away, even if the symptoms improve or go away. Do not call your GP first or wait "to see if it passes". The emergency call activates the stroke code.
  2. Note the time the symptoms started, or the last time the person was well if they woke up like this or nobody saw it happen. That time decides the treatment.
  3. If they are conscious, keep them lying down with the head slightly raised; if they vomit or are drowsy, on their side.
  4. Loosen tight clothing and stay with them.
  5. Have their list of medicines ready, especially if they take anticoagulants, and a relative's phone number.
  6. Open the front door so the emergency services can get in.

What not to do

  • Do not give them food, drink or pills: they may have trouble swallowing and choke.
  • Do not give aspirin: if it is a bleed, it makes it worse.
  • Do not try to lower their blood pressure with medicines.
  • Do not drive them yourself to the nearest hospital: the ambulance will take them to the one that can treat them and will warn the team in advance.
  • Do not leave them alone or put them to bed to sleep it off.

If the symptoms have passed

It may have been a transient ischaemic attack, a warning that a stroke may follow in the next hours or days. It is also an emergency: go to hospital the same day.

Why the hurry?

In a large stroke, almost two million neurons are lost every minute without treatment. The treatments that unblock the artery, thrombolysis and thrombectomy, work better the sooner they are given: the benefit is greatest in the first hour.
