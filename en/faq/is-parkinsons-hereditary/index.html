FAQ

Is Parkinson's hereditary?

In the vast majority of cases, no. Only 10-15% of people with Parkinson's have a relevant genetic variant. What it means to have an affected relative.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. In most cases, no
  2. The genes that do matter
  3. Should I have a genetic test?
  4. Environmental factors
  5. What you can do
  6. Sources

In most cases, no

About 85-90% of people with Parkinson's disease have no known genetic cause: it is sporadic Parkinson's, involving age, many genetic factors of small effect and environmental factors. Having a parent or sibling with Parkinson's increases the risk somewhat (roughly doubling or tripling it), but the vast majority of relatives will never develop it.

The genes that do matter

A relevant genetic variant is found in 10-15% of patients. The most common:

Gene Inheritance What it means
LRRK2 Dominant with incomplete penetrance Many carriers never develop the disease. The G2019S variant is common in Ashkenazi Jews and in North Africa; another variant (R1441G) is common in the Basque Country
GBA1 Risk factor Increases the risk 5 to 20 times and is linked to more cognitive decline; it is the most common genetic factor
PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1 Recessive Young-onset Parkinson's (before 40), often slowly progressive
SNCA Dominant Very rare; early, aggressive forms

Should I have a genetic test?

Genetic testing is considered mainly when:

  • Parkinson's starts before the age of 50.
  • Several relatives are affected.
  • The person belongs to a population with a high frequency of particular variants.
  • The aim is to take part in clinical trials targeting a gene (there are trials for LRRK2 and GBA1).

In healthy relatives, testing is not usually recommended outside research, because there is no preventive treatment today and many carriers will never become ill. It should always be done with genetic counselling. See genetic testing in neurology.

Environmental factors

  • Pesticides such as paraquat and rotenone, and solvents such as trichloroethylene, are linked to higher risk.
  • Repeated head injuries.
  • Factors linked to lower risk: regular physical exercise, coffee and, paradoxically, smoking (which is obviously not recommended).

What you can do

If you have a relative with Parkinson's, the most useful thing is to lead an active, healthy life with regular exercise, the measure with the most evidence behind it. And see a doctor if symptoms appear such as tremor at rest, slowness, loss of smell or very restless dreams with movements.
