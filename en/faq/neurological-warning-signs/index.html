Neurological warning signs: when to call 112

A short list of nervous system symptoms that are an emergency, what to do while help is on its way and what not to do.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. Call 112 (or your local emergency number) if any of these appear suddenly
  2. While help is on its way
  3. What not to do
  4. Symptoms that are not an emergency but do need a consultation
  5. Sources

Call 112 (or your local emergency number) if any of these appear suddenly

Symptom What it may be More information
Drooping face, weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking or understanding, sudden loss of vision, severe unsteadiness Stroke How to recognise a stroke
Explosive headache, the worst of your life, reaching its peak in under a minute Subarachnoid haemorrhage When to worry about a headache
A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, several in a row without recovery in between, or a first-ever seizure Status epilepticus What to do during a seizure
Fever with headache and a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when pressed, drowsiness or confusion Meningitis or encephalitis Meningitis
Sudden confusion, drowsiness or change in behaviour Stroke, infection, poisoning, low blood sugar Encephalitis
A blow to the head followed by loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, increasing drowsiness or a worsening headache, especially in people on anticoagulants Bleeding after a head injury Head injury
Weakness in both legs that progresses over hours or days, with or without loss of bladder or bowel control Spinal cord compression, Guillain-Barré syndrome Guillain-Barré
Difficulty swallowing or breathing together with muscle weakness Myasthenic crisis, Guillain-Barré Myasthenia gravis

Symptoms that improve or go away are still urgent: a transient ischaemic attack warns of a stroke that may come within hours or days.

While help is on its way

  • Note the time the symptoms began, or when the person was last seen well.
  • If the person is unconscious but breathing, place them on their side (recovery position).
  • Have their list of medicines ready, especially if they take anticoagulants.
  • If possible, film a seizure or the symptoms: it helps the doctor a great deal.

What not to do

  • Do not wait to see if it passes or make your own way to a GP surgery: calling the emergency number gets the person to the right hospital.
  • Do not give food, drink or medicines (not even aspirin: the stroke could be a haemorrhage).
  • Do not put anything in the mouth of someone having a seizure or try to hold them down.

Symptoms that are not an emergency but do need a consultation

Tingling lasting weeks, tremor, memory lapses that are getting worse, headaches that change pattern or wake you at night: make an appointment with your family doctor, who will decide whether you need to see a neurologist. See tingling in hands and feet and is forgetfulness Alzheimer's?.

This list is a guide, but it does not replace the judgement of emergency professionals: if in doubt, call.
