Can epilepsy be cured?

Many people with epilepsy become seizure-free with treatment and some can stop it. Some childhood epilepsies go away on their own and others are cured with surgery.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. It depends on the type of epilepsy
  2. Three paths to seizure freedom
  3. What helps control it
  4. Sources

It depends on the type of epilepsy

The short answer is: often yes, or at least it is controlled so well that the person lives without seizures. The International League Against Epilepsy considers epilepsy resolved when:

  • It was an age-dependent syndrome and the person is now past that age, or
  • They have been seizure-free for 10 years, with the last 5 off medication.

Three paths to seizure freedom

1. Epilepsies that go away on their own

Some childhood epilepsies are self-limited: they disappear with age, with or without treatment. Examples are epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (rolandic epilepsy) and, in most cases, childhood absence epilepsy. See childhood epilepsies.

2. Control with medication

With the first or second well-chosen antiseizure medicine, around 70% of people become seizure-free. Many can lead completely normal lives: study, work, drive (after a year without seizures) and have children.

After two to five years without seizures, stopping medication gradually can be discussed with the neurologist. About 6 in 10 people will stay seizure-free. The risk of relapse is higher if:

  • The epilepsy took a long time to control or there were many seizures beforehand.
  • There is a lesion on the MRI or the electroencephalogram remains abnormal.
  • It is one of certain syndromes, such as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, which usually need lifelong treatment.

Withdrawal must always be gradual and medically supervised, and it is best to avoid driving during that time.

3. Surgery

In 30% of patients, seizures continue despite two appropriate medicines (drug-resistant epilepsy). For them, an epilepsy unit should assess whether the area where seizures start can be removed or disconnected. In temporal lobe epilepsy due to hippocampal sclerosis, surgery leaves 6-7 in 10 patients seizure-free, a much higher rate than continuing to try medicines. Yet it is underused: many people who could benefit are never assessed, or only after many years.

Other techniques are laser thermal ablation, radiofrequency and, if surgery is not possible, neuromodulation (vagus nerve, deep brain or responsive stimulation) and the ketogenic diet.

What helps control it

  • Taking the treatment every day, without missing doses.
  • Sleeping well and avoiding too much alcohol.
  • Knowing and avoiding your own triggers.
  • Going to check-ups and getting advice if the seizures change.
