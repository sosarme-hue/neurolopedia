FAQ

Why do my hands or feet go numb?

Brief tingling from a posture is normal. When it is frequent or persistent, the most common causes are carpal tunnel syndrome, diabetic neuropathy or vitamin B12 deficiency.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. When it is nothing to worry about
  2. When to see a doctor
  3. Warning signs
  4. What tests are done
  5. What you can do
  6. Sources

When it is nothing to worry about

Feeling your hand or foot "go to sleep" after leaning on it for a long time, crossing your legs or sleeping on your arm is normal: a nerve is briefly compressed and the feeling goes within minutes once you move. Mild tingling in the cold, or with anxiety and fast breathing, is also normal.

When to see a doctor

If the tingling (doctors call it paraesthesia) is frequent, persistent or getting worse, or comes with pain, weakness or clumsiness. The pattern and location of the tingling are a strong clue to the cause:

Where and how Most likely cause
Thumb, index and middle finger, especially at night, better after shaking the hand Carpal tunnel syndrome
Little and ring finger, when leaning on the elbow Compression of the ulnar nerve at the elbow
Both feet, in a "sock" pattern that creeps upwards Polyneuropathy: diabetes, alcohol, vitamin B12 deficiency
A strip running down the arm or leg from the neck or back Radiculopathy (herniated disc, arthritis of the spine)
Outer thigh, with burning Meralgia paraesthetica (a nerve compressed at the groin by tight clothing or excess weight)
Around the mouth and in both hands, with fast breathing Anxiety and hyperventilation
Half the body, lasting days or coming and going, in a young person Multiple sclerosis should be considered

Warning signs

Call 112 (or your local emergency number) if tingling or numbness comes on suddenly on one side of the body (face, arm and leg on the same side), especially with weakness, difficulty speaking or loss of vision: it may be a stroke.

Go to A&E if:

  • Weakness spreads over hours or days from the legs upwards: it may be Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • There is tingling in both legs with problems passing urine or numbness around the genitals: it may be compression of the spinal cord or cauda equina.

What tests are done

  • An examination of sensation, reflexes and strength.
  • Blood tests: glucose, vitamin B12, thyroid, kidney function.
  • Electromyography, which measures how well the nerves conduct.
  • An MRI of the spine or brain depending on what is suspected.

What you can do

  • Avoid postures that compress nerves: do not lean on your elbows for long, do not cross your legs, use night splints if you have carpal tunnel syndrome.
  • Keep diabetes well controlled.
  • Limit alcohol.
  • Have your vitamin B12 checked if you are vegan or have taken metformin or omeprazole for years.
