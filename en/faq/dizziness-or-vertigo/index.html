Is it dizziness or vertigo?

"Dizziness" can mean feeling faint, unsteady or that the room is spinning. Telling them apart points to the cause: the inner ear, blood pressure, the heart or, rarely, the brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

One word, four sensations

"I feel dizzy" can mean very different things, and telling them apart is the first step to finding the cause:

Sensation How it is described Most common origin
Vertigo "Everything is spinning", "I feel I'm moving even when still" Inner ear; rarely the brain
Presyncope "I'm going to faint", blurred vision, cold sweat, weakness Drop in blood pressure, heart, dehydration
Unsteadiness "I walk like I'm on a boat", "my balance is off", mainly when standing or walking Nerves in the legs, cerebellum, eyesight, medicines, age
Non-specific dizziness "Light-headed", "woozy", a floating feeling Anxiety, medicines, chronic functional dizziness

Vertigo: nearly always the ear

The most common causes of vertigo are in the inner ear:

  • Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo: seconds of vertigo when turning over in bed, lying down or looking up. It is the most common cause and is cured with a manoeuvre.
  • Vestibular neuritis: intense, continuous vertigo for days, with vomiting, after a cold.
  • Ménière's disease: attacks lasting hours with hearing loss, ringing and a blocked ear.
  • Vestibular migraine: attacks of vertigo in people with migraine.

Presyncope: blood pressure and the heart

The feeling that you are about to faint is due to too little blood reaching the brain:

  • Low blood pressure on standing, especially due to blood pressure medicines, dehydration or heat.
  • Vasovagal reaction: from standing for a long time, seeing blood or being in pain.
  • Arrhythmias or heart disease, especially if the dizziness comes on with exertion or palpitations.

See fainting.

Unsteadiness: many factors

In older people, unsteadiness is usually due to several problems added together: loss of sensation in the feet (neuropathy), poor eyesight, arthritis, muscle weakness and medicines (sedatives, blood pressure medicines, antidepressants). It increases the risk of falls, which can be prevented with strength and balance exercise and by reviewing medication.

When it is urgent

Call 112 (or your local emergency number) if the dizziness or vertigo comes on suddenly with:

  • Inability to stand or walk.
  • Double vision, difficulty speaking or swallowing.
  • Weakness, clumsiness or numbness on one side of the body.
  • A severe headache or neck pain.
  • Loss of consciousness, chest pain or palpitations.

It may be a stroke of the cerebellum or brainstem, or a heart problem.

What to tell the doctor

  • Exactly what you feel: is it spinning? Are you about to faint? Is your balance off?
  • How long it lasts: seconds, minutes, hours or days.
  • What triggers it: turning your head, standing up, exertion.
  • Accompanying symptoms: ear problems, headache, palpitations.
  • The medicines you take.
