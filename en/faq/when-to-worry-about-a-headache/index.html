When should I worry about a headache?

Most headaches are harmless, like migraine or tension-type headache. These are the warning signs that mean you need urgent medical attention.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

General information only. It does not replace advice from a health professional. For sudden neurological symptoms, call emergency services.

In this article

  1. Most are not serious
  2. Warning signs: go to A&E or call 112 (or your local emergency number)
  3. Signs that mean seeing your doctor soon
  4. What if I often have headaches?
  5. Common myths

Most are not serious

Headache is one of the most common reasons for seeing a doctor: almost everyone has had one. In more than 90% of cases it is a primary headache, meaning the headache is the condition itself, such as migraine, tension-type headache or cluster headache. They can be very unpleasant, but they are not life-threatening.

A small proportion are secondary headaches, symptoms of another problem: an infection, a haemorrhage, a tumour or inflamed arteries. To detect them, doctors use a list of warning signs.

Warning signs: go to A&E or call 112 (or your local emergency number)

  • Sudden, explosive pain that peaks in less than a minute (a "thunderclap headache", the worst pain of your life): it may be a subarachnoid haemorrhage.
  • Headache with fever, stiff neck, vomiting or a skin rash: it may be meningitis.
  • Headache with neurological symptoms: weakness, difficulty speaking, loss of vision, confusion, drowsiness or a seizure. It may be a stroke, a haemorrhage or another lesion.
  • Headache after a blow to the head, especially if you take anticoagulants or are over 65.
  • Severe headache in pregnancy or after giving birth: it may be pre-eclampsia or cerebral venous thrombosis.

Signs that mean seeing your doctor soon

  • A new headache from the age of 50.
  • A headache that clearly changes its pattern or becomes progressive over days or weeks.
  • Pain that gets worse when lying down, coughing or straining, or that repeatedly wakes you at night.
  • Pain that improves when lying down and worsens when standing (it may be due to low cerebrospinal fluid pressure).
  • In people over 50, pain in the temples with pain on chewing, fever or visual disturbances: it may be giant cell arteritis, which can cause blindness if not treated promptly with steroids.
  • Headache in people with cancer, HIV or who take immunosuppressants.
  • Blurred vision, brief visual blackouts or a pulsing whooshing in the ears (possible intracranial hypertension).

What if I often have headaches?

  • If you have headaches on more than 8-10 days a month, or take painkillers very often, see a doctor: you may need preventive treatment, and you may be developing a medication overuse headache.
  • Keep a headache diary: days with pain, severity, accompanying symptoms, medication and possible triggers. It helps a great deal with diagnosis.

Common myths

  • "If it hurts a lot, it must be something bad." Severity does not indicate seriousness: migraine can be very intense and is benign.
  • "I need a scan to find out what I have." In typical primary headaches, scans are normal and not needed.
  • "High blood pressure causes headaches." Only very sudden, severe rises do; ordinary high blood pressure almost never hurts.
  • "It's my eyes" or "it's my neck." These are uncommon causes of chronic headache.
