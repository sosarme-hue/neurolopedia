Pío del Río Hortega

A pupil of Cajal, he discovered microglia and characterised oligodendrocytes using his own staining method. Twice nominated for the Nobel Prize, he died in exile in Buenos Aires.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Pío del Río Hortega was born in 1882 in Portillo, a village in the province of Valladolid (Spain), into a well-off farming family. He studied medicine at the University of Valladolid. He was drawn to microscope research, and in 1915 he moved to Madrid to work in the laboratory of Nicolás Achúcarro, one of Cajal's most brilliant pupils. He died in exile in Buenos Aires in 1945.

A new method

Río Hortega was an extraordinary technician. He developed a variant of the silver stain, using ammoniacal silver carbonate, which showed the cells of the glia, until then almost invisible, with unprecedented clarity.

The "third element"

At the time, Cajal had described two types of cell in the nervous system: neurons and astrocytes. There remained a poorly understood group of cells that Cajal called the "third element". With his method, Río Hortega showed in 1919-1921 that this third element was in fact two different types of cell:

Cell What he discovered What we know today
Microglia (1919) Small branching cells that moved and changed shape after an injury, engulfing debris They are the brain's immune cells, defenders and "refuse collectors"; key in Alzheimer's, ALS and multiple sclerosis
Oligodendroglia (1921) Cells with few processes ("oligo") in contact with axons They make the myelin of the central nervous system

He proposed, correctly, that microglia had a different origin from neurons and the other glia, coming from the mesoderm. A century later it was confirmed that they come from cells of the embryo's yolk sac, in a similar way to immune cells. That is why microglia were called "Hortega cells" for years.

The conflict with Cajal

His findings contradicted some of Cajal's ideas, and there were personal tensions in the laboratory. In 1920 Río Hortega had to leave Cajal's laboratory and continued his work in a laboratory of the Board for the Extension of Studies at the Residencia de Estudiantes in Madrid, where he trained many Spanish and foreign researchers. Over the years, the relationship between the two softened.

Brain tumours

Río Hortega applied his methods to the study of brain tumours, and proposed a classification of gliomas according to the type of cell they derived from, which was very influential in its time. He directed the National Cancer Institute in Madrid. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize twice.

Exile

When the Spanish Civil War broke out in 1936, Río Hortega, who supported the Republic, left Spain. He worked in Paris and then in Oxford, where he received an honorary doctorate and worked with the neurosurgeon Hugh Cairns. In 1940 he settled in Buenos Aires, where he set up a laboratory and continued his research until his death from cancer in 1945. His remains were moved to Valladolid in 1986.

Legacy

Río Hortega is, together with Cajal, one of the great names of the Spanish School of Neurohistology. For decades microglia were a forgotten cell; today they are one of the most active fields in neuroscience, because we know that brain inflammation is involved in almost every neurological disease. A university hospital in Valladolid bears his name.
