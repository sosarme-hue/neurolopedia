Alois Alzheimer

German psychiatrist and neuropathologist who in 1906 described, in a 51-year-old patient, the plaques and tangles of the disease that bears his name.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Alois Alzheimer was born in 1864 in Marktbreit, a small town in Bavaria (Germany). He studied medicine in Berlin, Tübingen and Würzburg. In 1888 he started working at the municipal asylum for the mentally ill and epileptics in Frankfurt, where he met the histologist Franz Nissl, with whom he shared an interest in studying the brains of psychiatric patients under the microscope. His idea was that many mental illnesses had a physical basis in the brain that could be seen.

Auguste D.

On 25 November 1901, Auguste Deter, a 51-year-old woman with a strange condition, was admitted to the Frankfurt asylum: memory loss, disorientation, groundless jealousy, difficulty speaking and understanding. Alzheimer interviewed her and noted down a phrase that would become famous: "Ich habe mich sozusagen verloren", "I have lost myself, so to speak".

He was struck that someone so young should have such severe dementia, which was then considered a disease of old age. In 1903 he moved to Heidelberg and then to Munich, to the clinic of the great psychiatrist Emil Kraepelin, but he kept following the patient.

The plaques and tangles

Auguste died on 8 April 1906. Alzheimer asked for her brain to be sent to Munich and studied it with a silver stain recently developed by Max Bielschowsky. He found:

  • An atrophied brain, with loss of neurons.
  • Abnormal deposits between the neurons: the plaques, which we now know are made of beta-amyloid.
  • Bundles of twisted fibrils inside the neurons: the neurofibrillary tangles, made of the protein tau.

On 3 November 1906 he presented the case at a meeting of psychiatrists in Tübingen, under the title "On a peculiar disease of the cerebral cortex". The presentation aroused little interest: there were no questions. He published it in 1907.

The name

In 1910 Kraepelin included the disease in the eighth edition of his influential psychiatry textbook under the name "Alzheimer's disease". For decades it was considered a rare form of presenile dementia, in young people. Only in the 1970s was it understood that dementia in older people was, in most cases, the same disease. See Alzheimer's disease.

Other work

Alzheimer was one of the great neuropathologists of his time. He studied brain syphilis (general paresis), Huntington's disease, epilepsy and dementia caused by arteriosclerosis (today's vascular dementia). His colleague Friedrich Lewy described in his laboratory the bodies that now bear his name (dementia with Lewy bodies), and Hans Creutzfeldt and Alfons Jakob also passed through it (Creutzfeldt-Jakob).

In 1912 he was appointed professor of psychiatry in Breslau. On the train journey there he caught an infection that affected his heart and kidneys. He died in 1915, aged only 51.

More than a century later

In 1995 Auguste Deter's original medical file was found in Frankfurt, and in 1997 the microscope slides of her brain were located, confirming the diagnosis. Today more than 55 million people live with dementia worldwide, most of them due to Alzheimer's. The plaques and tangles that Alzheimer described are the target of the new anti-amyloid antibodies and can be detected in life with PET, lumbar puncture and blood tests.
