Auguste Deter

The first patient diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease. Her medical file, lost for almost a century, was found in Frankfurt in 1995.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

  3. "I have lost myself"

Who she was

Auguste Deter (née Auguste Hochmann) was born on 16 May 1850 in Kassel, Germany, into a working-class family. She married Carl Deter, a railway clerk, and they had a daughter. They lived in Frankfurt. She led a normal life until, at around the age of 50, she began to change.

The first symptoms

According to her husband, the first symptom was unfounded jealousy. Then she began to forget things, to hide objects around the house, to get lost and to make mistakes when cooking. She became suspicious, believed someone wanted to harm her, and sometimes screamed for hours or knocked on the neighbours' doors. Her husband, overwhelmed, took her to the doctor.

"I have lost myself"

On 25 November 1901 she was admitted to the municipal asylum for the mentally ill and epileptics in Frankfurt, which people called the "castle of the insane". The next day she was examined by the doctor Alois Alzheimer, who recorded the interview in detail:

  • "What is your name?" "Auguste."
  • "And your surname?" "Auguste."
  • "What is your husband's name?" "Auguste, I think."

When he asked her to write her name, she began to write "Frau" and stopped: she had forgotten. And she said the phrase that today sums up the experience of the disease: "Ich habe mich sozusagen verloren", "I have lost myself, so to speak".

Auguste also had difficulty naming objects, understanding and reading, and did not know where she was. Alzheimer took an interest in her because he had never seen such severe dementia in someone so young: at that time dementia was considered a feature of old age.

The last years

When Alzheimer moved to Munich in 1903, he kept asking for news of the patient. The family could not pay for her stay and there were plans to move her, but Alzheimer intervened so that she could stay. Her condition worsened steadily: she stopped speaking, recognising anyone and getting out of bed. She died on 8 April 1906, aged 55, of a generalised infection from pressure sores, a common complication of advanced dementia.

Her brain was sent to Munich, where Alzheimer described the plaques and tangles that characterise Alzheimer's disease.

Rediscovered

For almost a century Auguste was just "Auguste D.". In 1995 the psychiatrist Konrad Maurer found her original medical file in the archive of the Frankfurt hospital, with Alzheimer's notes, four photographs and samples of her handwriting. In 1997 the microscope slides of her brain were located in Munich, confirming the diagnosis.

Because she fell ill so young, researchers have investigated whether she had a hereditary form. A 2013 study found a possible mutation in the PSEN1 gene, one of the genes of familial Alzheimer's, but other researchers could not confirm it. Her APOE genotype was the most common one (APOE3/3). See genetic testing in neurology.

Why remember her

Auguste Deter is one of the most important patients in the history of medicine. Her case is a reminder that behind every disease there is a person and a family. Her phrase, "I have lost myself", describes what people with dementia go through better than any definition. Today the first symptoms of Alzheimer's can be recognised earlier, at the stage of mild cognitive impairment, and research is looking for treatments that slow the disease. See also is forgetfulness Alzheimer's?.
