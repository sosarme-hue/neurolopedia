Wilder Penfield

The neurosurgeon who stimulated the brains of awake patients during epilepsy surgery and drew the famous homunculus. He founded the Montreal Neurological Institute.

Who he was

Wilder Graves Penfield was born in 1891 in Spokane, Washington (United States). He studied at Princeton University, where he played American football, and with a Rhodes scholarship studied at Oxford under Charles Sherrington, who passed on his passion for the nervous system. He trained as a surgeon in the United States and, in Boston, came to know the work of Harvey Cushing.

In 1924 he spent several months in Madrid, in the laboratory of Pío del Río Hortega, to learn the glial staining methods of Cajal's school. He wanted to understand the scar left by brain injuries, which he believed caused epilepsy. He died in 1976.

The Montreal Neurological Institute

In 1928 he moved to Montreal (Canada), and in 1934, with support from the Rockefeller Foundation, he founded the Montreal Neurological Institute, bringing neurologists, neurosurgeons and researchers together under one roof. It became a model for neuroscience centres around the world. That year he became a Canadian citizen.

The Montreal procedure

Penfield operated on patients with epilepsy that did not respond to treatment, to remove the area of the brain where the seizures started. To avoid damaging important areas, he operated on them awake, under local anaesthetic only, since the brain has no pain receptors. With an electrode he stimulated different points of the cortex with very weak currents and asked the patient what they felt.

In this way he discovered that:

  • Stimulating a strip of the frontal lobe made a specific part of the body move.
  • Stimulating the strip just behind it made the patient feel tingling in a part of the body.
  • Stimulating certain areas left the patient unable to speak.

The homunculus

With thousands of stimulations, Penfield and his colleagues drew the homunculus, the famous map of the body in the motor and sensory cortex (1937, and in the book The Cerebral Cortex of Man, 1950). The face, lips, tongue and hands take up an enormous space, because they need very fine control; the trunk and legs, very little. This confirmed what Hughlings Jackson had suspected from the march of seizures. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

Memories that come back

When the temporal lobe was stimulated, some patients said they relived experiences: hearing a song, seeing a scene from their childhood, feeling that they had lived through something before. Penfield thought he had found the place where memories were stored. Today these responses, which only appeared in some patients with epilepsy, are thought to be more complex, but they were a milestone in the study of memory.

Memory and Brenda Milner

Two of his patients, operated on a single temporal lobe, unexpectedly lost the ability to form new memories. His colleague Brenda Milner studied them and deduced that the other hippocampus was already damaged. This later led her to study the famous patient H. M. and to discover the role of the hippocampus.

The man

Penfield was also a great communicator and writer: he published historical novels and, at the end of his life, The Mystery of the Mind (1975), in which he asked whether the mind could be explained by the brain alone. In 1928 he had to operate on his own sister for a brain tumour.

Legacy

The Montreal procedure, refined with the EEG of his colleague Herbert Jasper, is the basis of epilepsy surgery and of the awake tumour surgery done today in many hospitals to protect speech and movement. See neurosurgery.
