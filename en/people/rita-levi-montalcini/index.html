Rita Levi-Montalcini

She did research in a makeshift laboratory in her bedroom during Fascist persecution and discovered nerve growth factor. Nobel Prize 1986, she worked until she was 103.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who she was

Rita Levi-Montalcini was born in Turin (Italy) in 1909, into a cultured Jewish family. She had a twin sister, Paola, who became a well-known painter. Her father, an engineer, believed women should not go to university. At 20, after her governess died of cancer, Rita decided to study medicine, and won her father round. She studied in Turin under the histologist Giuseppe Levi, in a group that produced three Nobel laureates: herself, Salvador Luria and Renato Dulbecco. She died in 2012, aged 103.

A laboratory in the bedroom

In 1938 Mussolini's racial laws banned Jews from working at universities. Levi-Montalcini did not give up: she set up a small laboratory in her bedroom, "Robinson Crusoe style", as she put it, with sewing tools and needles sharpened into scalpels. There she studied how nerves grow in chick embryos, using eggs she bought from farmers and which her family then ate.

During the German occupation the family hid in Florence under false identities. After liberation she worked as a doctor with refugees and sick soldiers of the Allied forces.

Nerve growth factor

In 1947 the embryologist Viktor Hamburger invited her to Washington University in St Louis (United States). She planned to stay for a few months and stayed for thirty years.

There, in 1951-1952, she made a decisive discovery. When she implanted a mouse tumour into chick embryos, she saw that the embryo's nerves grew excessively towards the tumour, and even towards other organs. She deduced that the tumour was releasing a substance that stimulated nerve growth. To prove it, she travelled to Rio de Janeiro, where she learned tissue culture: when a piece of tumour was placed next to nerve ganglia in a culture, a "halo" of nerve fibres appeared within a few hours.

With the biochemist Stanley Cohen she purified the substance: nerve growth factor (NGF). In a control experiment Cohen used snake venom and discovered by chance that it was a very rich source of NGF; they later found it in large amounts in the salivary glands of male mice. It was the first growth factor to be discovered. Cohen later discovered epidermal growth factor (EGF).

The Nobel Prize

In 1986 Levi-Montalcini and Cohen received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries of growth factors". She was the fourth woman to win the Nobel Prize in Medicine.

A committed woman

She returned to Italy, where she directed the Laboratory of Cell Biology of the National Research Council in Rome. She was appointed senator for life in 2001, and set up a foundation to give study grants to African women. She always championed science and women's equality. Her autobiography is called In Praise of Imperfection. She kept going to her laboratory until she was 100, and used to say: "The body does what it wants. I am not the body, I am the mind."

Legacy

NGF opened up the field of neurotrophins, a family of proteins that keep neurons alive and guide their connections during brain development. It confirmed an intuition of Cajal's: axons grow attracted by chemical signals. One of them, BDNF, is the one that increases with physical exercise and promotes plasticity. In 2017 NGF eye drops were approved for a disease of the cornea caused by nerve damage, and neurotrophins are being studied in Alzheimer's, pain and neuropathies.
