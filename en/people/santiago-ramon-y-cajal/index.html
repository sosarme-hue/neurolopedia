Santiago Ramón y Cajal

The most influential Spanish scientist in history. He showed that the nervous system is made of independent neurons, worked out how the impulse travels and left thousands of drawings still in use today. Nobel Prize 1906.

Who he was

Santiago Ramón y Cajal was born on 1 May 1852 in Petilla de Aragón, a small enclave of Navarre surrounded by Aragonese land, and grew up in several villages in Aragon, such as Ayerbe. As a child he was rebellious, mischievous and not very studious: he built home-made cannons, climbed on rooftops and drew constantly, because he wanted to be a painter. His father, Justo Ramón, a doctor and anatomy teacher, apprenticed him to a barber and a shoemaker to straighten him out, and ended up taking him to a cemetery at night to collect bones for studying anatomy. That was when drawing and science came together.

He studied medicine in Zaragoza (1873). As an army doctor he was posted to Cuba during the Ten Years' War, where he caught malaria and dysentery; he returned greatly weakened and later also had tuberculosis. He was professor of anatomy in Valencia (1883) and of histology in Barcelona (1887) and Madrid (1892). He died in Madrid on 17 October 1934.

Discovering Golgi's method

In 1887, in Madrid, the neurologist Luis Simarro showed him some preparations stained with Camillo Golgi's method. Cajal was captivated: for the first time he saw whole neurons. He improved it with a double impregnation and, above all, had a key idea: to study the tissue of embryos and young animals, where myelin does not yet block the stain and neurons are smaller and easier to see whole. It was, as he put it, studying the forest while it was still young.

1888: the peak year

In 1888, working in Barcelona almost alone and paying out of his own pocket for a journal to publish his findings, he studied the cerebellum of birds and reached a revolutionary conclusion: neurons are independent cells that communicate by contact (contiguity), without fusing with one another in a network. This is the neuron doctrine, the foundation of all neuroscience. See the neuron.

Since his papers in Spanish were barely read abroad, in 1889 he travelled to a congress in Berlin with his microscope and preparations, and convinced the eminent anatomist Albert von Kölliker, who learned Spanish to read him and spread his work.

Ideas ahead of their time

Idea What he proposed Confirmation
Dynamic polarisation The impulse enters through the dendrites and cell body and leaves through the axon Basis of neuronal physiology
Growth cone (1890) Axons grow guided by a mobile tip that "seeks out" its path Confirmed in the 20th century
Chemotaxis Chemical substances attract axons to their target Factors such as the NGF of Levi-Montalcini
Dendritic spines They are real, not a staining artefact Electron microscopy
Plasticity (1894) Learning is based on the growth of new connections Basis of memory
Regeneration Peripheral nerves regenerate; the central system, very little Current research

He also described the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gut, now known to act as pacemakers for digestion. His great work, Textura del sistema nervioso del hombre y de los vertebrados (Texture of the Nervous System of Man and the Vertebrates, 1899-1904), is one of the founding texts of neuroscience, together with Degeneration and Regeneration of the Nervous System (1913-1914).

The Nobel Prize

In 1906 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with Golgi, who defended the opposite theory in his lecture. In the 1950s the electron microscope confirmed that Cajal was right. He was, and remains, the most cited and influential Spanish scientist in history.

The artist

He left nearly 3,000 drawings of neurons, in ink and pencil, of extraordinary beauty and precision, which are still used in textbooks. They have toured museums around the world, for example in the exhibition The Beautiful Brain in the United States (2017-2021). In 2017 UNESCO added his legacy to its Memory of the World register. He was also a pioneer of photography, including colour photography, on which he wrote a book.

His school and his commitment to Spanish science

In 1901 the Laboratory of Biological Research was created for him, the origin of today's Cajal Institute of the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC), and in 1907 he chaired the Board for the Extension of Studies, which sent young researchers abroad to train and boosted Spanish science. He founded the so-called Spanish School of Neurohistology: Pío del Río Hortega, Rafael Lorente de Nó, Fernando de Castro, Jorge Francisco Tello and Nicolás Achúcarro.

He wrote books for the public and for young people: Advice for a Young Investigator (Reglas y consejos sobre investigación científica, 1897), in which he argues that perseverance matters more than talent; his memoirs, Recollections of My Life; and even science fiction stories, the Vacation Stories.

His legacy today

He called neurons the "butterflies of the soul". More than a century later, his work still underpins research on glia, the synapse, regeneration after spinal cord injury and neurodegenerative diseases. His legacy is preserved at the CSIC's Cajal Institute in Madrid.

His portrait is the cover image of Neurolopedia.
