Carl Wernicke

At just 26 he described an area of the temporal lobe needed to understand language and proposed a model of connections between areas that is still taught.

Who he was

Carl Wernicke was born in 1848 in Tarnowitz, Upper Silesia, then Prussia and now Tarnowskie Góry in Poland. He studied medicine in Breslau (now Wrocław, Poland) and trained in Vienna with the neuroanatomist Theodor Meynert, who inspired the idea that the brain works through connections between areas of the cortex. He worked in Berlin and was professor of neurology and psychiatry in Breslau and Halle. He died in 1905, aged 57, in a cycling accident in the Thuringian Forest.

A different aphasia

In 1874, aged only 26, he published Der aphasische Symptomencomplex ("The aphasia symptom complex"). He described patients with a language disorder very different from that of Broca's patients:

  • They spoke fluently, even excessively, but with wrong words, altered sounds or invented words, to the point of meaningless "jargon".
  • They did not understand what was said to them.
  • They were often unaware of their problem.

At post-mortem the lesion was in the back part of the first left temporal convolution, near the auditory cortex. This is Wernicke's aphasia, and the area is called Wernicke's area. See language.

The first model of language

Wernicke did not just describe another area. He proposed a model of how language works: a posterior area storing the "sound images" of words (comprehension), an anterior area storing the "motor images" (speech, Broca's area), and fibres connecting them.

From his model he predicted a type of aphasia nobody had yet described: if only the connecting fibres were damaged, the patient would understand and speak fluently but make errors and be unable to repeat sentences. This conduction aphasia was later confirmed and linked to the arcuate fasciculus. It was one of the first times a model of the brain predicted a disease. Ludwig Lichtheim extended the model in 1885 (the Wernicke-Lichtheim model), and in the 20th century Norman Geschwind revived it with his idea of disconnection syndromes.

Wernicke's encephalopathy

In 1881 he described three patients (two men with alcoholism and a young woman who had swallowed sulphuric acid and was vomiting constantly) with an acute picture of:

  • Confusion.
  • Paralysis of eye movements.
  • Unsteady gait (ataxia).

He found small haemorrhages around the ventricles. We now know it is caused by a lack of vitamin B1 (thiamine). This is Wernicke's encephalopathy, a medical emergency: if not treated with thiamine in time, it can leave Korsakoff syndrome, a severe memory disorder. Besides alcoholism, it occurs after weight-loss surgery, persistent vomiting in pregnancy or malnutrition. See alcohol, tobacco and drugs.

Other contributions

  • He wrote a three-volume textbook of brain diseases.
  • The typical posture of the arm and leg after a stroke is named Wernicke-Mann.
  • He attempted a classification of psychiatric diseases based on the brain.

Legacy

Wernicke showed that language is not in a single spot but depends on connected areas. This is the basis of neuropsychology and of the modern idea of brain networks. See functional neuroimaging and the connectome. Broca's and Wernicke's aphasias are still the first concepts learned when facing a stroke with a language disorder.
