Otto Loewi

A dream gave him the idea for the experiment that proved neurons communicate through chemical substances. Nobel Prize 1936, he had to hand over the prize money to the Nazis to leave Austria.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Otto Loewi was born in 1873 in Frankfurt am Main, Germany, into a Jewish family of wine merchants. He was more interested in philosophy and art than medicine, but ended up studying medicine in Strasbourg and Munich. He worked in London with the physiologist Ernest Starling, where he met Henry Dale, who would become his close friend. In 1909 he was appointed professor of pharmacology at the University of Graz, in Austria. He died in New York in 1961.

The big question

In the early 20th century there was debate about how nerves communicated with organs: by electricity or by some chemical substance? It was known that stimulating the vagus nerve slowed the heart, but not how. Loewi had thought of the chemical possibility since 1903, but did not know how to prove it.

The dreamt experiment (1921)

As he told it himself, on the night of Easter Sunday 1921 he dreamt of the experiment. He woke up, jotted some notes on a piece of paper and went back to sleep. In the morning he could not make out what he had written. The following night the dream came back at three in the morning. This time he got up and went straight to the laboratory.

The experiment was simple and elegant:

  1. He took two frog hearts, which keep beating outside the body in a salt solution. The first still had its vagus nerve; the second did not.
  2. He stimulated the vagus of the first heart, which beat more slowly.
  3. He collected the fluid bathing that heart and transferred it to the second heart.
  4. The second heart also slowed down, even though nobody had touched its nerves.

The conclusion was clear: the nerve had released a chemical substance that slowed the heart. He called it Vagusstoff, "vagus substance". When he stimulated the accelerator nerve, he found another substance that sped up the heart (Acceleransstoff).

Acetylcholine and noradrenaline

Years later it was shown that Vagusstoff was acetylcholine, a substance Henry Dale had studied. The substance from the accelerator nerve turned out to be noradrenaline. They were the first two neurotransmitters discovered. See synapses and neurotransmitters and autonomic nervous system.

In 1936 Loewi and Dale shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries relating to chemical transmission of nerve impulses".

Fleeing the Nazis

In March 1938, after Nazi Germany annexed Austria, Loewi was arrested for being Jewish, together with two of his sons. To be released and allowed to leave the country, he was forced to transfer his Nobel Prize money, held in a bank in Stockholm, to a Nazi-controlled bank. He emigrated to Brussels, to Oxford and, in 1940, to New York, where he was professor of pharmacology at New York University until his death.

Legacy

Loewi's discovery opened the field of neuropharmacology. Almost all medicines that act on the nervous system do so on chemical transmission: medicines for dementia increase acetylcholine; treatments for myasthenia gravis prevent it from being broken down; botulinum toxin blocks its release; and Parkinson's medicines replace another neurotransmitter, dopamine.

Loewi said the story of his dream showed that it is sometimes better "not to think too much" and to trust intuition, but also that intuition only comes to those who have worked hard on a problem.
