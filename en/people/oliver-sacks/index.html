Oliver Sacks

The writer-neurologist who told his patients' stories with curiosity and humanity. "The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat" and "Awakenings" brought neurology to millions of readers.

Who he was

Oliver Wolf Sacks was born in London on 9 July 1933, into a Jewish family of doctors: his father was a GP and his mother one of the first women surgeons in England. As a child, during the Second World War, he was sent to a boarding school outside London, an experience he found very hard. He loved chemistry, as he told in Uncle Tungsten. He studied medicine at Oxford. In 1960 he moved to the United States, and from 1965 lived in New York, where he worked as a neurologist for fifty years. He died on 30 August 2015.

Awakenings

In 1966 he began working at Beth Abraham Hospital in the Bronx, a home for the chronically ill. There he found some eighty patients who had been motionless for decades, like statues, barely speaking or moving. They were survivors of a mysterious epidemic of encephalitis lethargica (the "sleeping sickness") that had swept the world between 1916 and 1927, leaving them with extreme parkinsonism. See encephalitis.

In 1969 Sacks gave them levodopa, the new Parkinson's medicine. The results were spectacular: many patients "awoke", walking, talking and taking an interest in the world again after forty years. But the effects did not last: involuntary movements, tics and sudden swings appeared and, in many, the benefit faded. See levodopa and Parkinson's medicines.

He told the story in Awakenings (1973), which was made into a film in 1990 with Robin Williams and Robert De Niro.

The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat

In 1985 he published his most famous book, a collection of case histories of patients with extraordinary neurological disorders:

  • Dr P., a musician who could not recognise faces or objects (visual agnosia), and who tried to lift his wife's head thinking it was his hat. See vision.
  • A man who, because of Korsakoff syndrome, lived trapped in 1945, with no memory of anything since. See memory.
  • Patients with Tourette syndrome, with autism or with disturbances in the perception of their own body.

Other books

  • Migraine (1970), his first book. See migraine.
  • An Anthropologist on Mars (1995): with the autistic scientist Temple Grandin and a surgeon with Tourette's.
  • Musicophilia (2007): on music and the brain.
  • Hallucinations (2012) and The Mind's Eye (2010), in which he described his own prosopagnosia (difficulty recognising faces) and the loss of sight in one eye from cancer.
  • On the Move (2015), his autobiography, in which he spoke openly about his homosexuality, his years as a motorcyclist and weightlifter, and his amphetamine addiction as a young man.

A doctor who listened

Sacks was inspired by the "romantic science" of Alexander Luria, with whom he corresponded: telling the illness from the person's experience, without reducing them to a case. He showed that neurological patients are not just a set of symptoms but people who find surprising ways to adapt, thanks to the brain's plasticity.

Some critics accused him of turning patients into characters; he replied that his aim was to show their dignity and humanity.

The end

In 2015 he learned that an eye cancer treated in 2005 had spread to his liver. He wrote a farewell article in the New York Times, "My Own Life", and then other essays collected in Gratitude: "Above all, I have been a sentient being, a thinking animal, on this beautiful planet, and that in itself has been an enormous privilege and adventure."

Legacy

Oliver Sacks brought neurology to the general public as nobody had before, and inspired many neurologists and neuroscientists to choose their careers. His work is a reminder that medicine is about listening to people.
