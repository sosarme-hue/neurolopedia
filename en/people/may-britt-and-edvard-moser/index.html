May-Britt and Edvard Moser

A married couple of Norwegian neuroscientists who discovered "grid cells", the coordinate system of the brain's GPS. Nobel Prize 2014 together with John O'Keefe.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who they are

May-Britt Moser was born in 1963 in Fosnavåg, and Edvard Ingjald Moser in 1962 in Ålesund, two towns on the west coast of Norway. They met at secondary school and studied psychology at the University of Oslo, where they married in 1985. They wanted to understand how the brain produces behaviour, and decided to do it together. They did their doctorates with the neurophysiologist Per Andersen, and then worked in Edinburgh with Richard Morris and in London with John O'Keefe, the discoverer of place cells. In 1996 they settled at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) in Trondheim, where they created the Kavli Institute for Systems Neuroscience.

The question

In 1971 John O'Keefe had discovered place cells in the rat hippocampus: neurons that fire only when the animal is in a particular spot. But how did the hippocampus "know" where the animal was? The Mosers suspected that the information arrived already processed from another area, the entorhinal cortex, the gateway to the hippocampus.

Grid cells (2005)

They recorded the activity of neurons in the entorhinal cortex while rats roamed freely around a large box looking for bits of food. They found something astonishing: each neuron fired at many points in the box, and those points formed a grid of triangles, a regular hexagonal pattern, like the cells of a honeycomb or the coordinates of a map.

They called them grid cells and published the finding in Nature in 2005. Different neurons have grids of different sizes and orientations, smaller in the upper part of the entorhinal cortex and larger in the lower part. Combined, they let the brain calculate position precisely, even in the dark, from the animal's own movement. It is a coordinate system inside the brain.

The brain's GPS

In the following years their team discovered other cells that complete the navigation system:

  • Cells that combine position with head direction (2006).
  • Border cells, which fire next to walls (2008).
  • Speed cells, which encode how fast the animal is moving (2015).
  • Cells that encode position relative to objects.

Together they form what has been called the "brain's GPS". See spatial navigation.

The Nobel Prize

In 2014 they received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, one half to John O'Keefe and the other half to May-Britt and Edvard Moser, "for their discoveries of cells that constitute a positioning system in the brain". They were the fifth married couple to win a Nobel Prize. They separated in 2016, but continued to work at the same institute.

In humans and in disease

Signs of grid cells have also been found in the human brain, in recordings from patients with epilepsy with implanted electrodes and with functional MRI. It has also been shown that the entorhinal cortex encodes not only space but also time and other kinds of information, linking it to memory.

The entorhinal cortex is one of the first areas damaged in Alzheimer's disease, which may explain why getting lost in familiar places is one of its earliest symptoms. Navigation tests are being studied to detect it earlier.

Legacy

The Mosers' work shows that the brain builds abstract representations, such as a map with coordinates, from experience. It is one of the clearest examples of how a complex cognitive function can be explained by the activity of specific neurons. See also Brenda Milner and Eric Kandel, who studied memory from other angles.
