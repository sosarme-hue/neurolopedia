Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley

Two British physiologists who, with the giant axon of the squid and a mechanical calculator, explained how the nerve impulse is generated. Their mathematical model is still valid.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who they were

Alan Lloyd Hodgkin (1914-1998) was born in Banbury, England, into a Quaker family. Andrew Fielding Huxley (1917-2012) was born in London, the grandson of the biologist Thomas Henry Huxley, champion of Darwin's ideas, and half-brother of the writer Aldous Huxley, author of Brave New World. Both studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, where Hodgkin taught Huxley. Both went on to become President of the Royal Society.

The question

By the middle of the 20th century it was known that nerves transmitted electrical impulses, but not how they were generated. What exactly happened in the membrane of a neuron to produce such a fast signal that was always the same?

The squid's giant axon

The problem was technical: axons are so thin that an electrode could not be put inside them. The solution came from the sea. In 1936 the zoologist J. Z. Young had discovered that the squid has a giant axon, up to a millimetre across, which controls the rapid contraction of its mantle to escape. At the marine biology laboratory in Plymouth, Hodgkin and Huxley managed in 1939 to insert a fine electrode into this axon and record the action potential from inside for the first time.

They found that during the impulse the inside of the cell did not just lose its negative charge, but briefly became positive. That contradicted the theory of the time. But then the Second World War broke out: Hodgkin worked on developing radar and Huxley on anti-aircraft gunnery.

The 1952 equations

After the war they resumed the work, together with Bernard Katz. They used a new technique, the voltage clamp, which held the membrane voltage constant and measured the currents flowing across it. In this way they showed that the nerve impulse is due to two successive currents:

  1. A rapid influx of sodium, which makes the inside positive.
  2. A slower outflow of potassium, which restores the negative charge.

In 1952 they published five papers in the Journal of Physiology. In the last one they presented a set of mathematical equations that described the action potential with astonishing accuracy. Huxley solved the equations on a hand-cranked mechanical calculator: working out a single impulse took him about three weeks. Today a computer does it in a fraction of a second. See action potential.

They also predicted that the membrane must contain "gates" that opened and closed depending on the voltage. Decades later these were shown to be real ion channels: proteins that can be seen and studied one by one.

The Nobel Prize

In 1963 Hodgkin and Huxley shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with the Australian John Eccles, who had studied transmission at synapses, "for their discoveries concerning the ionic mechanisms involved in excitation and inhibition in the peripheral and central portions of the nerve cell membrane".

After the Nobel

Huxley went on to study muscle, and in 1954 proposed the sliding filament theory, which explains how a muscle contracts and is still accepted. He was Master of Trinity College, Cambridge. Hodgkin studied the retina and the transmission of visual signals.

Legacy

The Hodgkin-Huxley model is one of the greatest achievements of 20th-century biology and remains the basis of computational neuroscience. It explains how many medicines work: local anaesthetics and many antiseizure medicines block sodium channels, and many diseases, such as some childhood epilepsies or certain types of neuropathic pain, are caused by faults in these channels (channelopathies).
