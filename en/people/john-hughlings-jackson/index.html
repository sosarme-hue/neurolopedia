John Hughlings Jackson

The "father of British neurology" understood epilepsy as a discharge of neurons and deduced, just by watching how seizures spread, that the cerebral cortex holds a map of the body.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

John Hughlings Jackson was born in 1835 in Green Hammerton, Yorkshire, England, the son of a farmer. He studied medicine in York and London. In 1862 he began working at the newly founded National Hospital for the Paralysed and Epileptic in Queen Square, London, which would become one of the most important neurology centres in the world. He worked there for more than forty years. He was a shy, modest man who never wrote a book: his ideas are scattered through hundreds of papers, often hard to read. He died in 1911.

A new definition of epilepsy

In his day, epilepsy was seen mainly as major generalised convulsions, and its origin was sought in the brainstem. Jackson observed his patients with enormous patience, among them, it is believed, his own wife Elizabeth, who had seizures. He saw that many seizures started in one part of the body and spread in an orderly way.

In 1870 he proposed a definition that still holds: an epileptic seizure is an "occasional, sudden, excessive, rapid and local discharge of grey matter". In other words, epilepsy arises in the cerebral cortex, and the type of seizure depends on where the discharge begins.

The Jacksonian march

He described seizures that began, for example, with jerking of the thumb, spread to the hand, then the arm and face, always in the same order. He deduced that the discharge was moving across the motor cortex, where the parts of the body are represented side by side. This is the Jacksonian march. Years later the experiments of Fritsch, Hitzig and David Ferrier, and later the maps of Wilder Penfield, confirmed that "map" of the body on the cortex. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

He also described the "dreamy state" of some seizures: a sense of familiarity or déjà vu, strange smells, memories bursting in. He linked these seizures to the medial temporal lobe, what we now know as temporal lobe epilepsy.

The hierarchy of the nervous system

Influenced by the philosopher Herbert Spencer, Jackson proposed that the nervous system is organised in hierarchical levels: the lowest and oldest (spinal cord, brainstem) control automatic functions; the highest and most recent (cortex) control the most complex and voluntary ones, and also hold back the lower levels.

When disease damages the higher levels (what he called dissolution), two kinds of symptoms appear:

  • Negative: the loss of a function (weakness, aphasia).
  • Positive: the release of the lower levels, which work unchecked (spasticity, exaggerated reflexes, such as the Babinski sign).

This idea of positive and negative symptoms is still used today in neurology and psychiatry.

Other contributions

  • He was one of the first to use the ophthalmoscope systematically to examine the back of the eye in neurological diseases. See neurological examination.
  • He studied aphasias, noting that patients could say words automatically (such as a swear word) even if they could not use them at will.
  • In 1878 he co-founded the journal Brain, which is still one of the most prestigious in neurology.

Legacy

He is regarded as the father of British neurology and one of the great thinkers in the history of neuroscience. His way of reasoning, deducing the site of the lesion from careful observation of the symptoms, is the basis of neurological diagnosis.
