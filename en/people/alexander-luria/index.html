Alexander Luria

Soviet neuropsychologist who studied thousands of soldiers with brain wounds and founded modern neuropsychology. He wrote two case histories that inspired Oliver Sacks.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Alexander Romanovich Luria was born in 1902 in Kazan, Russia, the son of a doctor. He studied social sciences and psychology, and as a young man published work on psychoanalysis, even corresponding with Sigmund Freud. In 1924 he met Lev Vygotsky, a brilliant psychologist who died young, and together they developed a psychology that studied how culture, language and education shape the mind. He later studied medicine. He worked all his life in Moscow, in the Soviet Union. He died in 1977.

Mind and culture

In 1931-1932 he led expeditions to Uzbekistan, in Central Asia, where the rural population was learning to read and write. He found that people who had not been to school reasoned in a more practical, concrete way, while those who had learned to read were better at solving abstract problems. It was one of the first studies to show that education changes the way we think, which relates to today's idea of cognitive reserve.

Soldiers with wounded brains

During the Second World War, Luria ran a rehabilitation hospital in the Urals for soldiers with gunshot wounds to the head. He studied hundreds of patients with injuries to different parts of the brain and designed methods to help them recover language, reading, writing and the ability to plan. From that experience came his book Traumatic Aphasia (1947) and, above all, a new discipline: neuropsychology.

The three units of the brain

Luria proposed that complex mental functions are not located in a single spot in the brain, but depend on functional systems in which several areas work together. He described three large functional units:

Unit Areas Function
First Brainstem and deep structures Maintaining arousal and alertness
Second Posterior lobes: occipital, temporal, parietal Receiving, analysing and storing information from the senses
Third Frontal lobes Programming, regulating and checking behaviour

This idea greatly influenced our understanding of the frontal lobe and executive functions. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

He also designed many examination tests still in use, such as the "fist, edge, palm" sequence, in which the patient has to repeat three hand positions: people with frontal lesions cannot keep the sequence going. See cognitive tests. His book Higher Cortical Functions in Man (1962) was a reference text all over the world.

"Romantic science"

Luria wrote two very special books, each telling the story of a single person with the depth of a novel:

  • The Mind of a Mnemonist (1968): about Solomon Shereshevsky, a journalist with a practically limitless memory who could not forget, partly because of his synaesthesia (he saw colours and tastes in sounds and words). See memory.
  • The Man with a Shattered World (1971): about Lev Zasetsky, a soldier wounded in the head in 1943 who lost the ability to understand space, read and remember, and who for 25 years kept a diary to try to rebuild his life.

Luria called this approach "romantic science": studying the disease without losing sight of the person. It directly inspired Oliver Sacks, with whom he corresponded.

Legacy

Luria is considered the father of neuropsychology. His ideas about functional systems anticipated today's study of brain networks, and his methods are the basis of the cognitive rehabilitation that patients receive today after a stroke or a head injury. See neurorehabilitation.
