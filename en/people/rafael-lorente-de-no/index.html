Rafael Lorente de Nó

Cajal's youngest pupil described the organisation of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus into columns and circuits, and was a pioneer of the physiology of neural circuits.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Rafael Lorente de Nó was born in Zaragoza (Spain) in 1902. He studied medicine in Madrid and, while still a student, in 1917, joined the laboratory of Santiago Ramón y Cajal, whose last great pupil he was. Cajal admired him greatly and asked him to study the cerebral cortex, one of the challenges he himself had not completed. He died in Tucson (Arizona, United States) in 1990.

The cortex as a network of circuits

Using Golgi's method, Lorente de Nó studied the cerebral cortex of the mouse, especially the auditory cortex and the hippocampus. He described in enormous detail how neurons connect between the different layers of the cortex. He arrived at a fundamental idea: the cortex is organised into vertical chains of neurons, repeated elementary units that process information together. It is a direct forerunner of the cortical columns later described by Vernon Mountcastle, and by Hubel and Wiesel. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

The regions of the hippocampus

In 1934 he proposed dividing the hippocampus ("Ammon's horn" or cornu Ammonis) into several regions, which he called CA1, CA2, CA3 and CA4, according to the type of neurons and their connections. This naming is still used worldwide today, in anatomy, in memory research and in the study of epilepsy and Alzheimer's. The CA1 region, for example, is especially vulnerable to lack of oxygen.

Circuits that feed back

Lorente de Nó observed that many neurons form closed loops: one neuron excites another which, through others, ends up exciting the first one again. He proposed that these reverberating circuits allowed activity to persist for a while after a stimulus. The idea influenced the psychologist Donald Hebb's theory of memory, and the first artificial neural network models of Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts in the 1940s. In other words, it lies at the distant origin of modern artificial intelligence.

The ear and balance

Another important part of his work was the study of the vestibular system, the balance system, and of the reflexes that keep our gaze steady when we move our head (the vestibulo-ocular reflex). He worked in Sweden with the Nobel laureate Robert Bárány and in Santander, at the Casa de Salud Valdecilla, where he ran an ear, nose and throat laboratory. See vertigo.

In the United States

In 1931, given the lack of resources in Spain, he emigrated to the United States. He worked at the Central Institute for the Deaf in St Louis and, from 1936, at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, where he stayed for more than thirty-five years. There he devoted himself to nerve physiology, publishing A Study of Nerve Physiology (1947), a monumental study of how the nerve impulse is produced and conducted. See action potential. He was elected a member of the US National Academy of Sciences in 1950.

Legacy

Lorente de Nó was one of the most important Spanish scientists of the 20th century, although he is less known in Spain than abroad. He joined Cajal's anatomy with modern physiology and proposed that brain function depends on its circuits. His idea of the cortex as a set of repeated units and networks that feed back is still present in neuroscience and in the study of the connectome.
