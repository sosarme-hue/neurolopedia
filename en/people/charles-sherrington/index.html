Charles Sherrington

The British physiologist who coined the word "synapse" and explained how the nervous system integrates excitation and inhibition to produce coordinated movements. Nobel Prize 1932.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Charles Scott Sherrington was born in 1857 in Islington, London. He studied medicine in London and at the University of Cambridge, and furthered his training in Germany, where he worked in Robert Koch's laboratory. He was professor of physiology in Liverpool and, from 1913, in Oxford. He was President of the Royal Society from 1920 to 1925. He was also a poet and philosopher. He died in 1952, aged 94.

The word "synapse"

In 1897, while writing a chapter for a physiology textbook, Sherrington needed a name for the point of contact between two neurons, which Cajal had shown were not fused together. With the help of a classical Greek scholar, he proposed "synapse", from the Greek synapsis, "joining" or "clasp". The word has been used all over the world ever since. See synapses and neurotransmitters.

From the study of reflexes alone, Sherrington deduced many of the properties of the synapse before it could be seen: that it introduced a delay in the signal, that transmission went in one direction only and that a neuron could add up signals from many others.

The study of reflexes

Sherrington studied the spinal cord reflexes of animals with enormous precision. His main discoveries:

  • Reciprocal innervation: when a muscle contracts, the opposing muscle automatically relaxes. For example, when you bend the knee, the muscles that straighten it relax. For this, inhibition must exist: neurons are not only excited, they are also held back.
  • Integration: the nervous system combines excitatory and inhibitory signals to produce coordinated responses. This is the central idea of his great book The Integrative Action of the Nervous System (1906), one of the most influential texts in neurophysiology.
  • Decerebrate rigidity: when the brainstem is separated from the higher centres, the antigravity muscles contract to the maximum. See brainstem.
  • Dermatomes: he mapped the areas of skin that correspond to each nerve root. See spinal cord.
  • The motor unit: a motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it controls.

Words he invented

Besides synapse, he coined terms that are now basic: proprioception (the sense of the body's position), nociceptor (the receptor for pain) and interoception.

The Nobel Prize

In 1932 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Edgar Adrian of Cambridge, "for their discoveries regarding the functions of neurons". Adrian had been the first to record the electrical impulses of individual neurons.

Teacher and thinker

His students included Wilder Penfield, the Canadian neurosurgeon; John Eccles, who would win the Nobel Prize for his studies of the synapse; and Howard Florey, who developed penicillin.

In his book Man on His Nature (1940) he described the waking brain as an "enchanted loom" where millions of flashing shuttles weave "a dissolving pattern, always a meaningful pattern though never an abiding one". It is one of the most beautiful metaphors about the brain.

Legacy

Sherrington turned neurophysiology into an experimental science and showed that the nervous system works as an integrated whole. The reflexes he studied are tested today in every neurological examination, and his work underpins the treatment of spasticity.
