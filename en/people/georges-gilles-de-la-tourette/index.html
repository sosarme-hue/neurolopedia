Georges Gilles de la Tourette

A pupil of Charcot who in 1885 described the tic syndrome that bears his name. His life, which included being shot, reads like a novel.

Who he was

Georges Albert Édouard Brutus Gilles de la Tourette was born in 1857 in Saint-Gervais-les-Trois-Clochers, near Loudun, in west-central France. He studied medicine in Poitiers and Paris, where he became a student and secretary of Jean-Martin Charcot at the Salpêtrière. He was known as brilliant, energetic, impulsive and difficult. He died in 1904, aged only 46.

The tic disease (1885)

In 1885, aged 28, he published a study of "a nervous condition characterised by motor incoordination accompanied by echolalia and coprolalia". He described nine patients with:

  • Motor tics: sudden, repeated, purposeless movements that began in childhood.
  • Vocal tics: noises, grunts or words.
  • Echolalia: repeating what others say.
  • Coprolalia: involuntarily saying rude or obscene words.

One of his cases was the Marquise de Dampierre, a Parisian aristocrat already described by the doctor Jean Itard in 1825, whose outbursts in the salons of Paris were famous. Charcot proposed naming the disease after his student: Gilles de la Tourette syndrome. See Tourette syndrome.

What we know today

Gilles de la Tourette thought the disease was hereditary (partly right) and incurable, tending to get worse (wrong: it usually improves in adulthood). Today we know that:

  • It affects almost 1 in 100 children, mostly boys, with tics of very variable intensity.
  • Coprolalia is the best-known feature, but it occurs in only 1 in 10 patients.
  • It is caused by a disturbance of the circuits between the cortex and the basal ganglia.
  • It is often accompanied by ADHD and obsessive-compulsive symptoms.
  • Tics improve from adolescence onwards in most people, and are treated with behavioural therapy, medicines, botulinum toxin and, in severe cases, deep brain stimulation.

Other work

  • He was one of the leading students of hysteria and hypnosis in Charcot's school, and wrote a treatise on hysteria.
  • He studied gait with an original method: he had patients walk on paper with their feet stained with pigment, and measured the length and angle of their steps.
  • He was chief medical officer of the 1900 Paris World Fair.

A life like a novel

In 1893 a former patient, Rose Kamper, shot him in the head in his consulting room. She claimed she had been hypnotised against her will. The bullet wounded him in the neck and Gilles de la Tourette survived. That same year his teacher Charcot and his young son died.

Soon afterwards he began to show changes in behaviour and mood, and developed general paresis, the brain form of syphilis, a common disease before antibiotics. He was admitted to a psychiatric asylum in Lausanne (Switzerland), where he died in 1904.

Legacy

His name has become one of the best known in neurology, often linked to stereotypes. Thanks to research and patient associations, we now know that people with Tourette's have normal intelligence and can lead full lives. Oliver Sacks described the case of a surgeon with Tourette's in An Anthropologist on Mars.
