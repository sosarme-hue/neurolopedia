Thomas Willis

The Oxford physician who coined the word "neurology", described the ring of arteries at the base of the brain and placed mental functions in the substance of the brain rather than in the ventricles.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Thomas Willis was born in 1621 in Great Bedwyn, in southern England, into a farming family. He studied at the University of Oxford in the middle of the English Civil War, in which he fought on the King's side. During Cromwell's republic he practised as a physician in Oxford and belonged to a group of scientists who met to carry out experiments: Robert Boyle, Robert Hooke, Christopher Wren and Richard Lower. That group gave rise to the Royal Society in 1660, of which Willis was a founding Fellow.

With the restoration of the monarchy in 1660 he was appointed professor of natural philosophy at Oxford. In 1667 he moved to London, where he became one of the most famous and wealthy physicians of his day. He died in 1675 and is buried in Westminster Abbey.

Cerebri anatome (1664)

His most important work, Cerebri anatome ("Anatomy of the brain"), published in 1664, was the most complete study of the brain up to that time. It introduced several new ideas:

  • He removed the whole brain from the skull before studying it, rather than cutting it in place, and used new techniques such as injecting ink into the vessels to follow their course.
  • The illustrations were made by the architect Christopher Wren, who would later build St Paul's Cathedral in London.
  • He placed mental functions in brain tissue, not in the ventricles as had been believed since Galen: memory and will in the cortex, sensation in the corpus striatum, vital functions in the cerebellum.
  • He proposed a classification of the cranial nerves and described the accessory nerve, still sometimes called "Willis's nerve".
  • He was the first to use the word "neurology", meaning the doctrine of the nerves.

The circle of Willis

His name is known to every medical student through the circle of Willis: the ring of arteries at the base of the brain that joins the carotid and vertebral systems. He was not the first to see it, but he was the first to describe its function: he showed that if one artery became blocked, blood could arrive through the others. At a post-mortem he observed that a patient with a blocked carotid had had no symptoms because the other arteries had compensated. See cerebral circulation.

An observant clinician

Willis was also a great practical physician:

  • He accurately described a muscle weakness that worsened through the day, which many regard as the first description of myasthenia gravis.
  • He described the discomfort in the legs that prevented sleep, now known as restless legs syndrome.
  • He noticed that the urine of diabetics was sweet "as honey", the origin of the term diabetes mellitus.
  • He wrote about epilepsy, headaches and hysteria, which he attributed to the brain and not the womb.

Legacy

Willis is considered one of the founders of neurology. He paved the way for the idea that brain functions reside in its tissue, which centuries later Broca and Hughlings Jackson would develop. Among his students was the philosopher John Locke.
