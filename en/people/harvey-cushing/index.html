Harvey Cushing

The father of modern neurosurgery. He cut the death rate of brain operations from over 30% to under 10%, operated on more than 2,000 tumours and described the disease that bears his name.

Who he was

Harvey Williams Cushing was born in 1869 in Cleveland, Ohio (United States), the youngest of ten children in a family of doctors. He studied at Yale and at Harvard Medical School, and trained as a surgeon at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore under William Halsted, a pioneer of modern surgery. He later worked in Boston and at Yale. He died in 1939.

An almost impossible surgery

At the end of the 19th century, brain surgery was a desperate business: most patients died from bleeding, infection or rising pressure inside the skull. Cushing changed that with a slow, meticulous technique that was gentle with tissues. At his best he reduced the death rate of his brain tumour operations to under 10%, something extraordinary for his time.

His innovations:

  • Controlling bleeding: he introduced silver clips to close vessels and, in 1926, with the physicist William Bovie, the electrosurgical knife to coagulate.
  • Safer anaesthesia: he was one of the first to measure blood pressure and record the patient's vital signs during surgery, on anaesthesia charts.
  • X-rays to plan operations.
  • Detailed records of every case, with drawings, photographs and follow-up.

Tumours and glands

He operated on more than 2,000 brain tumours and studied all of them under the microscope:

  • With Percival Bailey he proposed in 1926 a classification of gliomas by cell type, the basis of today's. See brain tumours.
  • He proposed the term meningioma for tumours of the meninges. See meninges.
  • He studied the pituitary gland and in 1932 described Cushing's disease: obesity of the trunk, a round face, stretch marks, high blood pressure and weakness, caused by a small pituitary tumour that produces too much of the hormone that stimulates cortisol.
  • He described the Cushing reflex: when pressure inside the skull rises sharply, blood pressure goes up and the pulse slows. It is a warning sign of brain herniation.

War and writing

During the First World War he worked as a surgeon in military hospitals in France, where he treated hundreds of gunshot wounds to the head and improved survival. He was also a fine writer: in 1926 he won the Pulitzer Prize for his biography of his teacher and friend, the physician William Osler.

His collection

Cushing kept the brains, slides and photographs of his patients in a brain tumour registry, one of the largest collections of its kind. It is now held at the Cushing Center at Yale University.

Legacy

Cushing is considered the father of modern neurosurgery. He trained a generation of neurosurgeons who spread it around the world. Thanks to his work and that of his successors, neurosurgery can now remove tumours, treat aneurysms or implant deep brain stimulation electrodes at much lower risk. One of his students was Wilder Penfield.
