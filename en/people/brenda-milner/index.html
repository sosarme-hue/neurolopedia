Brenda Milner

The neuropsychologist who, by studying patient H. M., showed that the hippocampus is essential for forming memories and that there are different memory systems. She kept working past the age of one hundred.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Her life

Brenda Milner (née Brenda Langford) was born in Manchester (England) in 1918. She studied experimental psychology at the University of Cambridge. In 1944 she moved to Montreal (Canada) with her husband, the engineer and later neuroscientist Peter Milner. She did her doctorate at McGill University with the psychologist Donald Hebb, who had proposed that learning is based on strengthening the connections between neurons. In 1950 she began working at the Montreal Neurological Institute, with Wilder Penfield. She is considered the founder of modern neuropsychology. She turned 100 in 2018.

Two unexpected patients

Penfield operated on patients with epilepsy by removing the area of the temporal lobe where seizures started. Two of them, operated on one side only, unexpectedly lost the ability to form new memories. Milner studied them and proposed that the hippocampus on the other side had already been damaged before surgery, so that they had been left without a working one. Years later, the post-mortem of one of them proved her right.

H. M.: the patient who could not remember

In 1953 the neurosurgeon William Scoville had operated in Hartford (United States) on a 27-year-old man, Henry Molaison, known for decades only as H. M., to treat very severe epilepsy. He removed the inner part of both temporal lobes, including both hippocampi. The seizures improved, but H. M. was left with profound amnesia: he could not remember anything new for more than a few seconds or minutes. He forgot people he had just met and read the same magazine over and over.

Milner travelled to Hartford to study him, and in 1957 published with Scoville the paper that showed the hippocampus is essential for forming new memories. See hippocampus and limbic system.

The mirror experiment

In 1962 she did an experiment that changed the idea of memory. She asked H. M. to draw a star while watching his hand only in a mirror, a difficult task. Over three days H. M. got better, like anyone else. But each day he said he had never done the task before.

The conclusion was that there is not just one memory: H. M. had lost memory for facts and experiences (declarative memory), but kept memory for skills (procedural memory), which depends on other brain areas. See memory.

Beyond H. M.

  • Frontal lobes: she showed that patients with frontal lesions have difficulty switching strategy when the rules of a task change (in the Wisconsin card sorting test), one of the executive functions.
  • Specialisation of the hemispheres: with the Wada test, which puts one hemisphere to sleep for a few minutes with an anaesthetic, she helped find out where each patient's language was before surgery. She showed that the left temporal lobe is key for verbal memory and the right for visual and spatial memory. See corpus callosum and hemispheres.

Honours

She received many awards, including the Balzan Prize (2009), the Gruber Prize in Neuroscience (2010) and the Kavli Prize in Neuroscience (2014), shared with John O'Keefe and Marcus Raichle. She kept doing research and going to the institute well past 90. Curiosity was her great driving force: she said she had always been more interested in "how" than "why".

H. M. afterwards

Henry Molaison died in 2008. His brain was cut into 2,401 thin slices and digitised so that scientists all over the world can study it. He is probably the most studied patient in the history of neuroscience. His case also influenced epilepsy surgery: since then both hippocampi are never removed.
