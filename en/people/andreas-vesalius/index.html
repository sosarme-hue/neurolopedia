Andreas Vesalius

The Flemish anatomist who dared to correct Galen by dissecting human bodies. His "Fabrica" (1543) includes some of the first accurate images of the brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Andreas Vesalius (Andries van Wesel in Dutch) was born in Brussels in 1514, into a family of physicians and apothecaries at the imperial court. He studied in Leuven and in Paris, where he learned anatomy from professors loyal to Galen who barely touched the bodies: they read the text from a chair while a barber did the dissecting. Vesalius, by contrast, wanted to see for himself, and even searched for bones in cemeteries and at the gallows outside the city.

In 1537 he took his doctorate at the University of Padua, and the next day he was appointed professor of surgery and anatomy, aged only 23.

Dissecting with his own hands

Vesalius changed the way anatomy was taught: he came down from the chair and dissected himself in front of the students. He soon found that many of Galen's descriptions did not match the human body but those of animals. For example, the rete mirabilis at the base of the brain did not exist in humans, and the breastbone and jaw were different from what was taught.

The Fabrica (1543)

In 1543, aged 28, he published in Basel De humani corporis fabrica ("On the fabric of the human body"), in seven books, with more than 200 woodcuts of unprecedented quality and beauty, attributed to Titian's workshop in Venice. It is one of the most important books in the history of science. In the same year Copernicus published his work on the motion of the Earth: 1543 is often seen as the start of the scientific revolution.

The seventh book is devoted to the brain. For the first time, the cortex, the ventricles, the corpus callosum, the cerebellum and the brainstem were shown accurately in successive sections. Vesalius was sceptical of the idea that mental functions resided in the ventricles, because animals had ventricles similar to humans.

Life in Spain

The Fabrica brought him fame, but also fierce criticism from the Galenists, among them his former Paris teacher Jacobus Sylvius. Vesalius left the university and became court physician to the Emperor Charles V and later to his son Philip II of Spain, so he lived in Brussels and in Madrid.

In 1562 he helped treat Prince Don Carlos, Philip II's son, who suffered a serious head injury after falling down a staircase in Alcalá de Henares. Together with other court physicians, a trepanation was performed and the prince survived.

In 1564 he set out on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem. On the way back he fell ill and died on the Greek island of Zakynthos, aged 50.

Legacy

Vesalius is considered the father of modern anatomy. His message was revolutionary: authority lies in direct observation of the body, not in ancient books. He opened the way for anatomists such as Thomas Willis, who a century later would describe the brain in even greater detail.
