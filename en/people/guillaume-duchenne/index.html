Guillaume Duchenne

A doctor with no hospital post who toured the wards of Paris with his electrical apparatus. A pioneer of muscle electrophysiology, medical photography and the description of muscular dystrophy.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Guillaume-Benjamin-Amand Duchenne was born in 1806 in Boulogne-sur-Mer, in northern France, the son of a sea captain. That is why he is known as Duchenne de Boulogne. He studied medicine in Paris and practised in his home town. After the death of his first wife, he moved to Paris in 1842.

There he never held an official post at a hospital or university. He went round the Paris hospitals, such as the Salpêtrière and the Charité, looking for interesting patients, whom he examined patiently and followed for years. Some doctors saw him as an intruder, but Charcot called him his "master" in neurology. He died in 1875.

Electricity as a tool

Duchenne was a pioneer of electrophysiology. He developed "localised electrisation": he applied electric currents to the skin with moist electrodes so that he could stimulate a single muscle without damaging the skin. In this way he studied which movement each muscle of the body produced and how it changed in disease. He published his results in De l'électrisation localisée (1855) and Physiologie des mouvements (1867), a reference work for decades. He was also one of the first to use electricity as a treatment.

The physiognomy of emotions

In Mécanisme de la physionomie humaine (1862) he electrically stimulated the facial muscles of an old man with little feeling in his face and photographed the expressions produced. It was one of the first uses of photography in medicine.

He discovered that a genuine smile does not just lift the corners of the mouth but also contracts the muscle around the eyes (the orbicularis oculi), creating "crow's feet". That muscle is hard to contract at will, which is why forced smiles do not engage it. Today it is called the "Duchenne smile". Charles Darwin used his photographs in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872). See emotions.

Diseases he described

Disease Contribution
Duchenne muscular dystrophy He described in detail boys with progressive weakness and apparently large calves ("pseudohypertrophic paralysis", 1861-1868). See muscular dystrophies
Tabes dorsalis "Progressive locomotor ataxia" (1858), a complication of syphilis affecting the spinal cord
Progressive bulbar palsy Weakness of the tongue, lips and throat, now part of ALS
Brachial plexus palsy at birth Duchenne-Erb palsy
Polio, lead poisoning Studies of atrophic paralyses

The muscle biopsy

To study his patients' muscle during life, he designed an instrument, the "histological harpoon" (emporte-pièce), with which he removed small pieces of muscle through the skin. He was one of the inventors of the biopsy in a living patient, a technique still used today.

Legacy

Duchenne is one of the founders of clinical neurology and neurophysiology. His method (careful observation, following patients for years and using the technology available) is the same one followed by modern electromyography and research into neuromuscular disease. Duchenne dystrophy, which affects one in 3,500-5,000 boys, now has gene and RNA therapy treatments.
