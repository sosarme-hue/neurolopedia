Joseph Babinski

A pupil of Charcot of Polish origin who in 1896 described the big-toe reflex that every doctor still uses to detect damage to the brain or spinal cord.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Joseph Babinski was born in Paris in 1857, the son of a Polish family who had fled their country after the failed 1848 uprising against Russia. He studied medicine in Paris and was one of Jean-Martin Charcot's favourite students at the Salpêtrière. In 1892 he failed the examination to become a university professor (the agrégation), reportedly because of rivalries between professors after Charcot's death. He never held a chair. Instead, for more than thirty years he was head of department at the Pitié hospital, where he devoted himself entirely to examining his patients. He died in 1932.

The Babinski sign (1896)

On 22 February 1896, Babinski presented to the Society of Biology in Paris a note of just 26 lines with a simple finding: in some patients with paralysis of one side of the body, when the sole of the foot was stroked, the big toe, instead of curling downwards as is normal, extended upwards. He later added that the other toes could fan out.

This "toe phenomenon", now the Babinski sign, indicates damage to the pyramidal tract, the pathway that carries movement commands from the cortex to the spinal cord. It can be caused by a stroke, multiple sclerosis, a tumour, a spinal cord injury or ALS.

It is probably the most famous sign in neurology. It is tested with a blunt object, from the heel towards the toes along the outer edge of the sole. In babies it is normal for it to appear, because the pyramidal tract has not finished myelinating; it disappears at around one or two years of age, when they start to walk. See neurological examination.

Telling organic from functional

Babinski's aim was practical: at the Salpêtrière of his time, many patients had "hysterical" paralyses, and it was hard to distinguish them from those caused by a real lesion. The Babinski sign cannot be imitated at will, so its presence proved there was an organic lesion.

From then on, Babinski moved away from his teacher's ideas. He argued that many symptoms of hysteria were the product of suggestion and could be cured by persuasion, and proposed calling it "pithiatism". It was an open criticism of the spectacle of Charcot's lectures. Today these conditions are called functional neurological disorders.

Other contributions

  • He described anosognosia (1914): patients with left-sided paralysis after a right-hemisphere stroke who are unaware of their paralysis. See corpus callosum and hemispheres.
  • He studied the signs of the cerebellum: asynergia and adiadochokinesia (clumsiness in rapid alternating movements).
  • With Alfred Fröhlich he described a syndrome of obesity and lack of sexual development caused by pituitary tumours, and with Jean Nageotte a syndrome caused by lesions of the medulla.
  • He promoted neurosurgery in France: he accurately diagnosed spinal cord tumours that his colleague Thierry de Martel successfully removed.

A curiosity

His brother Henri Babinski, a mining engineer, was a famous gastronome who, under the pen name Ali-Bab, wrote Gastronomie pratique, one of the great French cookbooks. The two brothers, both unmarried, lived together all their lives.

Legacy

Babinski embodies examination-based neurology: with a hammer, a pin and careful observation, a lesion could be located before X-rays or MRI existed. His sign is tested every day in every hospital in the world.
