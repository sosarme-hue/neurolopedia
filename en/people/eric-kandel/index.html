Eric Kandel

He fled Vienna as a child in 1939 and decades later discovered, in a sea slug, the molecular changes in synapses that allow us to learn and remember. Nobel Prize 2000.

His life

Eric Richard Kandel was born in Vienna on 7 November 1929, into a Jewish family who owned a toy shop. In November 1938, during Kristallnacht, the Nazis raided his home. He was nine, and those memories, still vivid decades later, would lead him to wonder how the brain stores memory. In 1939 the family emigrated to New York.

He studied history and literature at Harvard, where he wrote his thesis on the attitude of German writers towards Nazism. He wanted to be a psychoanalyst, and studied medicine with that aim. But during a spell at the National Institutes of Health he discovered laboratory research and decided to look for the biological basis of the mind. He has spent almost his whole career at Columbia University in New York.

A sea snail

He began by studying the hippocampus of mammals, but realised it was too complex to study learning cell by cell. In the early 1960s he made a risky decision: to study memory in a simple animal, Aplysia californica, a large sea slug. Its nervous system has only about 20,000 neurons, many of them giant, visible to the naked eye and always in the same place, so they can be identified and studied one by one.

Aplysia has a defensive reflex: if its siphon is touched, it withdraws its gill. Kandel studied how this reflex changes with experience:

  • Habituation: if it is touched many times with no danger, the reflex weakens.
  • Sensitisation: after an unpleasant stimulus to the tail, the reflex becomes stronger.
  • Conditioning: the animal learns to associate two stimuli.

How a memory is stored

Kandel and his team discovered that learning changes the strength of synapses, as Cajal had suspected:

Type of memory What happens in the neurons
Short-term (minutes) Substances such as serotonin are released and messengers inside the neuron (cyclic AMP, protein kinase A) are activated, increasing neurotransmitter release. No new proteins are needed
Long-term (days, weeks) The signals reach the neuron's nucleus and switch on genes (through a protein called CREB). New proteins are made and new synapses grow

In other words, lasting memories change the structure of the brain. He later showed that the same mechanisms work in the hippocampus of mice. See memory and brain plasticity.

The Nobel Prize

In 2000 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with the Swede Arvid Carlsson, who had discovered that dopamine is a neurotransmitter and that its lack causes Parkinson's, and the American Paul Greengard, who had studied how neurotransmitters act inside neurons.

Communicator and humanist

Kandel is co-author of Principles of Neural Science, the most widely used neuroscience textbook in the world. He has written books for the general public:

  • In Search of Memory (2006), his autobiography and the story of the science of memory.
  • The Age of Insight (2012), on art, mind and brain in Vienna around 1900, with Klimt, Kokoschka, Schiele and Freud.
  • The Disordered Mind (2018), on what brain disorders teach us about ourselves.

He argues that biology and psychoanalysis, science and the humanities, must talk to each other to understand the mind.

Legacy

Kandel showed that memory has a molecular basis and can be studied with the tools of biology. His work underpins research on memory disorders, such as Alzheimer's and age-related decline, and on how experience, including psychotherapy, changes the brain.
