James Parkinson

A London neighbourhood doctor, also a geologist and political activist, who in 1817 precisely described the "shaking palsy" from six people, some of them observed in the street.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

James Parkinson was born on 11 April 1755 in Shoreditch, then a district on the edge of London. His father was a surgeon-apothecary, a mix of family doctor and pharmacist, and he followed the same trade in the same house, in Hoxton Square, all his life. He was a man of very wide interests: doctor, political activist, popular science writer and palaeontologist. He died in 1824. No portrait of him survives.

An Essay on the Shaking Palsy (1817)

In 1817, aged 62, he published a 66-page pamphlet: An Essay on the Shaking Palsy. It described six people: some were his patients, but others he had simply observed in the street, walking around his part of London.

He defined the disease as: "Involuntary tremulous motion, with lessened muscular power, in parts not in action and even when supported; with a propensity to bend the trunk forwards, and to pass from a walking to a running pace: the senses and intellects being uninjured."

He described with great precision:

  • Tremor at rest, which lessens when the hand moves.
  • The stooped forward posture.
  • Festinating gait: short steps that speed up, as if the patient were chasing their centre of gravity.
  • Difficulty writing, constipation, problems swallowing and drooling, and sleep disturbances.

He was wrong on one point: he thought the intellect remained intact, whereas we now know that many patients develop cognitive problems over the years. Nor did he highlight rigidity, which Charcot would describe later.

The name

The essay attracted little attention for decades. It was Jean-Martin Charcot, in Paris, who around 1870 proposed calling it Parkinson's disease in his honour, pointing out that patients did not always have tremor or weakness, so "shaking palsy" was not a good name. See Parkinson's disease.

A committed man

  • Politics: he was a radical, a supporter of universal suffrage and workers' rights, and wrote pamphlets under the pseudonym "Old Hubert". In 1794 he was questioned before the Privy Council about a possible link to the "Pop Gun Plot", an alleged conspiracy to kill King George III; he was not charged.
  • Public health: he wrote medical books for the public and campaigned for the protection of people confined to asylums and for health education.
  • Palaeontology: he wrote Organic Remains of a Former World (1804-1811), one of the first works on fossils in English, and was a founding member of the Geological Society of London in 1807. A fossil ammonite bears his name.
  • With his son, he published in 1812 one of the first descriptions in English of a perforated appendicitis.

Legacy

His essay is a model of clinical observation: describing carefully what is seen, without further tests. More than two centuries later, the diagnosis of Parkinson's is still based mainly on the examination. In his honour, World Parkinson's Day is held every 11 April, his birthday, and its symbol is a red tulip. Today the disease affects more than 10 million people worldwide, and treatment with levodopa arrived in the 1960s.
