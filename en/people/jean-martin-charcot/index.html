Jean-Martin Charcot

The "Napoleon of the neuroses" turned the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris into the cradle of modern neurology. He described multiple sclerosis, ALS and the joint disease that bears his name.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Jean-Martin Charcot was born in Paris in 1825, the son of a carriage maker. He was a brilliant student with a great talent for drawing, which he used all his life to record what he saw in his patients. In 1862 he was appointed physician at the Salpêtrière, a huge asylum housing thousands of poor, elderly and chronically ill women. What others saw as a place of oblivion, he saw as a "living pathological museum". In 1882 the world's first chair of diseases of the nervous system was created for him. He died in 1893.

The anatomo-clinical method

Charcot followed his patients for years, described their symptoms in detail and, when they died, studied their brain and spinal cord at post-mortem. In this way he linked each symptom to a lesion. This anatomo-clinical method allowed him to separate and describe many diseases that had previously been confused.

Diseases he described or named

Disease Contribution
Multiple sclerosis Described it as a separate disease (1868), with its plaques at post-mortem and "Charcot's triad": nystagmus, intention tremor and scanning speech
ALS Described it between 1869 and 1874; in France it is still called "Charcot's disease"
Charcot-Marie-Tooth Hereditary neuropathy (1886), with his student Pierre Marie and, independently, Howard Tooth. See peripheral neuropathy
Charcot joint Destruction of joints due to loss of sensation, in syphilis and diabetes
Parkinson's Completed James Parkinson's description, added rigidity and proposed the name "Parkinson's disease"
Charcot-Bouchard aneurysms Tiny dilatations of arteries in brain haemorrhage

Hysteria and the Tuesday lessons

In the last part of his career Charcot devoted himself to hysteria, a disorder with neurological symptoms (paralysis, seizures, blindness) without any visible lesion. He argued that it was a real disease of the nervous system, not deception nor something peculiar to women, and described cases in men.

His Tuesday lessons, in which he examined patients in public, sometimes under hypnosis, drew doctors, writers and the curious from all over Europe. André Brouillet's famous painting A Clinical Lesson at the Salpêtrière (1887) shows him presenting a patient, Blanche Wittmann. Over time it became clear that many of those attacks were shaped by suggestion and the atmosphere of the ward. His own student Babinski later criticised him. Today these conditions are called functional neurological disorders and are considered a real problem with how the brain works.

His students

Charcot created the most influential neurological school of his time. His students included Joseph Babinski, Georges Gilles de la Tourette, Pierre Marie and Pierre Janet. In 1885-1886 a young Viennese doctor, Sigmund Freud, spent several months with him, and his interest in hysteria influenced the birth of psychoanalysis. Spanish doctors such as Luis Simarro also visited his department.

Legacy

Charcot is considered the father of modern neurology. He turned neurology into a specialty, with a method based on the examination and correlation with lesions. His son, Jean-Baptiste Charcot, was a famous Antarctic explorer.
