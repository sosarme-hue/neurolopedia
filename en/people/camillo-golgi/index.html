Camillo Golgi

In a hospital kitchen he invented the "black reaction", which at last made whole neurons visible. He shared the Nobel Prize with Cajal, even though he defended the opposite theory.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Camillo Golgi was born in 1843 in Corteno, a village in the Lombard Alps (Italy) now called Corteno Golgi in his honour. He studied medicine at the University of Pavia, where he became interested in histology, the microscopic study of tissues. After graduating, for financial reasons he accepted a post as chief physician at a hospital for the chronically ill in Abbiategrasso, near Milan. He later returned to Pavia, where he became professor, rector and a senator of the Kingdom of Italy. He died in 1926.

The "black reaction"

In 1873, in a small hospital kitchen in Abbiategrasso that he had turned into a laboratory lit by candles, Golgi discovered a new staining method. He hardened pieces of brain with potassium dichromate and then soaked them in silver nitrate. A black precipitate of silver chromate formed in the tissue: the reazione nera, the "black reaction".

The method had a surprising property that nobody can fully explain: it stains only a small percentage of neurons (about 1-5%), but stains them completely, with the cell body, all the dendrites and the axon, black against a yellow background. For the first time whole neurons could be seen, like isolated trees in a forest.

With his method Golgi described:

  • That dendrites end freely and are related to blood vessels.
  • Two types of neuron: those with a long axon (Golgi type I) and those with a short axon that stays in its local area (Golgi type II).
  • Glial cells and the cells of the cerebellum, olfactory bulb and hippocampus.

The reticular theory

Golgi defended the reticular theory: the axons of neurons fused with one another to form a continuous network, like blood vessels, through which information flowed. It was the dominant view of the time.

Using Golgi's own method, improved, Santiago Ramón y Cajal reached the opposite conclusion: neurons are independent cells that communicate by contact. This is the neuron doctrine. See the neuron.

A shared Nobel, and a clash

In 1906 Golgi and Cajal shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "in recognition of their work on the structure of the nervous system". It was the first time the prize had been shared. But in his Stockholm lecture Golgi attacked the neuron doctrine and defended his reticular theory, to the surprise of the audience. Cajal wrote in his memoirs about how awkward the situation was. Time, and the electron microscope in the 1950s, proved Cajal right: there is a gap between neurons, the synaptic cleft. See synapses and neurotransmitters.

Beyond the brain

Golgi made other important discoveries:

  • The Golgi apparatus (1898): an organelle present in almost every cell, which modifies and packages proteins. For decades its existence was doubted, until the electron microscope confirmed it. Every biology student learns about it today.
  • The Golgi tendon organ: a sensor in tendons that detects muscle tension.
  • Malaria: he showed that bouts of fever coincide with the release of a new generation of parasites into the blood, and distinguished forms of malaria by the periodicity of the fever.

Legacy

Although he was wrong about the reticular theory, the Golgi stain was the tool that allowed modern neuroscience to be born, and it is still used in laboratories today. Without Golgi, Cajal could not have proved the neuron doctrine.
