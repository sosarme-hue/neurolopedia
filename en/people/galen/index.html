Galen

The Greek physician from Pergamon who showed through experiments that the brain, not the heart, controls voice and movement. His authority lasted 1,400 years.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Galen (Claudius Galenus, in Greek Galenós) was born around 129 in Pergamon, a wealthy Greek city of the Roman Empire in present-day Turkey. The son of a well-off architect, he studied philosophy and medicine in Pergamon, Smyrna, Corinth and Alexandria, the great medical centre of the age. On his return he became physician to the gladiators of Pergamon, which let him see deep wounds and learn about the inside of the body. Around 162 he settled in Rome, where he became famous for public demonstrations and eventually served as physician to the emperors Marcus Aurelius, Commodus and Septimius Severus. He died around 216.

He was one of the most prolific writers of antiquity: more than a hundred of his works survive, some three million words.

The brain is in charge

In Galen's time there was a great debate: where is the seat of the mind? Aristotle believed it was the heart, and that the brain merely cooled the blood. Some Alexandrian physicians, such as Herophilus and Erasistratus, had already pointed to the brain, and Galen proved it with experiments.

In his most famous experiment, before an audience in Rome, he held down a squealing pig and cut the recurrent laryngeal nerves, two thin nerves in the neck. The animal stopped squealing, although it kept struggling. The voice depended on nerves that came from the brain, not the heart. That nerve is still sometimes called "Galen's nerve".

His contributions to the nervous system

  • Spinal cord experiments: by cutting it at different levels in animals, he saw that movement and sensation were lost below the injury, and that if it was cut very high the animal stopped breathing. This is the basis of what we know today about spinal cord injury. See spinal cord.
  • Cranial nerves: he described seven pairs. See cranial nerves.
  • He distinguished between sensory nerves (softer) and motor nerves (harder).
  • He described the brain's ventricles and the great cerebral vein that now bears his name (the vein of Galen).
  • He classified symptoms such as paralysis, apoplexy (what we now call stroke) and epilepsy.

Errors that lasted centuries

Dissecting human corpses was forbidden in Rome, so Galen studied mainly monkeys, pigs and oxen, and attributed to humans structures that we do not have, such as the rete mirabilis, a network of vessels at the base of the brain in ruminants.

He also believed that the mind resided in an "animal spirit" (pneuma psychikon) formed in the ventricles and travelling along the nerves, which he thought were hollow. And his medical theory was built on the four humours of Hippocrates (blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile).

Legacy

His authority was so great that for almost 1,400 years, in Europe and the Islamic world, anatomy was taught "according to Galen", and contradicting him was almost unthinkable. It was Andreas Vesalius who, in 1543, corrected his errors by going back to dissecting human bodies. But his great lesson still stands: observe and experiment to find out how the body works.
