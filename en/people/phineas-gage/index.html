Phineas Gage

A railway foreman whose skull was pierced by an iron rod in 1848. He survived, but he was "no longer Gage". His case is the most famous patient in the history of neurology.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Phineas Gage was born in 1823 in New Hampshire, in the United States. At 25 he was foreman of a gang building the Rutland and Burlington Railroad in Vermont. His employers considered him the best foreman they had: efficient, capable and well-balanced.

The accident

On 13 September 1848, near the village of Cavendish, Gage was preparing a blast to clear rock for the railway. His job was to pour gunpowder into a hole, cover it with sand and tamp it down with an iron rod. Probably distracted, he struck the powder before his assistant had added the sand. A spark flew, the powder exploded and the rod shot out like a projectile.

The rod was 1.1 metres long, about 3 centimetres in diameter and weighed 6 kilos. It entered below his left cheekbone, passed behind the eye, went through the frontal lobe and came out of the top of his skull. It landed about 25 metres away, smeared with blood and brain.

Incredibly, Gage did not lose consciousness (or only for a moment). Within minutes he was talking, and he was taken, sitting up in a cart, to the village hotel. There he was treated by the doctor John Martyn Harlow. He had a serious infection, but within ten weeks he had recovered physically, although he lost the sight in his left eye.

"No longer Gage"

Harlow followed his progress and in 1868 reported that his personality had changed: he had become "fitful, irreverent, indulging at times in the grossest profanity", impatient and unable to stick to his plans. His friends said he was "no longer Gage". The company did not give him his job back.

For a few years he worked in a livery stable and exhibited himself with his rod. Between 1852 and 1859 he lived in Chile, where he worked as a stagecoach driver on the route between Valparaíso and Santiago, a job that required planning and dealing with passengers. He returned ill to his family in California and began to have epileptic seizures, probably due to the scar in his brain. He died in San Francisco in 1860, twelve years after the accident.

Why he is so famous

Gage's case was one of the first pieces of evidence that the frontal lobe is involved in personality, social behaviour and decision-making, not just movement. See cerebral cortex and lobes.

In 1994 Hanna and Antonio Damasio used a computer to reconstruct the path of the rod through his skull and concluded that the damage was mainly to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex on both sides. Antonio Damasio linked this to present-day patients with similar injuries, who reason well but make disastrous decisions, and developed his somatic marker hypothesis: emotions are essential for good decision-making. See emotions.

Myth and reality

Over time Gage's story has been greatly exaggerated: some books portray him as a violent drunk and drifter, without evidence. The psychologist Malcolm Macmillan, who spent decades studying the case, argues that Gage partly recovered, as his work in Chile shows, and that his brain adapted thanks to plasticity. In 2009 the first known photograph of Gage appeared, holding his rod.

His skull and the rod

The skull and the rod are kept in the Warren Anatomical Museum at Harvard University. Today, personality changes after frontal lobe damage are seen in head injuries, tumours and frontotemporal dementia.
