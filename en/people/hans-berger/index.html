Hans Berger

A German psychiatrist who was looking for the physical basis of telepathy and ended up recording, in 1924, the first human electroencephalogram. He discovered the alpha rhythm.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Hans Berger was born in 1873 in Neuses, near Coburg, in Germany. His maternal grandfather was the poet Friedrich Rückert. He began by studying astronomy in Berlin, but gave it up to do military service in the cavalry. There something happened that, as he told it, changed his life.

A feeling at a distance

In 1892, during manoeuvres, Berger fell from his horse and was nearly crushed by an artillery gun. That same evening he received a telegram from his father asking whether he was all right: his sister, many kilometres away, had suddenly had a premonition that something had happened to him. Berger became convinced that some form of transmission of "psychic energy" had taken place between them. He decided to study medicine to understand how the mind worked and whether its activity could be measured.

A secret quest

He studied medicine in Jena, where he worked all his life at the university psychiatric clinic, becoming its director in 1919 and rector of the university in 1927. He was a reserved, methodical man. For years he worked in secret, in a small laboratory, trying to record the electrical activity of the human brain, which had already been detected in animals at the end of the 19th century.

On 6 July 1924 he obtained the first recording in a 17-year-old boy who had had skull surgery, with the electrodes over the area without bone. He then refined the technique to record through the scalp, often using his own son Klaus as a volunteer. It took him five years to be sure that what he was seeing was not an artefact.

The electroencephalogram (1929)

In 1929 he published Über das Elektrenkephalogramm des Menschen ("On the electroencephalogram of man"). He described:

  • The alpha rhythm, about 10 waves per second, which appeared at rest with the eyes closed and disappeared when they were opened or when concentrating on a task. For years it was called the "Berger rhythm".
  • The beta rhythm, faster and of lower amplitude, during mental activity.
  • Changes in the EEG with sleep, anaesthesia, epileptic seizures and some diseases.

See electroencephalogram.

Scepticism and recognition

At first the scientific community greeted his results with suspicion: Berger was a little-known psychiatrist and his recordings seemed too good to be true. In 1934 the British physiologist and Nobel laureate Edgar Adrian repeated his experiments in Cambridge and confirmed the alpha rhythm. From then on the EEG spread rapidly around the world. Berger was nominated for the Nobel Prize.

A dark ending

Under the Nazi regime, Berger sat on a court that decided on the forced sterilisation of people with diseases considered hereditary, an aspect of his life that historians have studied in recent years. In 1938 he was forced to retire. He suffered from severe depression and a skin disease, and in 1941 he took his own life.

Legacy

The EEG is today an essential tool for diagnosing epilepsy, studying sleep and assessing coma and consciousness, and it is the basis of non-invasive brain-computer interfaces. Paradoxically, the instrument Berger created to look for telepathy became one of the most useful and objective tests in neurology.
