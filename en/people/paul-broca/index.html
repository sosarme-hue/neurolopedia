Paul Broca

French surgeon and anthropologist who in 1861 located an area in the left frontal lobe needed for speech. It was the first solid evidence that mental functions have a specific seat in the brain.

Text: Alejandro Rosàs, family physician · Updated: 26 November 2026

Who he was

Pierre Paul Broca was born in 1824 in Sainte-Foy-la-Grande, near Bordeaux, the son of a Protestant doctor. He studied medicine in Paris and was a brilliant surgeon, but his interests went much further: pathology, cancer, aneurysms and, above all, anthropology. In 1859 he founded the Society of Anthropology of Paris, the first in the world. Towards the end of his life he was made a life senator. He died in 1880, aged 56.

The patient "Tan"

In April 1861, at the Bicêtre hospital, Broca examined Louis Victor Leborgne, a 51-year-old man who had been in hospital for more than twenty years. He had lost his speech when young and could only say one syllable, "tan", which he often repeated twice and accompanied with gestures. That is why the hospital called him "Tan". Yet he understood what was said to him and answered intelligently with gestures.

Leborgne died a few days later. At the post-mortem Broca found a lesion in the back part of the third frontal convolution of the left hemisphere. The next day he presented the brain to the Society of Anthropology. Months later he studied a second patient, Lelong, who could say only five words and had a lesion in the same place.

"We speak with the left hemisphere"

After collecting more cases, Broca reached a famous conclusion in 1865: "Nous parlons avec l'hémisphère gauche", we speak with the left hemisphere. It was the first convincing demonstration of two fundamental ideas:

  • Mental functions are localised in specific areas of the cortex.
  • The two hemispheres are not the same: there is lateralisation.

The area now bears his name, Broca's area, and the loss of speech he described is Broca's aphasia: non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech with relatively preserved comprehension. See language and cerebral cortex and lobes.

There was a priority dispute: a country doctor, Marc Dax, had written in 1836 that loss of speech was associated with left-sided lesions, but his work was not published until 1865.

Other contributions

  • He described the "great limbic lobe" (1878), the ring of cortex on the inner surface of the hemispheres, the basis of today's limbic system.
  • He studied how tumours spread through veins and the inheritance of cancer.
  • He designed numerous instruments for measuring the skull.

Light and shadow

Broca was also a champion of craniometry, measuring the skull and brain to compare human groups, and held views on the inferiority of women and some races based on brain weight that we now know to be false. The palaeontologist Stephen Jay Gould analysed them in The Mismeasure of Man (1981) as an example of how prejudice shapes science.

Today

Leborgne's brain is preserved in Paris. In 2007 it was studied with MRI, which showed that the lesion was larger and deeper than Broca described, also affecting the white matter. Today we know that language depends on networks rather than a single spot. Even so, Broca's work opened the way for Carl Wernicke and the whole neurology of language. Aphasia remains a common symptom of stroke.
